After the Songs

Chapter Six

The Things They Forgot

By the time the rains returned, the children had learned which sounds meant home and which meant hiding.

The rain began with the roof.

Not the sky, which had been dark since morning, or the wind that drove the fishermen to beach their boats before midday. The roof announced it first. A single drop fell through the thatch and struck the rim of Mēni's cooking pot. Another followed, then a third, each arriving in a different place, until the pot sounded as if someone were trying to play it with wet fingers.

Mēni looked up from the dough beneath her palms.

“That is not where I mended it.”

“It is where it has decided to break,” Ilā said.

“Fetch the ladder.”

“The small one?”

“The ladder that reaches the roof.”

“There is only one.”

“Then why ask?”

Ilā grinned and went into the rain.

Analā moved the sleeping baby away from the damp patch spreading across the floor. He was no longer quite a baby, though Mēni still called him one. He had discovered the delight of standing up while holding a table, and the equal delight of letting go at precisely the moment someone looked away. His knees were permanently marked with little bruises, most of which he had earned in the service of reaching things he was not supposed to touch.

He woke as Analā lifted him. His face puckered with the offence of being disturbed, but the sound of rain distracted him.

“Da,” he said, pointing towards the door.

“Yes,” Analā said. “Water. Very clever.”

He pointed harder.

“Da.”

“The whole world agrees with you.”

She had not intended to sound like her uncle.

The resemblance arrived in her own ears after the words were spoken. It was no particular phrase he had used. It was the patient seriousness with which he could entertain a foolish argument, as if a small child and a royal minister deserved equal attention until one of them became unreasonable.

She held the baby close and listened to the rain strike the roof.

Three months had passed since the boats brought them from Lanka. On the island there were no palace bells to mark the hours, no school tablets to announce the beginning of lessons, no servants who knew when a meal ought to appear. Time collected itself in other ways. A fishing net repaired and torn again. Salt drying on a window ledge. The baby's first tooth. The angle of the sun when Ilā returned from the shore. A new line of hard skin on Analā's palms where the rope had once blistered them.

Her mother had promised to follow.

For the first month, Analā had counted days.

In the second, she had counted boats.

Now she was learning not to count at all.

The children came to Mēni's house because the rain had made the paths too slippery for their usual games.

Six sat beneath the eaves, wet to the knees, while Mēni tried to turn a leaking roof and insufficient flour into dinner for more people than she had invited. Ilā had found an old drum with a cracked rim. A boy named Dev beat it quietly with two fingers because the baby disliked loud noises. The others sang a school song from Lanka about a boat that mistook the moon for a harbour lamp.

They knew the beginning well. In the middle, one child remembered a different line and another said that line belonged to an entirely different song. They argued until the youngest boy offered a version containing neither line and twice as many boats.

“No,” Dev said. “The second boat didn't have a lantern.”

“It had the king's lamp,” the little boy answered.

“That isn't the song.”

“My brother told me.”

“Your brother told you about the evacuation.”

The boy shrugged. To him there was little difference. Both songs had boats and darkness and someone trying to reach the other shore.

“The king made sure we all got out,” he said.

Analā was sitting at the doorway with a bundle of damp firewood across her knees. She looked up.

“He gave the order,” Dev said.

“He came to the boats,” the little boy insisted. “My brother said he checked every one.”

“Your brother was already aboard,” Analā said.

“He saw him.”

“Where?”

“At the harbour.”

Analā remembered men shouting from one boat to another, water slapping against the hull, a woman she could no longer touch pushing her towards a space between strangers. Her mother had come after the passenger lists were sealed. A sailor had objected. Someone had pulled Analā aboard by the arm. The moment had contained smoke and rope and terror, not the orderly inspection the little boy described.

But she remembered her uncle on the cliff.

She had waved. The boat had turned. She had not seen whether he waved back.

“He wanted us to leave,” she said.

The little boy nodded, satisfied. “So he checked.”

Analā opened her mouth.

Mēni, from the cooking place, asked her to bring another piece of wood.

She did.

The children began the song again. By the time they reached its last line, the king was standing beside all the boats, holding a lamp so bright that every child could see the way across the water.

Analā knew that wasn't how it happened.

She also knew the boy had arrived on the island without his mother.

She laid another piece of wood beneath the pot and let him sing.

The first vessel came for rope after the heavy rains had ended.

It was a shallow supply boat from the north, painted dark along the waterline and marked with a pale emblem on its forward sail. Someone saw it before breakfast, approaching against a wind that ought to have delayed it until afternoon.

Sēnan stood at the harbour steps with a cloth wrapped around his shoulders. Two islanders were weighing lengths of cordage in the storehouse. The island sold rope to visiting fishermen and merchant captains; the fibre came from plants grown above the eastern terraces, soaked, scraped, twisted and laid out to dry along the wall beside the salt pans. It was work that left a person's fingers sore and their clothes smelling of wet bark.

The men on the boat began shouting before the vessel was tied.

“Jai Shri Ram!”

Their voices carried over the water. One man beat the side of the hull with the flat of his hand. Another stood on a thwart with both arms raised, laughing when the motion nearly upset his balance. They wore mixed pieces of military clothing, some clean and some stained with salt. The officer at the bow had a coloured sash tied over a tunic whose collar did not match it.

Sēnan recognised the manner before he recognised the men.

He had heard the same cry from the ship that took away a hundred and three children. He remembered the swaying bodies, the officer's shut eyes, the question about how many Rakshasa children were ashore. He remembered answering with a number that was not true.

Now the harbour had another vessel, and the children were in plain sight.

Two girls were carrying shell baskets across the open ground. Dev was helping a smaller boy push a broken wheel towards the repair shed. Analā and Ilā were on the upper path with a net between them.

“Get them away from the harbour,” Sēnan said to the rope-weigher.

“Which children?”

“All of them.”

The man looked towards the path.

“Now?”

“Before they tie up.”

The island had no watchtower. It did not need one to understand how a warning moved. A fisherman left his basket in the sand and walked quickly up the path. A woman called her daughter indoors. Someone took the wheel out of Dev's hands. Ilā heard her name from the lower houses and turned.

Analā saw the boat then: the raised arms, the pale emblem, the sunlight on a blade at one man's hip.

A coldness went through her that had nothing to do with the wind.

“Come,” Ilā said.

“Why?”

Ilā looked at her.

Analā had meant to ask why they had to run from a boat buying rope. She had meant to ask why those men were permitted to make the island smaller merely by arriving. But Ilā was already pulling the net from her hands, and a little boy was standing alone in the path, uncertain which way to go.

Analā took the boy's hand.

They went into the groves.

No one asked her whether she was Vibhishana's daughter.

No one suggested that her father might make her safe.

The islanders hid her because she was one of the Rakshasa children.

She was grateful for that, and angry that she was grateful.

The rope buyers wanted forty coils.

Sēnan had twenty-seven ready. He explained that the rest would have to be made and dried. The officer looked at the coils as though he believed an islander's refusal to produce something was an attempt to cheat him.

“You have trees,” he said.

“We have fibre plants.”

“Then make more.”

“We do. Slowly.”

The officer put his foot against one coil and rolled it a little way across the storehouse floor.

“You people have had a peaceful life while men fought to free the coast.”

Sēnan watched the coil come to rest.

“We have had work,” he said. “The rope is part of it.”

A sailor behind the officer laughed.

The officer asked whether any of the old king's people had settled here. Sēnan said travellers came and went, which was true. He asked whether the island supplied anyone who resisted the new order. Sēnan said the island supplied fishermen who could pay. The officer smiled at this answer without finding it amusing.

Beyond the open door, a young islander was trying to bring in a jar of drinking water. One of the sailors stepped into her path and asked whether she was married. When she moved around him, he told his companions that the southern islands bred women who thought themselves queens.

Sēnan called for the water himself.

The officer bought twenty-seven coils. He argued over every measure, made the storekeeper weigh three of them twice and complained that the price was higher than he had been promised at another port. At last he paid in mixed coin, counting it onto the table with the little theatrical sighs of a man being cheated by a shopkeeper who had never seen such generosity.

Before leaving, he asked again about Rakshasas.

“Dangerous creatures,” he said. “We are told to watch for them. They hide among decent folk.”

“Children hide during storms,” Sēnan said. “Men hide from debts. Perhaps everyone hides from something.”

The officer gave him a long look.

Then he laughed, called for the ropes to be carried aboard and departed shouting his god's name towards the shore.

In the grove, the children heard the cries grow distant.

They remained where they were until Ilā's mother came for them.

That afternoon Sēnan told the household heads that when certain sails appeared, the children were to be brought inland before the boat reached the harbour.

“Even if they are only buying rope?” one man asked.

“Especially then,” said the storekeeper. “They will have time to ask questions.”

A woman asked whether hiding made the island look guilty.

Sēnan thought about the officer rolling the rope with his foot.

“We can discuss appearances after the children are safe.”

No one argued further.

By the next change of season, hiding had acquired rules.

The first attempts were noisy and badly organised. A girl ran back for her sandals. Two brothers quarrelled over a wooden horse, which one insisted belonged to him although the other had carved it. A small child refused to leave the beach because he had buried a shell in the sand and intended to recover it before supper.

Sēnan tried to establish a signal. The first version required three blows on an iron plate near the storehouse. On a windy day only half the village heard it, and the other half thought someone was calling fishermen to mend a net. They settled on a shout passed from the harbour to the terraces and from the terraces to the groves, with a second cry meaning the visitors had gone.

The signal was not secret. Secrecy would have required more people to understand it than the island possessed. It was simply a way to move children out of sight without waiting for an explanation.

The children practised without being asked.

Ilā worked out which path stayed firm after rain. Dev discovered that the smallest children walked faster if he let them carry the ends of a long reed together. Analā learned who was frightened by sudden noises, who could not be persuaded to leave a toy, and whose feet would begin to hurt after the first climb.

She counted heads at the grove, then counted again.

At first she counted three hundred and nine out of habit, because that had been the number written down when they arrived. Once she caught herself beginning the old count and stopped. One hundred and three had gone away on the recovery ship. Two hundred and six remained. The ten children from other communities whom Sēnan's panicked answer had left behind were counted among them, and none was asked to explain why they were still here.

She learned each child's preferred hiding place.

She learned that panic spread faster when adults whispered.

She learned that a little boy who laughed whenever he was frightened could make the others think danger was over when it was only beginning.

Mēni's baby learned a new word.

“Hide,” Ilā told him one afternoon, making a game of covering her face with both hands and uncovering it again.

The baby imitated her so badly that only one eye disappeared. He laughed. Mēni laughed too. Then someone called from the harbour path, and the older children stopped smiling at once.

Mēni looked at her son's hands, still raised beside his face.

She lifted him without a word.

That evening she asked Ilā to teach him the names of fish instead.

News arrived in scraps that smelled of lamp oil and foreign harbours.

A trader came with salt and said a Rakshasa household near the northern coast had been emptied after the father was accused of sheltering soldiers loyal to the old court. A different trader said the father had attacked a patrol and his sons had fled. A third had seen the house himself, with its door hanging crooked and ash on the threshold, but could not say who had set the fire.

Sēnan listened to each account. He asked which port, which street, who had witnessed the arrest. The islanders mocked him gently for making traders speak as though they were giving evidence before a magistrate.

“Would you prefer I simply believed the first story?” he asked.

“Prefer? No,” the storekeeper said. “But I would sleep better if the truth arrived before the next ship.”

The stories did not become less disturbing when questioned.

One man had seen prisoners marched through a southern market with their wrists tied in pairs. He recognised two as former members of Lanka's harbour guard. He did not know what they had been accused of. Another had carried grain to a village where the houses belonging to Rakshasa families had been marked, although he could not explain who had made the marks or whether the families had left willingly. A third had watched soldiers beat a man who refused to name his brother's whereabouts. The trader insisted he had seen this himself. When Sēnan asked whether the man survived, he said he did not know. The soldiers had taken him away.

Other accounts came swollen with distance.

Forty dead in one port, said a sailmaker.

Twelve arrested, said an oil trader who had been there a fortnight later.

The same men, said the sailmaker.

Perhaps, said the oil trader, and then asked why everyone wanted a number when nobody could name the dead.

At night the islanders discussed the difference. Children listening from outside the houses heard the parts spoken most loudly.

Forty.

Rakshasas.

Killed.

By the following week, a boy told Dev that forty Rakshasas had been killed in the street where his mother's family once lived. His mother's family had never lived there. He had heard the name of the port correctly, but the rest had found places to settle around it.

Analā corrected him about the street.

She did not correct the number. She no longer knew what number to use.

One evening Ilā asked whether the men chanting on the rope boats were the same men doing the killing.

“No,” Sēnan said. “Not necessarily.”

“They say the same words.”

“A great many people say those words.”

“Then how do we tell which are dangerous?”

Sēnan was repairing a split handle. He turned the wood in his hands, searching for a place where the fibres had not yet parted.

“You don't always know,” he said.

Ilā looked dissatisfied.

Analā, beside the doorway, said, “We hide from all of them.”

No one answered.

The rule had already been made.

The second rope boat came at the end of the dry months.

Its sail was patched and its hull scarred by a recent journey. It carried eight men who made enough noise for twice that number. They arrived after noon, when the rope lengths were stretched along the sun-warmed walls and the workers had gone to eat.

“Jai Shri Ram!” the men shouted, laughing as the sound came back to them from the headland.

A child saw them first and called from the harbour. Before the officer aboard could step ashore, the smaller children had begun climbing towards the trees.

Analā was carrying the baby. He was heavier now and displeased at being taken away from a bowl of fruit skins with which he had been playing. He twisted in her arms, reaching back towards Mēni's house.

“Fish,” she said urgently, pointing towards the grove. “We'll look for fish.”

He believed her. This was one of his endearing faults.

Ilā caught up with them on the path.

“There's no fish in the grove.”

“Don't tell him.”

“Where will we find one?”

“We will think of something.”

They had begun to use jokes like small pieces of cloth pressed against a wound: not enough to heal it, but enough to make the next few steps possible.

At the harbour the officer demanded sixty coils of rope and heavy cord suitable for mooring large vessels. Sēnan said there were thirty-four coils of the thickness requested. The officer told him to bring the rest when they were made, as though this were a matter already agreed.

The storekeeper set out the weights.

The officer waved them aside.

“The new administration requires these supplies.”

“Then the new administration will be welcome to pay for them,” Sēnan said.

The officer put a hand on the sword at his waist.

For a moment the harbour was quiet enough for the storekeeper to hear the little clicking noise of a crab moving beneath the steps.

“Would you have your island listed as refusing assistance?” the officer asked.

“I would have it listed as supplying rope.”

“Then supply it.”

The officer ordered the coils carried down to the boat. He inspected none of them. He was offended not by the price but by the suggestion that he might owe anything to the people standing before him.

A younger sailor lingered beside the table while the others worked. He glanced at the storekeeper's open hand and then at the coins tied in a pouch beneath his belt. He began loosening the knot.

“Get that aboard,” the officer called.

The sailor let the knot fall and went to carry a coil.

The storekeeper watched him go.

Later, when Analā asked whether the sailors had stolen the rope, the storekeeper said yes.

“Every one of them?” she asked.

He looked down at his empty hands.

“They took it,” he said.

He did not describe the young man's hesitation. He had not found a use for it.

The loss was measured in more than coils.

The island had promised rope to three fishing households before the next open-water season. One boat needed a new mooring line; another could not be safely dragged above the highest tide without thick cord around its bow. A third family had paid for material that was now sailing north in a soldier's hold.

The storekeeper returned their money in uneven piles of coin. One of the fishermen refused it.

“I don't want my money back. I want to keep my boat.”

“We will make more,” Sēnan said.

“How long?”

“As long as it takes.”

“The sea doesn't wait for us to finish twisting rope.”

An islander who had worked for years on Lankan merchant vessels said no captain of the old harbour service would have dared take so much without an order and a receipt.

The fisherman with the torn sail answered that he remembered a Lankan officer who once took his brother's timber.

“That was one man,” the storekeeper said.

The fisherman had heard those words before. He looked towards the sea and decided not to repeat the argument.

The following week, a merchant arrived with oil and said a family had been killed after refusing to surrender a former Lankan soldier. The tale had reached him from a port he had not visited. Sēnan asked for names and received none.

The storekeeper believed it.

“They stole our rope,” he said. “Why wouldn't they steal someone's life?”

No one could answer him in a way that made the rope return.

By the next rains, Analā had grown taller than Ilā by enough that Ilā began accusing her of standing on stones.

They marked their heights against the inner doorpost with charcoal. Analā's first mark remained visible beneath the second; Ilā made hers deliberately thick so that neither could say precisely where it began. The baby, now able to walk three steps without holding anything, insisted upon having his mark too. Mēni drew it low on the wood while he tried to bite the charcoal.

Analā had been fourteen when the boats arrived. Now she was fifteen.

The birthday had passed quietly. Mēni cooked a sweet dish from what little rice and jaggery she could spare. Ilā gave Analā a small shell with a hole through its centre, threaded onto a length of blue cord. Analā had never received a gift made from so little and kept so close to her skin.

She began carrying out small tasks for Sēnan. He trusted her to check the younger children before a boat arrived and to walk the households after visitors had departed. He sometimes asked her to read a mark in the register when his eyes were tired. She knew which names were written twice by mistake and which had been recorded under a child's nickname because nobody had been certain of the name given at birth.

She also knew which stories the other children wanted to hear.

They liked the story of Ravana on the harbour wall, asking why the landing steps were broken. The old fisherman had told it a dozen times. When Dev told it, the king moved the stone alone. When the younger children told it, he rebuilt the entire harbour before supper.

They liked the story of the evacuation. The king ordered the children away and stayed to defend them. In one version he stood at the cliff with a lamp. In another he had designed the flying craft himself, built every boat and known by name each child who sailed.

Analā knew he had not known every child by name.

She knew he had never repaired the island harbour alone.

Once, when a girl said Ravana understood every art there was, Analā told her that her uncle had spent his life asking other people how things worked.

“He had ten minds,” the girl replied.

“That was what people said.”

“So he knew everything.”

“No.”

“Then why did he have ten?”

Analā found herself thinking of a supper long ago when her uncle had asked her father about something he did not understand, and her father had answered him with the patient irritation of a younger brother who had explained the same thing before. She remembered laughter around the table. She remembered being too young to understand what the argument was about.

“He wasn't ashamed of asking,” she said.

The girl looked disappointed.

That evening the children sang about the king who knew the languages of birds, the weight of clouds and the secrets under the sea. None of these things had been in the story that morning.

Analā listened from Mēni's doorway.

She had once been angry with a child for making up words about her uncle. Now the songs made her feel that he had not disappeared completely. Each new verse gave him another place to exist.

She did not ask them to stop.

The stories from the mainland changed with the winds.

An oil trader brought a painted scrap from a tavern wall: an ugly man with many heads, his mouths open in a row of animal teeth. He said such pictures were appearing in ports where the victorious soldiers drank. Beside the man had been a monstrous woman, green-faced and bent towards anyone who looked. The trader had torn down the scrap because it was a poor likeness of anything human, and because a shopkeeper had promised him a copper for the cloth beneath it.

The children gathered around to look.

Ilā was the first to recognise it.

“That's what the men on the ship said,” she told Analā. “The ten heads.”

Analā took the painted scrap between two fingers.

It bore almost no resemblance to her uncle. She thought of the sugared fruit, the teasing voice, the dark figure above the sea. She thought of the woman who had been his sister, whose face had been scarred before the war. The green creature beside him had no human sorrow in it at all.

“That's not him,” she said.

No one disputed her.

They knew she had known Ravana. They had not.

Dev asked what they should do with the painting.

“Throw it away,” someone said.

“Burn it,” said another.

Analā carried it to the edge of the cooking fire. For an instant she wanted to press the painted heads into the coals and watch them disappear.

Then she remembered how she had tried to draw her uncle's face from memory and failed to get his eyes right. She had been angry with herself because the face she loved would not remain obedient to her hand.

She folded the scrap so that the heads faced inward and put it beneath a stone.

“Why keep it?” Ilā asked.

“Someone made it,” Analā said. “I want to remember what they made.”

She did not say she wanted to remember who had made it. She did not know.

The first celebration began because the children were hungry for something that was not an anniversary of loss.

Someone had proposed lighting lamps on the day the boats arrived. Another child had objected that they had reached the island on different days, and a third that the day they left Lanka was not one he wished to remember. The argument was still going when Dev asked Analā whether her uncle had ever enjoyed a birthday.

“He liked other people's,” she said.

“Did he have one?”

“Everybody has one.”

“Did he celebrate it?”

She remembered a small courtyard filled with the smell of toasted sesame. Her uncle had come late to a family meal and complained that the cooks were making him older by putting too many sweets on one plate. Her father had told him age was the one thing no court official could be persuaded to reduce.

She could remember the season. She was less certain of the day.

The children decided to make sesame sweets anyway.

Mēni said they did not have enough sesame. They made smaller sweets. One child wanted a lamp for every child who had escaped; another pointed out that there were not enough lamps on the island. They settled for a lamp at each household hosting the children, and one at the harbour steps.

No proclamation was made. No island elder declared a festival. There was no priest and no official prayer. The evening became a gathering because people arrived with food and stayed to help wash the bowls.

An old woman sang a tune she remembered from merchant ships. One of the children changed the words so that the boats carried the king's children away from danger. Another added a verse about the king standing alone against the northern army. By the time the refrain came round again, he had stood there without fear.

Analā did not know whether her uncle had been afraid.

She thought of the cliff, the burning city, the moment when the boat turned.

She wanted him to have been afraid. A frightened person who stayed was braver than an unafraid one. But the words were beautiful, and the children sang them so earnestly that she could not bear to interrupt.

Sēnan stood at the edge of the gathering with his hands clasped behind his back.

A fisherman beside him said, “It is a good thing for them to remember.”

“Yes,” Sēnan replied.

“They will forget him otherwise.”

Sēnan listened to the song about the king who had known every child's name.

“They are remembering,” he said. “That is not quite the same as knowing.”

The fisherman watched the lamps trembling in the wind.

“Perhaps knowing isn't what they need tonight.”

Sēnan looked towards Analā. She was standing with Ilā, singing a line he had never heard before.

“No,” he said. “Perhaps not.”

A little before the rains returned in earnest, the merchant brought a bundle of notices wrapped in oiled cloth.

He was not the same man who had first told Sēnan that Vibhishana had crossed to Rama's camp. This one had come through a northern harbour whose officials now charged a new toll on vessels carrying timber and rope. He had argued with them for half a morning, he said, and finally paid because his cargo would spoil while he fought over the law.

Sēnan bought oil and salt from him. Then the merchant spread the notices across the storehouse table, pleased to demonstrate that his complaint was supported by writing.

“Read that,” he said. “They've changed the dues on rope twice in one season.”

The tablet was a copy made for merchants at a coastal office, its authority marked by a stamped seal and the signature of a clerk. Sēnan read the list of goods, quantities and exemptions. The instructions were dry and precise. A coil of rope above a certain weight now cost more to land. Timber cut for rebuilding was exempt if carried under an approved licence. Oil was taxed differently depending upon its vessel.

At the bottom, beneath the clerk's mark, stood the name under whose authority the order had been issued.

Vibhishana, King of Lanka.

Sēnan read it once.

Then again.

“Who wrote this?” he asked.

“A clerk with very clean hands and a very poor temper.”

“Who ordered it?”

“The king, according to the notice.”

“Who is the king?”

The merchant looked at him as though he had asked who owned the sun.

“Ravana's brother. Vibhishana. Rama put him there after the war. At least that's what the harbour men said. I don't know which part of it was agreed before the fighting.”

Sēnan put his hand flat on the table.

He thought of Analā asking whether the merchant who named her father had seen him with his own eyes. He thought of the bark letter he had returned to her. He thought of the children hiding in the groves while men shouting Rama's name carried away their rope.

“And how long has he been king?” Sēnan asked.

“Since the city fell, more or less. Are you buying the oil?”

Sēnan paid for the oil.

He asked to keep the notice. The merchant said it was a copy and he could make another, provided Sēnan paid for the writing material.

Sēnan paid for that too.

He found Analā behind the house, teaching the smallest children how to make knots that would hold even when wet.

They were using strips of fibre too short to sell. One little boy had tied the ends together so tightly that the entire length had become a single stubborn lump. Analā sat beside him, pulling the knot loose one strand at a time.

Sēnan waited until the children were called to eat.

“I have something to show you,” he said.

She looked at the tablet in his hands.

“Another list?”

“A notice from a harbour.”

“About what?”

“Rope. Dues.”

She smiled a little. “You're teaching me taxes now?”

“No.”

He sat on the low wall and offered it to her. She took it carefully, as she always took things that could be broken by carelessness.

She read the lines. At first she seemed puzzled by why he had brought her a document about landing charges. She reached the bottom and stopped.

Her lips moved once around the name.

“King?” she asked.

Sēnan nodded.

“Of Lanka?”

“Yes.”

She looked again. The letters did not change.

“Since when?”

“I've been told since the war.”

“The war was months ago.”

“Yes.”

She stared at the tablet for so long that Sēnan could hear the children returning from their meal behind the house.

“He took my uncle's place.”

“Yes.”

“And the men who come for rope—”

“I don't know whose orders they act upon.”

“They say Rama's name.”

“Yes.”

“And Rama put him there.”

“That is what I was told.”

Analā turned the notice over. There was nothing on the back but the roughness of the clay.

“All this time,” she said.

Sēnan wanted to tell her about the missing names. He knew only his own register, not the failures of the records in Lanka. He could tell her that he had lied to save the children, that the ship carried back a false number, that the people searching in the mainland might believe two hundred and six children had vanished before reaching the island.

He had told her parts of that story already. He did not know whether it would comfort her now.

“Your father may not know where you are,” he said.

She handed the tablet back.

“He knows where Lanka is,” she replied. “He knows where his throne is.”

Sēnan said nothing.

“He knows where the soldiers go.”

“I cannot say that.”

“He knows they took the city.”

“Yes.”

“He knows they took our rope.”

“Perhaps he doesn't.”

She looked at him. Her face was composed, but it was a child's composition, made with great effort, the mouth held still because moving it would allow something else to move.

“Why do you always know less when it's him?” she asked.

Sēnan could not answer at once.

At last he said, “Because I won't tell you something I cannot prove.”

She nodded as though she had been given another difficult lesson. Then she stepped away.

“Analā,” he said.

She stopped.

“He might still be looking for you.”

She did not turn.

For a moment he thought she would ask how that could be possible, or whether he had received a message. Instead she asked a question he had not expected.

“Will the children need hiding tomorrow?”

“If another vessel comes.”

“Then I should finish the knots.”

She walked back towards the house.

Sēnan remained by the wall with the notice in his hands. He had thought the name of a king might make a child ask to be taken home.

He had never imagined it could make her more determined to stay.

That night Ilā found Analā sitting beside the doorway, her hands busy with a rope she had already twisted too tightly.

Mēni had taken the baby inside. The last bowl was washed. The island was quiet except for insects in the grass and the soft scraping of a boat being dragged beyond the shore.

“You didn't eat,” Ilā said.

“I wasn't hungry.”

“You said that before the rains last year. You were hungry then.”

Analā looked up.

Ilā sat beside her, shoulder against shoulder. They had grown accustomed to sitting this way, not quite leaning on each other but leaving no space for a person who wanted to stand between them.

“Sēnan told Mother,” Ilā said.

“About the notice?”

“Yes.”

Analā unwound the rope once, then wound it again.

“If your father comes,” Ilā asked, “will you go?”

Analā had imagined the question so often that the words had lost their shape. She had once imagined her father stepping from a boat with his sandals wet, gathering her into his arms, asking a thousand questions before she could answer the first. Later she had imagined confronting him on the harbour steps. Now she saw neither scene clearly.

“I don't want to,” she said.

Ilā nodded. She did not express surprise.

“Would you tell him?”

“I don't know.”

“What if he asks why?”

Analā looked across the courtyard at the doorway where Mēni was moving in the lamplight. Somewhere inside the baby made a small sleepy sound. She knew where the drinking jar stood and which roof beam creaked when the wind came from the north. She knew the names of the children in every household. She knew which child would have to be carried if the warning came in the morning.

“I have things to do here,” she said.

Ilā reached for the rope and helped her loosen a stubborn loop.

After a while Analā spoke again.

“I keep trying to remember Father before all this.”

Ilā waited.

“He used to sit with my uncle. They would argue over nothing and then laugh as though the argument had been a game. I thought they would always be brothers.”

“They were brothers,” Ilā said.

“Were.”

“Are. Even if one is dead.”

Analā shook her head.

“The man I knew wouldn't have taken his place.”

Ilā had no way of knowing whether that was true. She took the rope from Analā's hands and laid it between them.

“You can still remember him,” she said.

Analā looked at the dark sea beyond the houses.

“I don't want to remember the man who sits on that throne.”

Ilā touched the blue cord around Analā's neck, the one she had given her for her birthday.

“Then remember what you want tonight,” she said.

It was not a wise answer. It was the answer of a girl who had never met a king and wanted her friend to sleep.

Analā put her head against Ilā's shoulder and remained there until Mēni called them in.

A few days later Analā went alone to the house across the water.

The path had grown narrow with the season's grass. Small flowers had opened among the stones. The broken gate still leaned where it had fallen; the gecko still lived in the wall, or another gecko exactly like it had taken its place. From the window she could see the pale water of the bay and, beyond it, the long northern horizon.

She found the letter where she had left it, beneath the broken weighing scale.

The bark had curled a little in the damp. The charcoal was still legible. She smoothed it across her knees and read the words she had written before she learned her father had crossed to Rama's camp.

She had told him about Sēnan, about Mēni and Ilā, about the babies and the fishing boats. She had asked after Arun and Taranisena. She had asked if her mother was safe. She had described how the islanders had protected them when the dreadful ship arrived.

At the bottom she found the words she had altered in anger.

Please come and find me.

A line through Please. Another through come.

find me remained.

She had once left those two words untouched because she could not bear to erase them.

She read them now and thought of all the times she had counted children in the grove. She thought of Sēnan telling the merchant they had no one to hand over. She thought of the rope carried away without payment and of the celebration in which the children sang about her uncle as if he had never made a mistake. She thought of Mēni's lamp, which had begun to seem more dependable than the lights she remembered in Lanka.

She could not make herself strike out the last two words.

Instead she folded the letter carefully, as her mother had taught her to fold cloth so it would lie flat without tearing at the creases. She put it beneath the scale again, farther back, where the rain could not touch it.

Then she left the house and walked home before darkness.

The next morning a fishing boat appeared at the edge of the bay. It was an island boat, its sail familiar and its owner known to everyone who lived near the shore. Nevertheless, a small child in the upper lane saw the canvas and called the warning before anyone recognised it.

The children began moving towards the groves.

Analā was halfway up the path with Mēni's son on her hip when Sēnan called that there was no danger. The children slowed. Some laughed at the mistake. One little girl cried because she had been frightened by the running.

Analā crouched to comfort her.

“It's all right,” she said. “You did what you were taught.”

The little girl wiped her face with the back of her hand.

“Will he come again?” she asked.

“Who?”

“The king.”

Analā thought of the king standing on the cliff with smoke behind him. Then she thought of the name at the bottom of the harbour notice.

“Which one?” she asked.

The little girl looked confused.

“The one who saved us.”

Analā held her a moment longer.

“He cannot come,” she said.

The child began crying again, not because she understood but because Analā's voice had changed. Analā drew her close and rocked her until the tears stopped. Around them the other children returned to their games, disappointed that the morning's emergency had turned out to be only a fisherman bringing his catch home.

From below came the ordinary noises of the island: knives against fish scales, the scrape of baskets on stone, Mēni calling Ilā to bring in the washing before the clouds gathered.

Analā stood and took the little girl's hand.

For the first time since she had arrived, she did not look towards the harbour to see whether someone might have come for her.

She looked towards the children who were waiting to be led home.

They were already calling her name.
