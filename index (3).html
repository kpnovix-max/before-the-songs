Before the Songs

Chapter Three

The Guest in the Garden

No one had ever imprisoned her so carefully.

Each morning two women came with warm water, fresh cotton and a disagreement about her hair. The older woman believed oil must be heated. The younger believed anything heated before sunrise was an insult.

For six mornings, the princess insisted she could braid it herself. On the seventh, she merely turned her back and lifted the heavy black length from her neck.

Neither woman smiled. This was how women protected one another from having gratitude mistaken for surrender.

The older woman was called Trijata. She had delivered children for twenty-three years, buried two husbands and acquired the habit of looking at powerful men as if they had arrived complaining of a familiar rash. Her hair was iron-grey and reached only to her shoulders because, during a difficult birth many years before, an anxious father had shut half of it in a door. She had cut it with a kitchen knife and continued working.

The younger woman was Mala. She tended the flowering trees, repaired clothes and knew which palace servant was in love with which gate guard. She claimed that these duties were connected because flowers, torn hems and foolish men all required regular attention.

On ordinary mornings Mala brought a shallow basket filled with fallen ashoka blossoms. She scattered them across the water in the bathing court and then complained when they blocked the drain.

“Why put them there?” the princess asked on the third morning.

“Because they are beautiful.”

“And then?”

“Then they become plumbing.”

“Your philosophy is unusually complete.”

Mala had looked pleased. “Trijata says I think only as far as lunch.”

“Lunch is farther than many kings think.”

Trijata, warming sesame oil over a blue flame, had made a sound that might have been agreement.

The garden compound lay behind the western women’s court. Its walls were pale coral washed with lime, and its roofs were covered in dark red tiles that held the day’s warmth long after sunset. Four ashoka trees stood around a square pool. Their flowers were not one pink but many: the colour inside a shell, the colour of watered wine, the colour beneath a fingernail pressed hard against the skin. When the wind moved through them, petals fell upon the white paths in such numbers that sweeping became less a task than a position on the weather.

The princess slept in a pavilion open on three sides. Fine cotton screens kept out insects and very little else. She could hear the palace kitchens before dawn, the changing of the women’s guard, the gardeners quarrelling over water, and the bronze wheel of the western gate turning twice each day.

She could walk from her bed to the pool, from the pool to the flowering court, from the court to the small shrine beneath the fig tree and from there to the covered passage where meals were served.

The path to the outer gate was longer by thirty-seven steps.

She knew because she had counted them.

The gate was made of sandalwood reinforced with bronze. It possessed no bars on the inside. It did not require them. The lock was on the other side.

For the first month she refused everything that had not belonged to her in the forest. She slept on a reed mat rather than the low bed. She washed her own clothes and hung them where court silk could be seen dripping beside the king’s pink roses. She returned trays of sweet cakes untouched and ate only rice, fruit and greens. When Mala brought a copper mirror, she turned it face down.

None of this opened the gate.

Resistance, she discovered, could become another household duty. It required planning, repetition and a surprising amount of laundry.

During the second month, rain entered the pavilion sideways for six days. Her reed mat developed a smell powerful enough to have political ambitions. She moved to the bed.

During the third month, she allowed Trijata to wash her hair after fever left her too weak to raise her arms. Trijata used hot water scented with vetiver and did not speak while combing out the knots.

The princess wept once, silently and with great irritation.

Trijata pretended the water had entered her eyes.

After that, some rules changed.

She continued to wash her own underclothes because there were limits to civilisation. She accepted help with the rest. She permitted Mala to mend the torn border of her yellow forest cloth. She ate one sweet cake when they were fresh and another when she believed nobody was counting.

Mala always counted.

The princess even allowed the mirror to remain upright.

The woman in it had become thinner. Forest sun remained across her face and forearms, but palace shade had softened the darker line at her brow. Her hands still carried small burns from cooking fires. The nail of her left thumb had grown back unevenly after splitting while she chopped wood. Trijata rubbed oil into the scar and said it would improve.

“It has looked like that for two years,” the princess said.

“Then it has had two years of poor advice.”

The women of the island did not speak to beauty as though it were a fragile guest. They handled it. They oiled it, fed it, criticised its posture and told it when it needed sleep.

Mala liked the princess’s skin because, she said, it made pale colours look expensive.

“They are expensive,” the princess replied the first time Mala held a rose-coloured cloth against her shoulder.

“Then you see how successful your skin is.”

The cloth had been woven so finely that it passed through a bracelet. The princess refused it because it came from the king.

Three weeks later, the yellow forest garment tore again beneath the arm.

“The pink one is still available,” Mala said.

“I remember.”

“It does not need mending.”

“That is its most persuasive quality.”

She wore it the following morning.

It was lighter than anything she had possessed in the forest. Air passed through it. The colour was not the deep pink of ceremony but the quiet pink of the ashoka petals after rain. Mala arranged the end over her shoulder, stood back and smiled before she could stop herself.

The princess saw the smile in the mirror.

“Do not look victorious.”

“I am looking employed.”

“You were employed when I wore yellow.”

“Barely.”

The princess laughed.

She regretted it at once, not because laughter was disloyal, but because pleasure had begun to feel like evidence someone might use against her.

Trijata placed a comb on the low table.

“You may like the cloth,” she said. “The gate will remain locked.”

The princess looked at her.

“That was almost comforting.”

“I have never been accused of too much comfort.”

There were things the princess did not miss about the forest.

She did not miss waking before the men to coax flame from damp charcoal. She did not miss pounding grain while smoke entered her hair, or finding grit in rice, or discovering that both brothers had placed wet clothes beside the fire and neither had considered turning them.

She did not miss carrying water uphill.

This last admission took her longest.

The first time Mala filled the bathing jar, the princess had watched the girl lift it from the cart and felt ashamed of the relief that passed through her body. She offered to carry it. Mala refused.

“It is my work.”

“I am capable.”

“So am I.”

There had been no polite answer to that.

Months later, while Mala braided a narrow strand above her ear, she asked whether the northern prince cooked.

“He believes he can,” the princess said.

“That is not what I asked.”

“Then no.”

Mala laughed so suddenly that she pulled the braid.

“Ow.”

“Forgive me.”

“This is why princesses are advised not to tell the truth.”

Trijata was sorting folded cloth by the window. “What does he make?”

“Once he made rice.”

“Once?”

“The pot remained useful afterward, but only as a weapon.”

Mala had to put down the comb.

The princess laughed with them. She loved him while she laughed. Perhaps she loved him more because of it. A husband remembered only as brave and righteous would have become a figure cut from stone. She remembered the man who placed his cold feet against her ankles, who could string a bow in darkness but never locate the salt when it stood before him, who listened gravely to every bird as though it had brought a message intended for the throne.

Missing him did not require pretending he could cook.

The women told her about their homes in return.

Trijata lived in a narrow blue house beside the dyers’ lane. The house had belonged to her mother and would pass to her eldest daughter. Her second husband had entered it with one wooden chest, three shirts and a confidence unsupported by any visible skill.

“What did he do?” the princess asked.

“He sang.”

“Professionally?”

“Not successfully.”

Mala’s husband baked flatbread in the eastern market while she worked at the palace. On evenings when she returned late, he cooked.

The princess turned her head. “He cooks?”

“He would starve if he did not.”

“Men in the north have discovered another solution.”

“What is it?”

“Marriage.”

Trijata folded a cloth more sharply than necessary to hide her amusement.

The princess learned the city through conversations that appeared to concern nothing.

When Mala complained that jasmine cost twice what it had the previous week, the princess knew the southern road had become unsafe. When Trijata said the palace laundry had returned one day late, she knew soldiers were using the lower wells. When the women argued about whether the fish market should move farther from the shrine, she learned which harbour still received boats.

She learned that two women sat on the council that settled merchants’ disputes. A woman commanded the western gate during the afternoon watch. The physician responsible for the royal children was a widow who had declined three offers of marriage because, according to Trijata, she already possessed furniture.

Women here owned shops, orchards and boats. They gave evidence in court without a father or husband speaking first. A widow might marry again, remain alone or make a proposal herself without being treated as though desire had survived where it was not permitted.

The princess thought of the king’s sister in the forest clearing.

She thought of the brothers laughing.

She did not excuse the threat that followed. She did not forgive the hand moving towards the knife, if it had moved. But for the first time she understood that the woman might not have known her proposal would be treated as comedy.

Understanding was inconvenient. It entered beneath doors that certainty had carefully shut.

The island was not the crude place northern stories described.

Its drains worked. Its public kitchens fed strangers. Girls attended lessons beside boys in the shrine courts. Servants complained about wages directly to stewards and expected an answer. Once, from behind the garden screen, the princess saw a flower seller tell the king that his household owed her for three baskets of lotus buds.

The king stopped.

“I paid the steward,” he said.

“Then you and I have both trusted the wrong man.”

He looked at the old woman, looked at the steward behind him and paid her again.

The princess had watched him continue towards the council hall with the flower seller’s receipt in his hand.

She admired that.

She wished she did not.

Admiration was more troublesome than hatred because it required attention. Hatred could flatten a person. Admiration kept returning the edges.

On the last night, Trijata woke her before the warm water arrived.

The older woman carried no lamp. Pink light already moved across the pavilion ceiling, though dawn was still some distance away.

The princess sat up.

“Which building?” she asked.

“The spice sheds.”

“How close?”

“Close enough that Mala has stopped complaining about waking early.”

Mala entered carrying a folded cloth over her mouth. Ash dusted her hair. She had dressed quickly and fastened her tunic with mismatched pins.

“I complained once,” she said.

“You complained while entering,” Trijata replied.

“That was smoke.”

“It had grammar.”

Outside, a burnt fragment of palm thatch drifted through the trees and settled in the pool. The water hissed. Ash lay among the flowers like grey flour.

The princess rose and crossed to the open side of the pavilion.

Beyond the garden wall, the sky above the southern quarter pulsed orange. Bells sounded from two directions. Men shouted on the roofs. Somewhere nearby, a line of clay tiles collapsed and broke one after another.

Mala held out the pale-pink cloth.

The princess looked at it.

“Now?”

“If we are to be burned alive,” Mala said, “I see no reason to do it in yesterday’s clothes.”

Trijata took the garment from her. “We are not being burned alive.”

“Then the cloth will remain suitable.”

The princess changed behind the cotton screen while Trijata explained that the garden pavilion might have to be evacuated. The inner women’s court had stone roofs. Beneath it were storage rooms built into the hill.

“No,” the princess said.

Trijata waited.

“You have not heard the route,” she said.

“Does it pass through the gate?”

“Necessarily.”

“And does it end outside the palace?”

“No.”

“Then I have heard enough.”

Mala emerged from behind the screen carrying the yellow forest cloth. She folded it carefully and placed it in the sandalwood chest.

“The roof may catch,” she said.

“It is his roof.”

“You will be beneath it.”

“That is the weakness in my argument.”

Trijata went to the outer gate and spoke through its narrow opening to the captain of the women’s guard. A messenger was sent to the western room.

He arrived while the king was still looking towards the smoke from the poisoned cistern.

The messenger was a young woman with a shaved strip above one ear, the mark of the western guard. She entered without waiting for ceremony.

“The woman in the garden refuses to be rescued,” she said.

Shuka sat with his injured foot raised upon a cushion. Someone had found him a proper carrying chair, though the king’s breakfast chair remained nearby with a crack along one leg. A servant had placed it against the wall as evidence in an argument no one had time to conduct.

The king closed his eyes.

“Of course she does.”

“The southern spice sheds are burning. The wind is carrying embers towards the western roofs.”

“Move her to the lower women’s court.”

“She refuses.”

“You mentioned that.”

“Trijata said repetition may succeed where authority has failed.”

The king opened his eyes. “Trijata has been waiting twenty years to govern this palace.”

The messenger remained serious. “She says she requires only three competent people.”

“That is how I know she is unqualified. Take forty workers to the garden. Wet every roof between the spice sheds and the western wall. Clear dry branches. Do not cut the flowering trees unless the fire crosses the tiled passage.”

“And the woman?”

“I will speak to her.”

Shuka lowered his foot from the cushion.

The king looked at him.

“No.”

“You have not heard what I intend.”

“You intend to come.”

“Then you have heard.”

“Your ankle is cracked.”

“The physician said it may be cracked.”

“Physicians use ‘may’ when they wish injured men to feel included in the decision.”

Shuka reached for the side of his chair. “You asked me to hear your account.”

“You have heard enough to distrust everyone involved.”

“That is when an account becomes useful.”

The king considered leaving him. Then another explosion sounded from the southern quarter, low and heavy enough to move the drinking cups on the table.

“Bring the chair,” he told the guards.

Shuka settled back. “This one has arms.”

“War improves furniture.”

They crossed the palace by the western service passages.

The grand halls were too exposed to falling embers, and their polished floors had become crowded with objects rescued by people who disagreed about value. A line of servants carried medical chests towards the inner court. Behind them, two kitchen boys rolled a barrel of pickled mango in the opposite direction.

The king stopped them.

“Where is that going?”

The taller boy stared at the barrel as if surprised to find himself attached to it.

“To safety, Majesty.”

“The physicians require this passage.”

“The lower pantry is burning.”

“Are there medicines behind you?”

“Three barrels of mango.”

The king looked at the chamberlain, who had appeared beside him carrying two ledgers and a cage containing a furious green parrot.

“One barrel,” the king said. “Leave the other two and clear the passage.”

The chamberlain raised an eyebrow. “Why one?”

“If we survive, people will want breakfast.”

The shorter kitchen boy put both hands over his heart.

“Majesty.”

“Do not make this emotional. Move.”

They rolled one barrel onward. The parrot watched it pass and shouted a word the king chose not to hear.

“Whose bird?” he asked.

“Your aunt’s,” said the chamberlain.

“My aunt has been dead for nine years.”

“The bird remains optimistic.”

At the next crossing, a female quartermaster waited with a wax tablet. She reported that the western armoury had been emptied, the lower granary opened and the public shelter beneath the music hall prepared for five hundred people.

“It will hold three hundred safely,” she said.

“Then admit three hundred.”

“There are already four hundred and twelve.”

“Remove the instruments. Open the dancers’ court beside it. Send the old and children below, everyone else above.”

“The musicians object to leaving their drums.”

“Tell them the drums have volunteered for military service.”

The quartermaster made a note without smiling. She had worked for the king too long to reward him every time.

Shuka watched her leave.

“You allow women to command armouries?”

“The armour has not complained.”

“The northern camp would find it unusual.”

“The northern camp finds drainage unusual.”

They passed beneath a low arch and entered the women’s court.

Men could enter the administrative rooms during daylight. The inner residences required permission at any hour. The captain at the bronze screen recognised the king and did not move.

“The guest has not admitted you,” she said.

Shuka looked at the king.

The king looked at the captain.

“The garden may be on fire shortly.”

“It is not on fire yet.”

“Tell her I am here.”

The captain sent a girl through the screen.

“You wait outside your own garden?” Shuka asked quietly.

“It is the women’s court.”

“You are the king.”

“Both facts have survived one another.”

The girl returned.

“Trijata says you may enter if you do not bring more than two men, any weapons longer than a forearm, or the parrot.”

The chamberlain drew the bird’s cage closer to his chest.

“She did not need to specify,” he said.

The parrot shouted the same word again.

“She did,” said the king.

The two guards carrying Shuka left their swords at the screen. The king removed the short blade at his belt and gave it to the captain. He, Shuka and the chair passed through.

The garden beyond was pink even beneath the smoke.

Ashoka flowers covered the branches in dense clusters. Mala and three gardeners were sweeping fallen embers from the tiled roofs with wet brooms. Women passed jars from the square pool to the western wall. The air smelled of roses, smoke, damp earth and the sharp green sap of freshly cut branches.

The princess stood beneath the largest tree.

Her pale-pink cloth moved against her ankles. Mala had braided only half her hair before the alarm; the rest fell over one shoulder, black and heavy, almost to her waist. Forest sun remained across her skin. The months in the garden had restored weight to her face without softening the steadiness of it.

The king stopped.

It lasted no longer than a breath.

The princess noticed.

She noticed everything.

“Your city is burning again,” she said.

The king resumed walking. “Only the spice quarter.”

“How reassuring for everyone who lives elsewhere.”

“The wind is moving west. You must leave the pavilion.”

“Good morning to you as well.”

The king inclined his head. “Good morning. You must leave the pavilion.”

Mala came down from the roof with soot across one cheek.

“I tried that,” she told him.

“Did you begin with good morning?”

“No.”

“There is your error.”

The princess looked at Shuka in the carrying chair.

“Who is this?”

“A man who returned from the northern camp.”

“In that chair?”

“The chair was not involved.”

Shuka bowed as far as his ribs allowed. “I am told I returned in one piece.”

The princess studied the bandages, the missing sleeve and the bruising around his eye.

“A generous assessment.”

“The palace is generous tonight.”

“With everything except doors.”

The humour left the air without anyone dismissing it.

The king looked towards the smoke above the wall.

“The lower women’s court is protected by stone. You will be safe there.”

“Safe from whom?”

“From the fire.”

“And after the fire?”

“We shall argue again after the fire.”

“You say that as though we require new material.”

Trijata approached carrying a wet cloth. She wiped soot from Mala’s cheek and handed the cloth to the king.

“Your face,” she said.

The king touched his forehead. His fingers came away black.

“Majesty,” Trijata added, because rank sometimes arrived after hygiene.

He cleaned his face.

The princess watched him. “How bad is the northern water?”

The king glanced at her.

“How do you know about that?”

“Your laundry arrived from the southern court. The western kitchens stopped boiling rice. Twenty jars passed this wall before midnight and returned empty. Either the northern water was lost or the palace has developed an organised thirst.”

Shuka looked at Mala.

Mala lifted both hands. “I said nothing about the cistern.”

“You complained that the bathwater smelled of lotus,” the princess said. “The lotus pool belongs to the palace reserve.”

Mala looked offended by the betrayal of her own conversation.

“The cistern was poisoned,” the king said. “The fire is out. No deaths have been reported.”

“Yet.”

“Yet.”

“And the young man your guards captured?”

This time the king looked at Trijata.

“Do not look at me,” the older woman said. “The flower seller heard it from a water carrier, who heard it from a guard, who was not present. By now the prisoner has confessed to poisoning every well from here to the mountains.”

“He is alive,” said the king.

The princess’s eyes remained on his face. “You spared him?”

“He has not been tried.”

“That was not my question.”

The king recognised his own words in hers.

Shuka did as well.

“He is alive,” the king repeated.

Something in the princess relaxed. It was slight enough that Mala missed it. The king did not.

He was the reason she needed protection in his city. Yet when his guards caught an enemy, she had expected him to be the person most capable of keeping the captive alive.

She hated that the safest man in the city was the man who had made the city unsafe for her.

The king hated it for a different reason.

“Come to the lower court,” he said.

“No.”

“The roof may catch.”

“Then wet it.”

“I am wetting it.”

“Your government is functioning beautifully.”

“You are enjoying this.”

“Only the efficient parts.”

The king lowered his voice. “I am offering you safety.”

“You have offered me many words. Guest. Hostage. Protected woman. Royal responsibility.”

“You are a hostage.”

“A hostage may be exchanged.”

“That was the intention.”

“You have refused every exchange that did not include surrender.”

“You are my guest, whether you accept the word or not.”

“A guest may leave.”

The king’s jaw tightened.

The princess stepped closer. She did not raise her voice.

“A prisoner is accused of something. A hostage has terms. A guest possesses a door. If I am none of these, what am I?”

The women passing water continued to move. No one looked towards the king. This required concentration from all of them.

“You are a woman under my protection,” he said.

“Protection from whom?”

The king did not answer.

An ember landed among the ashoka flowers above them. Mala struck it down with her wet broom.

The princess looked up at the trembling branch.

“I will not be moved,” she said, “because someone knows where to find me.”

Shuka leaned forward in the chair.

The king went still.

“Who?” he asked.

The princess touched the thin cord concealed beneath the pink cloth at her throat.

“You met him.”

The king looked at the cord beneath her fingers.

“The monkey,” he said.

The princess’s expression changed.

“He was a man.”

“He arrived with a monkey stitched upon his shoulder, climbed my wall through a tree and ate enough fruit to alter the garden accounts.”

Mala lifted her broom. “The guavas were already missing.”

“You told the steward it was parrots.”

“I was protecting diplomatic relations.”

Shuka looked from the king to the princess. “A man entered this compound?”

“A commander,” she said.

“A spy,” said the king.

“A messenger.”

“He killed seven guards.”

“After they tried to kill him.”

“That defence becomes exhausting during war.”

The princess held his gaze. “So does yours.”

The man had entered the garden twenty-one nights earlier.

It had been late afternoon, when the day’s heat remained in the stone but the ashoka trees had begun to throw long shadows across the pool. Trijata was cleaning a brass lamp beneath the covered passage. Mala sat on the pavilion step with a basket of guavas between her knees, separating the ripe fruit from the fruit she intended to claim would be ripe tomorrow.

The princess was reading an old collection of songs borrowed from the women’s library. She had reached the same line three times without understanding it because something in the garden was wrong.

No bird had called from the northern tree for several minutes.

She lowered the palm leaves.

A single pink flower fell onto the page.

There was no wind.

The princess looked up.

Leaves shifted high above her. The movement travelled against the natural weight of the branch.

Mala noticed her face.

“What?” she whispered.

The princess placed one finger against her lips.

A man’s voice came from the tree.

He spoke in the careful court language of the north, but the sounds beneath it belonged to hills and forests. The first words were not her name. They were her husband’s.

He spoke of a prince who had left a kingdom wearing bark cloth because his father’s promise had become more binding than his father’s love. He spoke of the brother who followed. He spoke of a woman who had chosen the forest rather than remain in comfort without the man she loved.

The princess stood.

Trijata put down the lamp.

“Show yourself,” the princess said.

The leaves remained still.

“Not while the wall guard can see me,” the voice replied.

“Then you should not have chosen a tree beside the wall.”

There was a pause.

“It looked less public from below.”

Mala’s eyes widened.

Trijata reached beneath the lamp table and removed the small knife used for cutting wicks.

“Come down,” the princess said.

The man descended without making the movements she expected. He did not climb trunk by trunk. A dark rope appeared first, dropping through the pink flowers. He lowered himself hand beneath hand and landed lightly inside the screen of leaves.

He was shorter than the northern prince and built without any flesh that did not serve movement. His arms were long, his hands heavily callused and his dark hair bound close to his head. Mud had dried to the colour of rust across his clothes. A narrow scar divided one eyebrow.

Upon his left shoulder was sewn the figure of a black monkey with its arms raised.

A coil of climbing rope passed twice around his waist and hung behind one hip.

Mala looked at the emblem and then at the guavas.

“Do not,” Trijata murmured.

“I said nothing.”

“Your face did.”

The stranger bowed to the princess.

“I come from your husband.”

“Anyone in this palace could know what you have said.”

“Not anyone.”

“The king knows more about my husband than I wish him to.”

The man’s eyes moved briefly towards the outer gate. “I was told the king could take another man’s appearance.”

“He can barely manage his own without three servants.”

Mala made a small choking sound.

The princess did not smile. “If you are waiting for me to be frightened by magic, you have crossed the sea for nothing.”

The man studied her. Then he reached slowly inside his tunic.

Trijata raised the wick knife.

He brought out a ring.

It lay upon his palm, dull gold worn smooth along one edge. A royal mark had been cut into the face, but the smallest line of the design failed to meet the circle around it. The goldsmith had apologised for the flaw on the morning of the wedding. Her husband had laughed and said a perfect royal seal would only encourage perfect royal documents, which were unbearable.

The princess crossed the space between them.

She did not take the ring at first.

“Where did you get this?”

“From his hand.”

“Where was he standing?”

“Beside a river south of the mountain. He had not slept. Your husband’s brother said he had not eaten. Your husband said this was false because he had eaten half a fig.”

Her breath changed.

The man continued.

“He asked me to tell you that the reed roof still leaks above the place where you kept the grain, though he has repaired it twice. He said you would understand why this proved it was truly him.”

The princess took the ring.

For one moment she was no longer composed, clever or royal. She was a woman holding the shape left by her husband’s finger.

She closed both hands around it and bent her head.

Mala looked away.

Trijata lowered the knife.

The forest commander waited without speaking. Whatever else he had been trained to do, he understood silence.

When the princess raised her face, tears had marked it. She did not wipe them.

“He knows where I am?”

“He knows the island. He knows you live in the western garden. He knows the wall, the harbours and the strength of the northern gate.”

Trijata’s eyes narrowed. “How?”

“I have been here for six nights.”

Mala looked towards the walls as though expecting six nights of strangers to fall out of them.

“Where have you slept?” she asked.

“In places.”

“That is not a place.”

“It is when guards are looking for you.”

The princess slipped the ring onto her finger. It was too large. Her husband’s hands had always been broader than hers. She removed it and threaded it onto the thin cord at her throat.

“How did you cross the water?”

“A fishing boat carried me near the southern reef. I swam the remaining distance.”

Mala looked at the man’s narrow chest. “All of it?”

“Enough of it.”

“And then you climbed the sea wall?”

“There is an old storm channel beneath it.”

Trijata and the princess exchanged a glance.

The commander noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing,” the princess said. “The king is having a difficult week with old channels.”

The man knelt and drew a rough map in the dust beside the pool. A narrow boat waited beyond the western rocks. Two of his people were concealed among fishermen on the lower shore. The garden wall could be crossed after moonrise. From there, a drainage passage reached an abandoned dye yard. They could be at the sea before the morning watch.

“We can leave tonight,” he said.

The princess looked at the map.

Home appeared astonishingly small when scratched with one finger in dust.

“How many guards between here and the dye yard?” she asked.

“Fewer than twelve if we are not seen.”

“And if we are?”

“More.”

“How many people can your boat carry?”

“Four safely.”

The princess looked towards Trijata and Mala.

The commander followed her eyes. “They remain.”

“And when I am gone?”

He did not answer.

Mala’s fingers tightened around the basket.

“The king does not kill women for another person’s crime,” the commander said.

Trijata gave him the look she usually reserved for men explaining childbirth.

“You have been in the city six nights,” she said. “Your scholarship is impressive.”

The princess crouched beside the map.

“If I disappear, every woman assigned to me will be questioned. The guards will be punished. The fishermen who brought you will be searched for. You may reach the water with me. You cannot promise what happens behind us.”

“I can return you to your husband.”

The words entered her like warmth.

For one dangerous moment, she wanted nothing else.

She imagined the forest house, even with its leaking roof. She imagined his hands closing around hers. She imagined waking before dawn to wet charcoal and feeling gratitude for the smoke.

Then she looked at the two women who had washed fever from her body and protected her tears by refusing to notice them.

“Not tonight,” she said.

The commander stared at her.

“You choose to remain?”

“Do not use his words.”

“I came across the sea to find you.”

“And you found me. Return. Tell my husband where I am. Tell him I am alive. Tell him to come openly and end this openly.”

“I can carry you over the wall.”

“I am not a parcel.”

“Princess—”

“If you take me through this city, men will die around me. If we reach the sea, I must trust a boat built for four and a passage you have used once. If I fall into their hands again, every guard in this garden will become crueler because kindness will be called negligence.”

The commander looked at Trijata and Mala.

“They are your captors.”

“They are women employed by my captor. The difference may be inconvenient to your report, but it exists.”

Trijata returned the wick knife beneath the table.

The commander sat back on his heels.

“Your husband will ask why I left without you.”

“Tell him I know him well enough to expect him to come.”

“He may bring an army.”

“That decision was made when the king brought me here.”

It was a hard answer. She knew it while giving it. Somewhere beyond the wall, people who had never seen her would pay for decisions made in her name.

But being taken secretly had turned her into an object. She would not allow her return to be another secret movement decided by men around a map.

She went to the sandalwood chest and removed a small cloth bundle from beneath the repaired yellow garment. Inside lay the ornament she had worn at the centre of her hair on her wedding day. Gold curved around a pale stone no larger than a fingernail.

She placed it in the commander’s palm.

“Give him this.”

“Will he recognise it?”

“He once spent half a morning searching for it while it remained in my hair.”

Mala smiled.

“Do not include that part,” the princess said.

“I will decide what improves the report,” the commander replied.

It was the first time she smiled at him.

He stayed until the evening guard changed.

Mala brought rice cakes, two cups of water and four guavas. The commander ate all four fruits and denied responsibility for the missing fruit of earlier days.

“Parrots,” he said.

“At last,” Mala replied, “a man of evidence.”

Trijata showed him where the wall shadow was deepest. Before he climbed, the princess caught his wrist.

“Tell him something else.”

The commander waited.

“Tell him I have been treated with respect.”

His face hardened. “You are imprisoned.”

“Both things are true.”

“He will not want to hear the first.”

“That is why you must say it.”

The man looked at her for a long moment. Then he bowed.

He reached the top of the wall before the alarm sounded.

A guard on the western roof saw the rope against the pale stone. Another saw movement in the ashoka branches. The commander could have dropped outside and disappeared into the lower streets. Instead he turned back when he heard the inner gate being opened.

He believed the guards had discovered the women.

The first man over the wall received the commander’s heel beneath his chin. The second fell into the pool. A third drew a short spear and shouted for the outer watch.

The commander moved through the branches as though the tree had been designed for him. He cut one rope, crossed the covered roof and pulled down part of the flower trellis behind him. Pink blossoms and wooden poles fell across the path.

Mala watched the destruction with both hands over her mouth.

“That trellis took three years,” she said.

Trijata pulled her towards the pavilion. “Mourn efficiently.”

The commander reached the outer court. There, flight became battle.

Seven guards died before twenty men brought him down beneath a weighted net. Three of the dead had been gardeners carrying borrowed weapons. One was nineteen years old and had spent the previous afternoon asking Mala whether pink flowers meant affection or apology.

War did not permit anyone to remain only charming.

The captured man was brought before the king after midnight.

His hands were tied in front. Blood ran from his mouth. The monkey emblem had been torn halfway from his shoulder. A guard carried the climbing rope behind him as though it were proof of an unnatural body.

The king sat in the smaller council hall because the grand hall was being prepared for a merchant delegation arriving the following week. The delegation never came. For months afterward, its chairs remained stacked behind the northern screen.

The king’s younger brother stood at the council table.

He had been summoned when the prisoner claimed to be a messenger rather than a spy.

“A messenger announces himself,” the king said.

The forest commander spat blood into the bowl a servant placed beside him. “A messenger who announces himself at your gate does not reach your prisoner.”

“My guest.”

The commander laughed once.

The king’s hands closed over the arms of his chair.

“You entered the women’s court. You killed my guards. You mapped my walls.”

“I found the woman you stole.”

“You will be executed before sunrise.”

The younger brother spoke.

“No.”

The word was not loud. It carried because no one in the hall expected it.

The king turned towards him.

“You mistake this table for your own.”

“I mistake nothing. An envoy may be detained, questioned, marked or expelled. He may not be killed for words carried from another ruler.”

“He carried a knife as well.”

“Then punish what he did with the knife. Do not execute the message.”

The king looked at the seven blood-marked weapons laid upon a cloth beside the wall.

Among the dead guards had been the youngest captain of the royal household, a boy the king had known since birth. His mother waited in another room because no one had yet found words worth taking to her.

“He is a spy,” the king said.

“He declared his king and purpose after capture,” the brother replied. “From that moment, he is an envoy in our custody.”

“A convenient transformation.”

“Law is most useful when it is inconvenient.”

The two brothers looked at one another across the table.

The forest commander watched them and understood something about the kingdom before either man intended him to.

The king did not execute him.

He would remember this later as mercy. His brother would remember it as obedience to law. The commander likely remembered only that he remained alive.

Pride still required an answer.

The king ordered the monkey emblem stripped from the man’s shoulder. He ordered the climbing rope and the long cloth belt worn by the forest companies soaked in lamp oil. They would be tied behind the prisoner and burned while he was driven through the lower military court. The message was intended to be plain: the island did not fear men who entered through trees.

It was an intelligent punishment for approximately three minutes.

The commander loosened one wrist while the guards argued over whether the rope should be lit from the end or the middle. When the flame reached the oil, he pulled the burning coil into the nearest man’s legs, seized a spear and climbed the awning supports before anyone understood that the punishment had changed owners.

He crossed the military court above their heads.

The burning rope struck a banner. The banner fell against a roof. The roof covered a store of lamp oil because the steward responsible had decided that oil should be kept near lamps.

From there, the fire developed its own opinions.

The commander ran across three warehouse roofs, dropped into the dyers’ lane and vanished through the storm channel before the harbour gates closed. Burning cloth fell behind him. By dawn, two storehouses, a guard barracks and half the southern market were black frames.

No two witnesses agreed about what they had seen.

Some said the forest man had dragged a burning rope. Some said his people wore long belts like tails. Some swore the tail had grown from his body. A child insisted it had moved by itself and set fire only to houses belonging to wicked people, though one of those houses belonged to a woman who sold sandals.

Within a week, the lower city called him the burning monkey.

Within a month, some people forgot there had ever been a man.

In the garden, the king looked at Shuka.

“You saw him in their camp?”

Shuka nodded slowly. “He commanded the forest companies along the eastern ridge.”

“On two legs?” the princess asked.

“Whenever I saw him.”

“Did the wind carry him?”

“Mostly his feet.”

Mala leaned upon her wet broom. “That version will never survive.”

Shuka looked at the monkey emblem in his memory. “His companies use the animal as their standard. They climb where other soldiers march. The northern men call them the people of the monkey.”

“A standard,” the king said. “Not a species.”

“Fear is not careful with grammar.”

The princess touched the ring beneath her cloth.

“Your soldiers called him an animal before he burned their roofs. His soldiers call your people demons before they kill them. Each side empties the other’s face.”

No one spoke.

Beyond the wall, a roof beam fell with a rush of sparks.

The princess continued.

“Soon they will say an army of beasts crossed the sea to fight a city of monsters. It will become a cleaner story.”

“Cleaner?” Shuka asked.

“No men in it.”

The king looked towards the pink flowers darkened by ash.

“Men are harder to burn,” he said.

“Only in memory,” she replied.

The captain of the women’s guard entered the garden. She bowed once and spoke quickly.

“The fire has crossed the first spice roof. We can hold the tiled passage, but the dry arbour beside the western wall must come down.”

Mala straightened. “The jasmine arbour?”

“Unless you know another dry arbour beside the western wall.”

“It flowers twice a year.”

“It will flower very brightly in a moment.”

The king pointed towards the gardeners. “Take it down. Clear everything between the arbour and the pool. Soak the pavilion screens. Move the sandalwood chest to the stone shrine.”

The princess looked at him. “You are allowing me to remain?”

“I am allowing Trijata to tell me whether you can remain.”

Trijata considered the wind, the roofs and the line of women passing water.

“Until the fire reaches the tiled passage,” she said. “After that, I will have her carried whether she forgives me or not.”

“Agreed.”

The princess opened her mouth.

Trijata raised one finger.

“I have buried two husbands,” she said. “I can endure your displeasure.”

The princess closed her mouth.

The king nodded to the guard captain. “Double the water line. Bring wet blankets from the bathhouse. No one climbs the western roof alone. If the wind turns north, clear the entire court.”

The captain left at once.

Mala hurried towards the jasmine arbour, stopped, returned and thrust a small plate of cut fruit into the king’s hands.

“Eat.”

The king looked down at it.

“Now?”

“Apparently this is when we change clothes and eat fruit.”

“I gave no order for fruit.”

Mala glanced towards the princess.

The princess looked at the burning sky.

The king understood.

“You noticed I had not eaten.”

“You are deciding whether to destroy half the garden,” she said. “I prefer the decision made by a man whose last meal was not yesterday.”

“You care whether I eat.”

“Do not build hope from a piece of melon.”

Shuka turned his face away, but not far enough.

The king took a piece.

It was overripe. Juice ran down his thumb.

“The fruit in my prison is usually better,” the princess said.

“At last you admit it is mine.”

“The fruit. Not the prison.”

Mala carried the plate to Shuka. “You also look hungry.”

“I am injured.”

“The melon cannot tell.”

She gave him two pieces and went to supervise the death of the jasmine arbour.

Workers cut through its lower supports. The old vines resisted, twisted around one another by years of growth. When the frame finally came down, it did not collapse neatly. It sighed into the path, releasing the green smell of broken stems and hundreds of small white flowers.

The princess watched Mala kneel among them and begin pulling living shoots away from the dry wood.

“She will try to save it,” the king said.

“Will she succeed?”

“She will tell us she has, regardless.”

The princess looked across the garden.

Women carried water. Women climbed ladders, kept lists, tied wet cloth across their mouths and ordered male labourers away from unstable tiles. The guard captain stood upon the wall directing the line. Nobody asked whether command made her less of a woman. Nobody praised her for being almost a man.

“In my husband’s kingdom,” the princess said, “a queen may command a thousand servants and still be judged by how quietly she follows one man.”

The king said nothing.

“When he went into exile, I followed by choice,” she continued. “Everyone praised my devotion because my choice carried me in the same direction as his duty. Had I chosen differently, they would have called it selfishness.”

“Here, my sister chose her own district, governed it and proposed marriage to the man she wanted.”

“And he humiliated her.”

“Yes.”

“Your women possess freedoms many northern women do not.”

The king’s face changed before he could prevent it. It was not triumph exactly, but it stood close enough to be recognised.

“Do not look pleased,” the princess said. “Their freedom does not become mine.”

The expression disappeared.

“I did not bring you here to make you a servant.”

“No. You brought me here to make another man answer you.”

“I gave orders that you were to be treated according to your rank.”

“I have been.”

The directness surprised him.

She looked down at the pale-pink cloth.

“I have been fed well. I have been given clean clothes, books, physicians and more concern for my hair than it has earned. No man has entered without permission. No one has struck me. No one has touched me except to care for me.”

The king waited.

“For months,” she said, “I have not carried water uphill. I have not cleaned a cooking pot. I have not woken in rain to discover that both men in my household have moved closer to the fire while leaving the wet wood for me.”

Shuka lowered his eyes to hide a smile.

“Some mornings I have enjoyed this place so much that I felt ashamed.”

The king looked at her.

“And then?”

“Then I decided shame was another household task and declined it.”

Trijata, directing wet blankets onto the pavilion roof, nodded approval.

The princess continued.

“Comfort is real. So is captivity. You keep trying to make one truth consume the other.”

“I have never harmed you.”

“You entered my home wearing the clothing of a holy traveller.”

The garden seemed to become quieter around them.

The king did not move.

“You asked me for water,” she said. “I crossed the threshold because refusing a hungry stranger would have shamed everything I had been taught. You used my kindness as a road into my house.”

The king looked at the fruit in his hand.

He had prepared answers for violence. He possessed explanations for armies, borders, murdered officers and his sister’s blood. Hospitality was more difficult. It belonged to the civilisation he claimed to defend.

“I needed to bring him into negotiation,” he said.

“Then you should have gone as a king.”

“He would have attacked me.”

“Perhaps.”

“Men would have died.”

“Men died afterward.”

“I did not know what would follow.”

“No,” she said. “You knew only what you wanted to follow.”

Shuka watched the king absorb the blow.

The princess’s voice softened, which made the words less merciful.

“That is what intelligent people call a mistake when they wish to keep respecting themselves.”

An alarm sounded beyond the western wall: three strokes, a pause, then two.

The king turned before the final stroke.

“Southern gate?” he called.

The guard captain answered from the wall. “Outer spice road lost. The fire crews are withdrawing to the tiled passage. No breach in the palace wall.”

“Move the public shelter from the dye yard. Open the western shrine crypt and send the children there. Keep the lower road clear for water.”

A runner repeated the order and disappeared through the screen.

The king returned his attention to the princess.

She had watched the change in him: accusation left aside, distances measured, lives sorted, fear given work to do. He was very good at being king when there was no time to think about why the danger existed.

Admiration reached her before anger could stop it.

She resented the speed of her own heart.

“You should have been a better man,” she said.

The king gave a tired smile. “I have been occupied.”

She almost smiled in return.

Almost was enough for both of them to notice.

“If I had come freely,” she said, “I might have loved this city.”

The words struck him more cleanly than hatred would have done.

“And its king?” he asked.

Trijata stopped arranging the blankets.

Shuka looked down at the melon.

The princess considered the man before her: smoke upon his clothes, water dried along his sleeves, fatigue beginning to hollow the skin beneath his eyes. He had built public wells and locked one private gate. He listened to flower sellers. He had entered her home through a lie. He had kept every soldier from her body and every road from her feet.

“I would have respected him,” she said.

The king waited for more.

None came.

“You understand me,” he said.

“Better than I want to.”

“You do not hate me.”

“No.”

Hope entered his face so briefly that she was sorry for what she had to do with it.

“That is not the gift you think it is,” she said.

The king looked away.

The fire crews reached the tiled passage. Water struck hot roofs with a sound like heavy rain. Steam moved through the garden and made the flowers shine.

Shuka rested both hands upon the arms of his chair.

“If the gate were opened now,” he asked the princess, “would you leave?”

“Yes.”

“Without waiting for your husband’s army?”

“Yes.”

The king looked back at her.

“Would you look behind you?” he asked.

She glanced towards Trijata on the pavilion roof, towards Mala kneeling among the broken jasmine and towards the pool in which pink flowers moved around a black fragment of ash.

“Yes.”

“At the city?”

“At many things.”

“At me?”

The princess met his eyes.

“Looking back is not the same as staying.”

The king finished the piece of melon.

The eastern sky beyond the garden roofs had begun to pale. It was not yet sunrise, but darkness had loosened around the edges of objects. The pink cloth upon the princess no longer borrowed its colour from fire.

A second runner arrived at the screen. She waited for permission, entered and bowed.

“Majesty, the eastern watch sees movement along the lower causeway. Many torches. The first companies will reach the outer fields before sunrise.”

“How long?”

“Less than an hour.”

The king gave orders without raising his voice. Close the market gates but leave the river gate open for civilians. Move the wounded from the eastern infirmary. Send no cavalry into the wet fields. Place archers above the potters’ quarter and tell them the houses beneath are occupied. No fire arrows within the city, whatever the enemy used.

The runner repeated each instruction and left.

The princess watched him.

“You still intend to fight.”

“Yes.”

“Knowing how it ends?”

“Knowing how someone says it ends.”

Shuka shifted in the chair.

“You told me four hours,” the king said to him.

“It is less now.”

“Time is behaving exactly as advised.”

Mala returned with a rescued bundle of jasmine roots wrapped in wet cloth.

“It will live,” she announced.

The king examined the broken brown stems.

“On what evidence?”

“It has not told me otherwise.”

“A standard used throughout government.”

She carried the roots towards the shrine.

The firebreak held.

The nearest roofs remained wet and dark. The orange light beyond the wall began to fade beneath the growing colour of morning. Women lowered empty jars, stretched aching shoulders and continued working because relief was another thing that could wait.

Trijata climbed down from the pavilion.

“She may remain,” she told the king. “For now.”

“If the wall is breached, take her below.”

“I heard you the first time.”

“I am the king. Repetition may succeed where authority has failed.”

Trijata looked almost amused.

The king turned to leave.

“Wait,” said the princess.

He stopped.

She looked at Shuka.

“You came from their camp. Will you return?”

“If I am alive when the gate opens.”

“And if you are not?”

“The king believes history will find someone less qualified.”

“It usually does.”

She walked towards him. The cord at her throat had slipped into view. Her husband’s ring rested against the pale-pink cloth.

“If you live,” she said, “tell them he gave me food. Tell them I slept beneath clean cloth. Tell them no man here touched me, and that the women they will call demons cared for me when I was ill.”

Shuka glanced at the king.

The princess did not.

“Tell them the city had laws. Tell them women spoke before men finished speaking and nobody struck them for it. Tell them I laughed here. Tell them there were mornings I was happy.”

The king’s face had become unreadable.

“Then tell them the gate was locked,” she said. “Tell them I asked to leave and could not. Do not let one truth devour the other.”

Shuka bowed his head.

“And the man who came through the tree?” he asked.

“Tell them he was a man.”

“They may prefer the monkey.”

“Preference is not memory.”

The king looked towards the ashoka tree beneath which the forest commander had first appeared.

“Songs prefer animals,” he said. “They rhyme more easily.”

“Then perhaps you should have treated your poets better.”

The king smiled despite himself.

At the garden screen, he took back his short blade. The captain of the women’s guard fastened it at his belt because his hands were occupied with the remaining fruit.

“Majesty,” the princess called.

He turned once more.

A pink petal had fallen upon his shoulder.

She raised her hand towards it, stopped before touching him and pointed instead.

The king brushed it away.

It landed between them.

The first light of morning passed through the locked gate without difficulty and laid itself, pink and quiet, across the floor.
