After the Songs

Chapter Seven

The Boat That Stayed

There are journeys made to return home, and journeys made to discover what home has become.

The first good news of the morning concerned water.

A channel beneath the western market had been cleared of stone and ash. The water-master had tested it twice. Three neighbourhoods that had been carrying drinking water uphill in jars for fourteen months could now draw it from the old public well.

The clerk reporting this was delighted. He had prepared a tablet describing the work, the number of labourers employed, the quantity of lime used, and the names of the families who had contributed oxen. He held the tablet as though it were a child whose examination marks had surprised everyone.

Vibhishana asked whether the water was safe.

“The water-master drank it, Majesty.”

“That is either reassuring or a sign that we are short of water-masters.”

“He drank from three cups.”

“Then he is very confident.”

Taranisena, who had been sorting the older inspection tablets at the far end of the table, looked up. For an instant the corners of his mouth moved. He lowered his eyes before the smile quite formed.

Vibhishana had begun giving his surviving son small pieces of work: checking that the names on supply lists matched the names on delivery receipts, sorting completed matters from those still awaiting an answer. At first Taranisena had been unable to remain in the room when a messenger arrived unexpectedly. Now he stayed, though he still flinched when a door struck its frame. He kept his sleeves pulled down over the pale marks on his hand, and he took care never to sit with his back to the entrance.

“Put that one with the wells awaiting the physicians,” Vibhishana said, passing him the tablet.

Taranisena read the notation before moving it. “Not with the completed wells?”

“Not until someone besides the water-master has survived drinking from it.”

This time his son did smile. It lasted only a moment, but Vibhishana saw it.

The clerk smiled. It was a small smile, quickly put away. There had been a time when laughter in this room came without permission and without apology. It had not entirely returned.

“Have the physicians tested it?” Vibhishana asked.

“They will today.”

“Keep the notices at the well until they have. No one drinks from it because a man with a title was thirsty.”

He marked the tablet and placed it on the completed side of the table.

There were more tablets on that side now.

For months the completed side had been an almost insulting little space. A cart repaired. A lane cleared. Six sacks of grain delivered without being stolen. Now there were lists of wells, schoolrooms, repaired quays, fishermen granted nets, births properly registered, debts forgiven after an appeal. The palace roof no longer leaked into the west archive. A public kitchen that had served four hundred people in the first month now served sixty, and some days fewer.

The city had begun to make its own noises again.

Below the window a carpenter was arguing with a potter over the right of a loaded cart to occupy the narrow lane. Their quarrel had become so involved that neither seemed able to remember which one had intended to pass first. Behind them a boy pushed a tray of sweet cakes between customers, calling out their price in the old market rhythm. Somewhere farther away a bronze worker struck a bowl and listened to the note until the metal stopped answering.

Fourteen months earlier, the same lane had been full of men carrying bodies.

Vibhishana still looked down whenever he crossed it.

The treasurer came after the water-master's clerk. He had no good news, though he had taken the trouble to arrange his figures as pleasantly as possible.

“Our grain deliveries are keeping pace with consumption,” he said.

“Because of the harvest?”

“Partly.”

“And the rest?”

“The northern agreement.”

Vibhishana nodded.

Rama had promised eighteen months of economic support after the war. Grain, tools, seed stock, timber, trained surveyors, access to markets that had once closed their harbours to Lankan ships. The agreement had been written in the language of restoration rather than tribute. Its shipments had arrived regularly. Vibhishana had seen children grow stronger because of them. He had watched shops reopen along roads made usable by crews sent from the north.

He was grateful. He had no difficulty saying so.

But the treasurer was pointing to a column of figures which refused to improve.

“When the assistance ends,” Vibhishana said, “we shall be able to feed how many?”

“With an ordinary harvest?”

“An ordinary one.”

“Perhaps seven in ten.”

“And if the fishing fleet has another poor season?”

The treasurer said nothing.

Four months remained of the original economic undertaking. Vibhishana had been learning to distinguish a repaired market from a recovered economy. A market could be roofed, swept and painted. A dead merchant did not return because the shutters on his shop had been replaced. A shipwright's son did not inherit the knowledge his father had carried into battle. Fields did not become productive merely because the survey tablets said the fields belonged to someone again.

“We shall need to ask for more,” the treasurer said.

“Yes.”

“Majesty, I know that displeases you.”

“It displeases me that we need it. I would rather have a kingdom that could thank its friends and then feed itself.”

He looked towards the harbour, where new masts stood among old broken ones.

“Prepare the figures without making them kinder. We must ask for what we need, not what allows us to appear proud.”

The extension of military assistance had arrived almost five months earlier.

Vibhishana remembered the morning of the request because he had drafted it himself, then sent it back to be rewritten in more dignified language, then discovered that dignity had made the meaning less clear. The original undertaking was for nine months. Lanka could not yet defend its roads or patrol its waters without help. He wrote that plainly on the final tablet.

He had expected questions. Perhaps conditions. A demand for additional oversight of the ports, or a new arrangement for customs revenues. He had rehearsed the difficult conversation in his head before the courier had even departed.

Rama's answer came sooner than the council expected.

Six more months of military assistance. No new condition attached. The resources to remain in place while Lanka completed its own training and repairs.

Vibhishana read the answer twice before showing it to his ministers.

The generosity was real. He knew that. He also knew that it would outlive the good opinion of some of the men sent to carry it out.

At the fourteenth month, the muster books showed that six parts in ten of the kingdom's deployable land and naval strength were now Lankan. Not the army his brother had commanded. That army had been broken beyond rebuilding. These were survivors, recruits, craftsmen who had learned new duties, fishermen trained to carry dispatches and keep bearings, younger officers promoted before they felt old enough to give orders, and men who had returned from captivity too thin to wear the armour that had once fitted them.

Four parts in ten remained allied.

It was an improvement. It was also a very large number of armed strangers.

The new harbour command had restored night signalling, inspected the reef markers, and brought three seaworthy patrol vessels back into service. Its officers now kept their own registers instead of relying upon copies supplied by allied administrators. Water was reaching settlements that had been cut off. Food passed through checkpoints more reliably. A man who disappeared into custody was more likely to have his name written down.

More likely was not the same as certain.

The northern temples had received new roofs and orderly accounts. A school in the old potters' quarter had opened with teachers who could read both Lankan and northern forms of script. Traders who once avoided the coast were returning under the protection of the new agreements. Some citizens greeted the sight of an allied banner with genuine relief.

Others crossed the street.

Vibhishana had begun noticing how often these two responses occurred within the same family.

There were men among Rama's forces who gave their pay to kitchens, carried injured labourers home, and stood watch at wells so that women could draw water without being pushed aside. There were men who had come to Lanka because they believed helping rebuild it was honourable work.

And there were others.

Near the restored market, a Lankan patrol captain had intervened when three allied soldiers demanded that an old basket seller shout Jai Shri Ram before they would let him pass. The seller had stood bewildered, a basket hanging from each arm. One soldier knocked the baskets into the gutter. Another laughed as the old man bent to rescue them.

The Lankan captain ordered the soldiers away.

“We serve Rama,” one said.

“Then serve him well,” the captain replied. “Help him gather the baskets.”

The soldier's expression changed. For a moment the market held still. Then a companion bent to lift the scattered fruit, and the captain helped the old man stand.

It was recorded as a dispute resolved without injury.

In a side street that afternoon, out of sight of any patrol, other soldiers made a man repeat the chant until his voice failed. They called him a Rakshasa as though the word itself explained what he deserved. One spat near his feet. Another performed the rolling, swaying dance the allied camps had made fashionable—arms held out without touching, hips circling, faces passing from fierce concentration into a smile of unbearable satisfaction.

A child who watched from a doorway copied the movement after they had gone.

His mother seized his wrist and dragged him inside.

The incident did not reach a tablet. The man who had been humiliated could not identify the soldiers and was afraid to describe them. He knew how to survive an afternoon. The government knew how to record a complaint. These were not yet the same thing.

Vibhishana had issued orders against harassment. He had removed commanders who ignored them. He had argued with allied officers over arrests made without registers and searches conducted without witnesses. He had won some of those arguments. He had lost others. He had come to recognise the slight change in a room when a man decided to obey him only because it was convenient.

He did not regret placing Lanka under Rama's supremacy. He believed in the alliance and had seen the good that came from it. Rama had helped him when he had little leverage with which to ask. Without northern food and northern soldiers, the kingdom might have broken again before its wounds had closed.

But at night, when the streets became quiet enough for sounds to travel, Vibhishana sometimes heard that chant drifting from distant barracks and could not tell whether the people answering had done so freely.

That was an uncertainty he had not learned how to govern.

The petitions about the missing never stopped.

A grandmother came with a child's sandal wrapped in cloth and asked whether anyone in the western shelters had seen its pair. A man brought the name of a wife who had been declared dead in one register and transferred to another district in a second. Two brothers spent half a day arguing over whether a line in the burial records referred to their father, who had a common name, or to a different man whose age had not been written down.

During the first months, officials had been praised for producing figures quickly. The living had needed food; the dead needed burial; the displaced needed somewhere to sleep before the rains arrived. A number written on a tablet allowed a clerk to move to the next emergency.

Fourteen months later, the numbers remained, and the families had not moved on.

Vibhishana ordered the evacuation and recovery reports brought to the long room overlooking the eastern court. He asked for the original tablets where they survived, not the clean copies made for the treasury. He asked for names of witnesses, routes taken, vessels used, locations of wreckage, reports of burial, and the signatures of men who had decided a search was complete.

The long table filled.

On the first morning there were two hundred and thirteen unresolved entries. By the fourth, they had found more.

The registrar was a careful man who had learned, during the recovery, to say we do not know without looking ashamed. He placed a thin tablet before the king.

“Twenty-seven workers missing from the southern quarry.”

“Who searched it?”

“An allied detachment and four local labourers.”

“Were the lower shafts opened?”

“They were recorded as unsafe.”

“Have they been opened since?”

“No, Majesty.”

“Then send an engineer first. If the shafts can be entered, send the searchers after him.”

The next record concerned forty-six people who had been moved from a damaged hospital. They were counted at departure, not at arrival. Eight names appeared in a different hospital register, each spelled differently.

“Ask the physicians,” Vibhishana said. “They may remember what the clerks did not.”

An officer objected that a caravan route had already been searched twice.

“Did you find the caravan?”

“No.”

“Did you find the place where it was lost?”

“No.”

“Then you have searched the road twice. That is not the same thing.”

He did not raise his voice.

There were reports from the river settlements, the old kiln district, a convoy of sick soldiers, seven boats last seen near the southern headlands. There were cremation registers with blank lines where names should have been. There were graves with names but no bodies identified beyond reasonable doubt. Vibhishana demanded a second inspection of burial grounds before authorising the closure of more cases. Where a family had no certainty, he wanted the tablet to say so.

The work was expensive. An admiral reminded him that every ship sent to investigate a possible loss was a ship unavailable to carry supplies or guard the coast.

“I know,” Vibhishana said.

“And some of these people will not be found.”

“I know that too.”

The admiral waited.

“Put their names on the next search orders.”

At midday a clerk read from a tablet made by an allied recovery vessel.

“One hundred and three children recovered from the island of the eastern passage. Two hundred and six reported lost before reaching the island.”

The king's eyes remained on the preceding document.

“Who counted the missing?”

“The recovery officer, Majesty.”

“From which list?”

“The report does not say.”

“Where was the vessel that carried them?”

“There is no vessel identified.”

“Wreckage?”

“None recorded.”

“Bodies?”

“None recorded.”

Vibhishana looked up.

“You have two hundred and six missing children and no account of the moment they became missing.”

The clerk moved his finger down the tablet, searching for a line that might help.

“It was an allied ship, Majesty. Thirteen months ago. The officer reported that the children had been lost before the ship reached the island.”

“Did he search the island?”

“He brought back one hundred and three.”

“That was not my question.”

The registrar lifted his head. He had spent the morning listening to the same questions asked of one unsatisfactory report after another.

“Shall I assign the original crew to return?” he asked.

“Yes,” Vibhishana said. “Have them—”

He stopped.

For a moment the writing on the tablet seemed to recede from him.

He remembered a boatman speaking of a woman who had come to the harbour after the evacuation lists were sealed. Well dressed despite the torn cloth. A girl lifted aboard. No name entered. In the weeks after that report, he had imagined his wife there so often that imagination had become a kind of pain. He had never learned whether the woman was she. The witness had vanished into the damaged settlements; the girl's boat had not been identified.

He remembered his wife's hand around Analā's wrist. Not as a witnessed scene—he had not been at the harbour—but as he had pictured it every night since the boatman's account. Her grip would have been firm. She would have refused to be turned away. She would have promised their daughter something she could not keep.

He looked again at the two hundred and six.

“Wait.”

The registrar put down his stylus.

“Send a Lankan vessel.”

“The same destination?”

“Yes. A captain from our own harbour service. A registrar who can take names himself. A physician, provisions, room for passengers. They are to inspect the landing site and speak to the people who received the first boats. If no one is found, they search the neighbouring coves. And they write down what they know, not what someone tells them must have happened.”

The admiral nodded.

“The vessel can be provisioned in two days.”

“Then provision it.”

“Shall we mark this a royal search?”

“They are all royal searches.”

The clerk turned to the next tablet.

“Thirty-one missing from the north canal—”

“Begin with the ferrymen,” Vibhishana said, and bent again over the names.

He did not know that a girl whose name was missing from his records had learned to mend fishing nets, hold a baby on her hip and make herself happy in a house he had never seen.

He did not know that she had stopped watching the harbour for him.

The captain received his orders in a room that smelled of pitch and drying sailcloth.

His name was Māran. He had served under two harbour masters before the war, lost a younger brother at the northern gate, and spent much of the last year teaching new sailors the difference between a reef marker and a fishing lantern. He did not speak loudly. His men listened because he seldom required a second instruction.

His vessel was a restored Lankan patrol ship, built low and broad enough to keep its footing in the southern currents. A dark-blue band ran beneath the rail; on the stern, newly cut into a square of teak, was the old Lankan harbour emblem: a silver fish beneath three small waves. The same mark had been painted, more modestly, at the upper corner of its white sail. There were no garlands or ceremonial banners. The deck was clear, the lines neatly coiled, the oars secured where a man could reach them in a hurry. Māran had insisted that the ship sail as a working vessel, not a proclamation.

The route tablet was old enough to have lost one corner. A mariner had incised the passage in short lines:

Southern departure. Keep the black headland astern. Beyond the double reef, turn with the eastern water. Island harbour before the following dawn, if the night wind holds.

It was the passage the evacuation boats had taken in fear, beneath smoke and uncertain lamps. A navy vessel with a sound hull, experienced crew and favourable wind could make it in a long day or a night. The departure preparations would take longer than the voyage.

Māran traced the turn beyond the reef.

“What did the first report say about the people there?” he asked.

“Only that one hundred and three children were returned.”

“And the two hundred and six?”

“Lost before arrival.”

He examined the blank spaces beneath the figure.

“Lost where?”

“No location recorded.”

“Then we begin with the harbour, not the number.”

Two days later his ship cast off shortly after sunrise. The harbour lines fell into the water with soft splashes. The vessel passed the repaired quay, the half-built warehouse, and the broken stone at the outer wall which no mason had yet found time to replace.

A boy on the quay waved.

Māran raised his hand in answer.

He had no idea whom he was going to meet.

On the island, Ilā had discovered that people would sit still for a story longer than they would sit still for a lesson.

This was useful, because she disliked lessons in which someone told her when she was allowed to speak.

She gathered the younger children in the shade of the tamarind tree and drew ten circles in the dust with the end of a reed. She put a small stone in each circle and asked what a king ought to know.

“How to fight,” said Dev.

“How to eat,” said a younger boy.

“Everybody knows how to eat,” Ilā said.

“My brother doesn't. He puts his fingers in the pot.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“How to build boats,” said a girl whose father had been a carpenter.

“How to find clean water,” another offered.

“How to listen,” Analā said from the step, where she was mending a tear in Mēni's cloth.

Ilā looked up.

“That's not a subject.”

“It should be.”

Ilā added a mark beside one of the stones.

The children leaned forward. They had begun calling these the ten minds, though no one could agree on whether the list ought to contain medicine or farming or music or navigation. Ilā changed the order whenever a younger child forgot it. She had discovered that a rhyme could make even a difficult word remain in a child's head.

“Did he really have ten minds?” the little boy asked.

“No,” Analā said.

Ilā said, “Don't spoil the beginning.”

“He had one. He asked other people for help.”

“I know that.”

“Then tell them.”

Ilā considered the circles in the dust.

“Perhaps,” she said, “that was his cleverest mind.”

The children approved of this answer. It was short enough to remember.

Analā smiled despite herself.

Fourteen months had passed since the night the evacuation boats left Lanka. On the island, Analā measured that time by other things: the height of Mēni's son against the doorpost, the growing distance between Ilā's old hem and her knees, the number of fishing knots Analā could now tie without looking. She had grown taller too. Her hands had become broad and sure where they had once been soft, and the new skin along her fingers no longer drew her attention.

She rose before anyone asked her to. She knew the place of the cooking pot, the roof tile that slipped in storms, the hour when Sēnan liked to see the day's catch counted. Mēni no longer thanked her for every task. She shouted when Analā forgot to bring in the washing and asked her to taste the soup before it was salted. Sometimes she scolded Ilā and Analā together, without making a distinction between the daughter she had borne and the daughter the sea had delivered.

Analā liked this most of all.

At breakfast Ilā took the crisp edge from Analā's flatbread. Analā reached across and took Ilā's whole bread in return. Mēni ordered them to exchange the food back. Neither obeyed until Mēni threatened to eat both portions herself.

Analā laughed. It was not the laugh she had given in the palace corridors, quick and bright and ready to summon another joke. It came less often now, and when it did it seemed to surprise her. But it was an ordinary laugh, and she did not apologise for it.

There were days when she worked for hours without speaking more than necessary. Ilā had learned not to fill every such silence. Sometimes she sat nearby and talked to the baby instead. Sometimes she simply made room for Analā on the step.

The name Vibhishana had stopped being an argument Analā rehearsed aloud. It was still there. A small hard thing she carried quietly. She could look across the northern water without wanting to cry or shout. She could remember her father tying a cord around a child's wrist, or laughing beside his brother, and finish the chore in her hands. Yet every so often she wondered how a man could have done all the things she had heard and still have been the man she remembered.

She never found an answer while washing pots.

One afternoon, when Sēnan asked her to count the provisions stored for the younger children, she completed the task and came back with the precise number of sacks, the number with damaged seams, and the number likely to last until the next traders came.

“You've made the list shorter,” he said.

“I've made it legible.”

“Those are not always the same thing.”

“They should be.”

He studied her with the expression of a man who had learned not to be surprised when a quiet girl said something that ought to have been obvious to everybody.

“I suppose they should.”

Ilā had begun taking her stories to people who had never seen Lanka.

At first she told them to the island's youngest children. Then the fishermen asked her to repeat a verse while they repaired nets. A woman from a neighbouring cove heard it and carried the words home. When a trader returned from another island, he recited a line about a king who remained behind so that the children could escape.

Ilā was delighted.

“That's mine,” she whispered to Analā.

“It's about my uncle.”

“I know. But those words are mine.”

The trader had changed them.

In his version Ravana had stood on the very deck of the last boat and lifted each child aboard with his own hands. He had then stepped ashore again and faced an army alone.

Analā listened, her fingers motionless on the net she was repairing.

“He didn't come aboard,” she said when the trader finished.

“That's how the story was told to me.”

“I was there. I saw him on the cliff.”

The trader looked embarrassed.

“Then I have learned something.”

He changed the line when Ilā asked him to repeat it. But by evening she heard a child singing the old version, in which Ravana carried the children through fire.

Ilā tried to correct him.

The child protested that the old version sounded better.

“But it didn't happen,” Analā said.

The child lowered his eyes.

“Did he save us?”

“Yes.”

“Then what does it matter how?”

Analā opened her mouth, then closed it.

She remembered the last sight of her uncle against the burning sky. Her hand lifted above the boat. No answering movement she could be sure of. A man she knew to be human, standing far away, too small at that distance for her to read his face.

“It matters to me,” she said at last.

Ilā put down her reed.

That evening the two girls argued while they fetched water. Ilā said a story had to be carried by people who could remember it. Analā said people could remember things that were true. Ilā said she had tried teaching the children a list of ten disciplines and they had forgotten half of them before supper. Analā said her uncle would have laughed at any list that made him sound as though he had never needed anybody.

They reached Mēni's house with the jars only half full because they had been walking too quickly and spilling water.

Mēni looked at the wet marks on their clothes.

“Were you bringing water home or teaching it to dance?”

“Ilā thinks a lie is better if it rhymes,” Analā said.

“I do not,” Ilā protested.

“Analā thinks no one should enjoy a story unless they were there,” Ilā said.

Mēni took the jars.

“Then neither of you will enjoy washing the floor.”

They washed it together, still arguing in whispers, then began laughing at how little water their argument had delivered.

The next morning Ilā changed the verse. It was less beautiful. Analā helped her find a rhythm that kept the cliff and the departing boats in the right order.

By afternoon the children were singing it.

It was the first story they had made together knowing that other people might repeat it.

The sail appeared before midday.

A child carrying fish heads towards the drying racks saw the pale triangle beyond the reef. He dropped his basket and ran uphill shouting the warning used ever since the first rope ship had come.

Sēnan stepped out of the storehouse and shaded his eyes. From the shore the approaching vessel looked unmistakably naval: its steady approach, its clear deck and the little square of dark blue showing beneath the white sail. Then it turned, and he caught sight of a silver fish beneath three waves. He knew the mark. It had belonged to Lanka's harbour service long before the war. It was not one of the saffron-marked supply craft that had come shouting for rope.

For the first time in many months, the sight of an unfamiliar ship made him feel something perilously close to relief. He did not send the children to meet it. He had learned the cost of being certain too soon.

Within moments the island began its familiar rearrangement. A mother gathered children from the shore. A door was closed. A basket of unfinished bread was lifted from the ground and carried into the house. Mēni took her son from the courtyard and settled him against her shoulder.

Ilā reached for the little ones.

Analā counted them.

She had stopped asking why people needed to hide. Her hands knew what to do before the question formed. She moved the smallest children to the path behind the houses, checked that Dev had not gone to the rocks alone, and sent an older boy to bring two girls who were gathering shells.

Then she looked at the vessel.

Its approach was different.

There was no shouted victory cry. No drums. It came in under a controlled sail, slowing before the reef marker while a man in the bow took a sounding. The helmsman waited for the harbour signal instead of pushing in as though the island's water belonged to him.

Sēnan went down to the landing place with two elders.

Analā joined the children in the grove all the same.

She sat beside a girl who was worrying the hem of her tunic into a rope of twisted threads. There was a time when Analā would have looked towards the sea with a dreadful, unreasonable hope that her father might be aboard. She felt no such hope now. She was listening for the tone of the first voice from the harbour.

They heard nothing for a long while.

At the quay, Māran had lowered a small boat before he brought his vessel alongside. He came ashore with a registrar and two sailors. The rest remained aboard to tend the ship.

He waited while Sēnan crossed the stones.

“I am Māran of the southern harbour service,” he said. “I have orders to review an evacuation report from this island.”

Sēnan looked at the seal, then at the man's face. The silver fish was stamped beneath the writing, just as it had been on the sail. He had hoped for that mark all morning, and for much longer than a morning.

“Under whose authority?” he asked, though he already knew what the seal said.

“The Lankan crown.”

Sēnan let the words settle. Behind him were households that had stretched their rice for fourteen months, mothers who had slept beside children not born to them, and men who had taken extra boats out in rough weather because there were too many mouths waiting for supper. None had asked for a reward. None had believed the responsibility could last forever.

“You have come for rope?” he asked, almost as a test.

“No. We've come to look again for the people the war left behind.”

Sēnan pressed his lips together. He had imagined this answer so often that hearing it made him wary of his own relief.

“Then,” he said, “perhaps we have finally had the right kind of visitor.”

Māran followed his glance to a stack of newly twisted cordage beside the warehouse. Sēnan told him, with the restraint of a man who had rehearsed the account many times, about the soldiers who had taken rope without payment. He spoke of the recovery ship, more than a year earlier, that had demanded the number of Rakshasa children and frightened the islanders into a lie.

Māran listened without interrupting. He asked for descriptions and dates. He asked whether anyone had been injured. His registrar scratched the answers onto a tablet. Only when Sēnan had finished did the captain ask why the island had never sent word.

“Fourteen months,” Māran said. “You had boats. Traders called here. Why did no one row to Lanka and tell us there were children alive?”

Sēnan looked at the water between the quay stones.

“To whom would I have sent him?”

“To a harbour master. To the navy. To the king.”

“Which harbour master? We heard the war was over, Captain. We heard your king had his crown. Then a ship came from Lanka and men frightened the children into hiding. They asked how many Rakshasas we had. I told them one hundred and three children could be taken. They took those children and left. Later other ships came from Lanka. Men shouting Rama's name took our rope. Once they didn't even pay. We heard that Rakshasa families were being hunted. Tell me which of those men I should have sent a boatman to ask for help.”

“Those men do not represent the navy I serve.”

“Perhaps not. But they came from Lanka. How was I to know the difference?”

Māran did not answer at once.

“You could have sent a man without the children's names,” he said.

“And asked him to find out whether it was safe to give you their names? Suppose he came back followed by a ship like the first. Suppose he never came back at all. We had no signal line to your shore, no office whose word we could test. I had two hundred and six children, Captain. I could not risk them to find out whether a rumour was true.”

“So you believed the worst.”

“No. Every time a sail appeared, I hoped it would be different. I thought if Lanka was becoming a kingdom again, a ship like yours would come sooner or later. I did not think it would take fourteen months.”

“And if we had not come?”

“Then I would have had to decide again.” Sēnan looked towards the houses. “Do not mistake waiting for certainty. Every month we wondered whether we were doing right. But I knew what we might lose if we were wrong.”

Māran looked at the two elders standing with Sēnan. Their faces showed neither triumph nor apology.

“We will carry your account back,” the captain said. “I cannot promise what will follow it. I can promise that the names and dates will not disappear between this shore and ours.”

The islanders were not yet willing to believe promises from men in uniform.

Māran seemed to understand. He asked permission to buy water and food for his crew. When the price was given, he paid it before the jars were loaded.

Sēnan watched the coins counted into the fisherman's palm.

“That is a new sight,” he said.

“It ought not to be.”

They stood for a moment without speaking.

Then Māran asked about the children.

“The report says one hundred and three were recovered here,” the registrar said.

“They were,” Sēnan answered.

“And two hundred and six were lost before arrival.”

Sēnan looked towards the houses above the harbour.

“No,” he said.

The registrar lifted his stylus.

Sēnan took a breath.

“No. Two hundred and six remained here.”

The captain's expression did not change at once. Perhaps he had misheard. Perhaps he had spent too long looking at numbers to accept one spoken sentence over an official tablet.

“Remained?”

“Alive.”

“All of them?”

“As far as my register says. We have buried none of those children.”

Māran turned towards the registrar.

“Read the figure again.”

The man did.

Sēnan had expected anger. He had imagined that admitting the lie would bring armed men into the houses and send the children running again. He had carried that fear for more than a year.

Instead, Māran sat down on an overturned crate.

“Two hundred and six,” he said quietly. “And no one came back.”

“No one came back to ask properly.”

The words sounded harsher than Sēnan intended. He did not withdraw them.

After a long silence the captain asked whether he could see the register.

Sēnan led him to the storehouse.

The book lay beneath a folded sailcloth where he kept it away from damp. He had written every child's name when the boats arrived, then added households, illnesses, changes of residence and the departures of the one hundred and three. A few pages had been repaired at the edges. Some names had been written so many times on scraps during the first weeks that they had become almost painfully familiar.

The registrar read the list aloud in a low voice while Māran followed with his finger.

He came to the page on which Analā's entry stood.

He stopped.

The name was ordinary enough in the lines above and below it. A girl. An age. A community. A household. Beneath it, written later with darker ink, was her father's name.

Analā. Daughter of Vibhishana.

“Who wrote this?” Māran asked.

“I did.”

“Who gave you the name?”

“The girl herself. And later we confirmed it as well as we could from what she knew.”

“Is she alive?”

Sēnan felt a sudden fear, different from the fear he had known when the drunken officer stood before him.

“Yes.”

“Here?”

“Yes.”

Māran put one hand flat on the table to steady the page.

For a moment Sēnan saw that the man in front of him had forgotten his rank. His eyes were wide with the effort of imagining the consequences of what he had just read.

“The king has searched for this child,” Māran said.

Sēnan did not answer.

“The king has searched for her since the fall of the city.”

“She told us he knew nothing of her,” Sēnan said.

“That was the truth.”

Sēnan looked down at his register.

He had feared that a government which could not find children might come to take them by force. He had not imagined a government in which a father was searching for the very girl he had been taught to regard as dead.

“May I speak to her?” Māran asked.

Sēnan thought of the grove, the path behind Mēni's house, the children tucked out of sight by a routine they no longer questioned.

“I will ask her,” he said.

Analā came down alone.

She had been told that a Lankan captain wanted to speak to her. She washed the flour from her hands and tied her hair back. Ilā walked with her as far as the bend above the harbour, then stopped when Analā asked her to.

“Do you want me to come?” Ilā said.

“No.”

“I could stand outside and look frightening.”

“You would have to stop smiling.”

“I can stop smiling.”

“You've never managed it for long.”

Ilā tried to look severe. Her mouth betrayed her.

Analā touched her arm and went down the path.

Māran stood when she entered the storehouse. Sēnan remained by the door. The registrar's hand hovered above the tablet.

“Are you Analā?” the captain asked.

“Yes.”

“Daughter of Vibhishana?”

“Yes.”

Māran swallowed.

“Your father has been looking for you.”

Analā kept her eyes on him.

For a year she had heard the same man's name in stories of betrayal, authority, punishment and royal decrees. She had pictured him sitting on her uncle's throne. She had stopped waiting for him. To hear, now, that he had been looking for her did not bring back the child who had watched the boats.

“How does he know I might be alive?” she asked.

“He doesn't. Not yet.”

“Then how do you know he is looking?”

“Because his officers have spent fourteen months searching. I have seen the orders.”

“Has he found my brother Arun?”

Māran's expression changed.

“I have no report of him.”

“And Taranisena?”

“He is alive. He is in Lanka.”

Analā's fingers moved towards the blue cord at her throat, then fell to her side. For an instant her face seemed younger. She turned slightly, as if she needed to look through the open door at something steady.

“Taranisena is alive,” she repeated.

“Yes.”

She nodded once.

Māran waited. He did not tell her she ought to be happy. He had lost people himself and knew that good news could uncover a grief it did not cure.

“Will you take me to him?” she asked at last.

“I can take you to Lanka. Your father will wish to see you.”

“I know.”

“When the ship can safely sail, I will take you back to Lanka,” Māran said.

Analā looked at Sēnan.

For fourteen months he had helped make a life for her and the other children. She had known that one day someone might come from Lanka; knowing it had not prepared her for the sound of a captain setting out her departure as an ordinary fact.

“All right,” she said.

Māran waited, perhaps expecting a question about her father's house. She asked none. There was nothing to settle about the journey itself. There were things to settle when it ended.

He bowed his head and went back to his work.

Māran had intended to leave as soon as the register had been verified. He ordered the ship watered, the passenger space cleared and the navigator to prepare the reef passage. Before the afternoon was over, the wind turned hard against the harbour. The tide pushed a long swell across the outer rocks. A pilot returning from the reef brought back a wet rope and a simple judgment: they could enter the passage, but he would not promise they could come out of it with a crowded ship intact.

Māran went to the quay himself. The vessel was sound. The sail had been inspected before departure from Lanka, the seams had been checked, and nothing on board had failed. But beyond the reef white water lifted where the safe channel ought to have lain.

“We could try with a light boat,” one sailor said.

“And with children?” Māran asked.

The sailor looked away.

The captain had found a daughter whom the king believed lost. To risk drowning her now, for the sake of reaching the harbour one day earlier, was not diligence. It was vanity. He ordered the ship secured, its departure ready for the first genuinely safe passage.

No message had gone ahead of the ship. The island had no signal line to Lanka; a message as particular as a child's name needed a boat to carry it. Neither the fishing craft nor the few traders sheltering in the inlet could cross the reef safely now. Māran entered the discovery in his log, told the navigator to wake him if the water changed, and kept the news with him.

Rain struck the harbour that night so hard the mooring ropes seemed to hum. Before dawn it stopped. Sēnan went down to the quay and found Māran standing beside the navigator. The sky was opening in the east; beyond the outer stones the sea was still breaking white across the passage.

“Will it clear by afternoon?” Māran asked.

“It might,” the navigator said.

“Tomorrow?”

“I can tell you where the water is breaking, Captain. I cannot tell you when it will stop.”

Māran watched another wave lift over the reef. Somewhere across that water a father still believed his daughter lost. He ordered the ship kept ready and turned to the registrar. There was work to do while they waited; he would not pretend that work was the reason they stayed.

Sēnan refused to let the children be divided into convenient groups for the sake of completing a manifest. He knew that the youngest had become attached to households whose names were not in Lanka's records. He knew which children still woke screaming and which no longer remembered the faces of their parents. He knew that a girl who had lost her whole family might insist she was ready to travel because her friend was leaving, then become frightened when the ship's rope touched the quay.

The registrar listened to him.

They began with the facts: name, age, where a child had been living before the war, whether any surviving relative was known, whether there was a place to return to. Then they asked the children and the households caring for them what they wanted.

Not every child knew.

Dev wanted to go to Lanka because he had an aunt in the eastern quarter. He could not remember her street, only a fig tree outside her door and the habit she had of calling everyone by the wrong name. He spent an hour describing the tree to the registrar, who wrote down every detail as though it were a navigational bearing.

A girl whose mother had died refused to board without the woman who had taken her in. The captain explained that no one was making her leave. She asked him to say it again, then asked Sēnan if the captain meant it.

A boy who had spoken of returning home for months went silent when the ship lowered its gangplank.

“I thought they would come for us,” he told Analā. “I didn't think we would have to go.”

Analā sat beside him on the warehouse steps until he was ready to stand.

A few families had come to the island with no children of their own. They had taken in frightened strangers and made room around hearths that had been quiet for years. Now they were being asked to think about goodbye. Some did so generously, some resentfully, some with an honesty that made the registrar uncomfortable. One man admitted that feeding two extra mouths through another poor fishing season would be difficult. His foster son heard him and walked away. Later the man found him on the beach and explained that wanting help did not mean wanting the boy gone.

The quartermaster inspected the cleared space below deck, counted the drinking-water jars, and gave Māran a number. With the present crew, attendants, stores and the likelihood of another difficult crossing, they could carry thirty-one children safely. No more. Analā would be among them. The other one hundred and seventy-five would remain with their island households for now.

Sēnan heard the figure and put down his stylus.

“No,” he said.

Māran looked up. “The ship cannot safely hold two hundred and six.”

“I know how many it holds. I also know what happened the last time a ship took some children and left the rest. You cannot sail away with thirty-one and expect me to trust that the others will be remembered.”

“I will send more ships.”

“In another ten months?”

Māran regarded him for a moment. “What do you require?”

“Food. Medicines. A date. And a record that does not say children were lost when they are sitting here waiting.”

“Then we will write it properly.”

They sat together over the register. Māran dictated a formal acknowledgement of all two hundred and six survivors: thirty-one to travel on this voyage, one hundred and seventy-five to remain temporarily under Sēnan's care. His own name, command and the royal naval seal went below it. A second tablet ordered a follow-up vessel, with space for further passengers and enough grain, pulses, oil, salt and medical stores for the remaining children for three weeks, plus an additional week's reserve. He directed that the ship be dispatched within seven days of his return, sea conditions permitting, and that delay be reported rather than silently allowed to become another disappearance. One sealed copy would travel to the harbour authorities; the other stayed with Sēnan.

“We can't leave you a month's food from a patrol vessel,” Māran said. “The holds were provisioned for the crew. But the quartermaster will leave the stores we can spare without making the crossing unsafe. The rest is a requisition to be filled from Lanka, under my responsibility.”

“And if you are not believed?” Sēnan asked.

“Then the record will show whose order was refused.”

Sēnan touched the seal with one finger. A mark on clay could not feed a child, and a date could not calm a storm. But this time the children had not been reduced to an unverified number in a departing officer's account. Their names, their households and the obligations owed to them were written on both shores of the same decision.

Māran arranged for letters to be carried and the children's family details to be checked at the harbour offices in Lanka. He could not promise that every child would find a surviving relative. He would not take a child simply because an old register named a home that might no longer exist.

The first departure list contained thirty-one names, including Dev and Analā. It did not include every child who wished to leave. The others would be offered later passages as their circumstances were traced, and those whose island households wished to keep them would be treated as people with ties to both shores, not cargo awaiting collection.

That afternoon the clouds passed west and sunlight lay across the harbour. The children rushed down to see whether the ship was leaving. The sailors had not touched the gangplank. Beyond the clear water inside the inlet, waves still struck the reef hard enough to throw spray above the marker. Māran asked the pilot again. The answer had not changed.

Sēnan looked at the list for a long time.

He had wanted, for more than a year, for the children to have somewhere to go.

Now he had to learn how to let them go there.

Ilā's first response to the list was to become very busy.

She helped pack Dev's clothes. She taught a girl from the far end of the village the new verse about the cliff. She made certain that everyone who was leaving knew the ten minds in the correct order, although she and Analā had still not agreed upon that order themselves.

“Medicine before music,” Analā said.

“Why?”

“Because someone who is ill cannot enjoy music.”

“People enjoy music when they're ill.”

“Then medicine after music.”

“You're only agreeing to stop me talking.”

“I thought that was impossible.”

Ilā threw a folded cloth at her.

They were sitting beside Mēni's doorway while the younger children packed the few possessions they owned. Ilā had drawn ten circles on a piece of bark for Dev to carry. In each she had written one word, with help from Analā when she could not remember how to form a letter. She tied it in cloth to keep it dry.

“Will you tell them the stories when you go?” Ilā asked him.

Dev looked offended.

“I know all of them.”

“You don't know the one about the listening mind.”

“I do. It was the cleverest.”

Analā and Ilā looked at one another.

“See?” Ilā whispered.

The children who had come with only what they could carry were leaving with verses, names, recipes, jokes, little drawings, and ways of remembering the island. No one was sending out a grand account of Ravana's reign. The stories travelled because a child might want something familiar to recite when the coast disappeared behind him.

Analā understood that. She had crossed a sea once with almost nothing she could keep.

She watched Ilā tie the bark tablet into Dev's bundle, and felt something unexpected: pride, edged with unease. The story was going places where neither of them could follow to correct it.

“Make sure he remembers the cliff,” she said.

Ilā nodded.

“I will.”

Ilā made Dev repeat the verse once more before she let him go. When he reached the line about the king remaining at the cliff, another child joined in; then a third. Their voices were uneven, but the words had begun to belong to them. Ilā watched with an expression Analā knew from the mornings she managed to teach a smaller child a knot: pleasure at discovering the child could now do something without her.

“Will they sing it in Lanka?” Ilā asked.

“They might.”

Ilā considered that. She did not say how far Lanka was from the other places the returning children might one day go.

On the second evening after speaking to Māran, Analā went to the house across the water.

The path had become familiar enough for her feet to find its turns without help. She knew where a root lifted the earth, where the stones remained slippery after rain, and which wall of the deserted house warmed first in the afternoon. The gecko flickered away from the window as she entered.

She took the letter from beneath the broken weighing scale.

The folded bark had grown brittle at one corner. Her handwriting sloped in the same awkward fashion it had when she wrote it. She read the words carefully: Mēni's name, Ilā's name, the questions about her brothers, the account of a frightening ship. At the bottom were the words she had once been unable to erase.

find me

For a moment she could see herself on the floor of this room months earlier, angry and ashamed that she still wanted the man who had left her world to return.

She was not that girl now.

The captain had brought her no proof that her father had been right. The new ship's courtesy could not make the old soldiers' cruelty unreal. The fact that Taranisena lived did not answer the questions she needed to ask. Nothing had become simple.

But the question had changed.

She no longer wanted to know whether her father would find her.

She wanted to hear what he would say when she asked him what he had done.

She folded the letter again and slipped it into her small bundle.

She did not strike out the last two words.

She did not add anything either.

From the deserted house she could see the naval ship riding at anchor. Its sail was furled. A little farther out, sunlight broke across a wave and made it look harmless until it reached the reef and burst against the rocks. Analā had watched the island's weather long enough not to trust the bright sky. She wondered whether the ship would leave in the morning. She found she wanted the answer to be yes, and then was ashamed of how quickly she wished it might be no.

When Analā told Mēni the captain hoped to leave as soon as the sea permitted, Mēni was washing rice.

The water had turned cloudy around her hands. She looked up once and nodded, as if Analā had told her she was going to the lower well.

“Have you eaten?” Mēni asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then eat first.”

Analā sat.

Mēni poured the rice into the pot, dried her hands on her skirt, and moved the cooking stones closer together.

“Are you going to stay there?” she asked after a while.

“I don't know.”

“Good.”

Analā looked up.

“Good?”

“You haven't gone yet. It would be foolish to know what you will do after.”

Mēni turned the pot on its stones.

“I'll send food for the crossing. Not because the sailors cannot cook. Because they'll cook what they like.”

Analā smiled.

Mēni worked another moment, then said, without looking at her, “This house has been yours.”

Analā felt the words move through her slowly, like warmth entering cold hands.

“I know.”

“If you come back, it will still be yours.”

Analā was grateful that Mēni had said if.

“I know that too.”

Mēni reached to adjust the fire. Her hand trembled once. She held it near the warmth until it steadied.

Later, when the baby slept, she took a small length of blue cloth from the storage chest. She had used part of it to mend a shirt during the rains. The remaining piece was barely large enough for a head covering.

“It is not very useful,” she said.

“It is.”

“It won't keep out much rain.”

“I don't need it to.”

Mēni folded it twice and put it into Analā's hand.

She did not explain what the cloth meant. She went back to the cooking pot and told the girls to stop getting under her feet.

Ilā talked from the moment she heard that Analā would leave.

She spoke about the ship, the food, the clothes, the way the harbour men tied knots. She complained that men who had access to whole oceans could not learn to dry their own shirts properly. She asked whether palaces still had floors so smooth that people slipped on them. Analā answered when she could and smiled when she could not.

They packed in the room where they had slept for fourteen months.

There was very little to pack.

Analā's spare tunic. The blue cloth from Mēni. A small wooden boat the girls had once repaired badly enough that it sank on its first trial, and repaired again until it floated crookedly. The bark letter. A comb with two teeth missing.

Ilā folded the tunic, unfolded it, and folded it again.

“The sailors will think you don't know how to care for your clothes,” she said.

“I don't care what they think.”

“They'll tell the king his daughter is untidy.”

“He'll survive.”

Ilā laughed too quickly.

She tried to tuck the wooden boat beside the comb. It would not fit. She took everything out and arranged it in a different order. The boat still would not fit.

“You can leave it,” she said.

“I could.”

“It would be safe here.”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps leave it.”

Analā looked at her.

“Do you want me to?”

Ilā began smoothing a crease that was no longer there.

“It was ours,” she said.

Analā took the little boat and set it between them.

“Then keep it safe for us.”

Ilā nodded. Her eyes remained on the toy. Before supper she slipped it back into Analā's bundle, where it made the comb fall out. Analā found it there, said nothing, and put it beside Ilā's sleeping mat. In the morning Ilā carried it to the water jar and inspected the old repair as though she might discover a new crack.

“Are you keeping it?” Analā asked.

“I thought you were.”

“Then why is it in your hands?”

“I was checking whether it still floats.”

“It hasn't been in the water.”

“That is why I needed to check.”

They went down to the basin behind the house and watched it lean to one side without sinking. When they brought it back, neither put it away. There was no reason to decide yet; the captain had sent word that the reef was still unsafe.

Outside, Mēni called them to eat. Ilā stood automatically, fetched two bowls and placed them side by side. She had done it every morning for more than a year. She stared at the bowls for a moment before lifting them.

“What's wrong?” Analā asked.

“Nothing.”

Ilā set one bowl down again.

“When you go, I'll forget and get yours.”

“You can eat twice.”

“Mother won't let me.”

“I'll write to her and ask.”

“You never write anything except lists.”

“Then I'll put it on a list.”

Ilā gave a small laugh. It broke halfway through.

Analā reached for her hand, but Ilā had already picked up the bowls and gone outside.

At supper she told a story so badly that Dev corrected her three times. She protested that he should show more respect to the person who had taught him. He said she was forgetting the ten minds because Analā would not be there to remind her. Ilā made a face at him, and everyone laughed.

Analā did not.

She looked at Ilā, who was smiling at the children with her eyes too bright.

That evening a sailor came up from the harbour to say the captain would inspect the passage again at first light. He did not promise departure. Ilā listened to the message as if it were a verdict, then went back to helping Mēni clear the bowls.

It was only after the others had gone to sleep that Ilā asked the question.

“Will you come back?”

They lay side by side on their mats. The house was quiet except for Mēni's son breathing through a blocked nose and the soft scrape of a branch against the wall.

Analā turned towards her.

She could have said yes. In the darkness the word would have been easy. Ilā would have believed it, or at least could have pretended to. They might both have slept.

“I don't know,” Analā said.

Ilā was silent.

“Neither do I,” she said finally.

Analā waited, thinking there must be more.

Ilā drew her knees close.

“It's a horrible answer.”

“I know.”

“It's the only one you'll give me.”

“Yes.”

Ilā pulled the edge of the blanket over her shoulder.

After a time she said, “If you stay there, who will tell me when I'm getting the stories wrong?”

Analā considered this.

“You'll have to notice.”

“That sounds very difficult.”

“It is.”

A long quiet followed.

Then Ilā said, “I'll tell them the one about the cliff properly.”

Analā could not answer immediately.

She reached across the narrow space between the mats. Ilā found her hand in the dark and held it beneath the blanket. They stayed like that until the sounds of the house grew still.

Neither girl slept much.

The next morning the pilot returned from the reef with wet sleeves and, at last, a different answer. The wind had eased, the swell was falling, and a ship leaving on the next suitable tide could pass without asking the children aboard to gamble their lives on it. Māran ordered the departure. Nobody celebrated the clear passage quite as loudly as the sailors had expected.

On the morning of departure, the island was crowded with people who had discovered practical tasks that needed doing at once.

A fisherman had brought extra water jars. The harbour carpenter insisted on checking a plank in the loading boat even though the naval sailors had already checked it. Mēni had wrapped food into small bundles and kept adding things until Analā protested that she would need another ship to carry it all.

Sēnan stood beside the registrar, checking names as the children boarded. Each name was spoken and answered. The new list was compared with the old one. No one was declared missing because a man had neglected to look behind a house.

Dev boarded with his bark tablet tied safely in his bundle. A little girl kissed the cheek of the woman who had cared for her and then began crying because she had not kissed the other cheek. The woman climbed the gangplank to correct the injustice. A sailor held her elbow while she leaned over the child's shoulder.

There were thirty-one on the departure list, Analā among them. Other children stayed with their island households, watching the ship with expressions that changed between relief and regret. Some would leave on later voyages. Some might never go. For the first time since the evacuation, their futures were questions being asked rather than decisions already made for them.

Māran stood by the gangplank and waited.

Analā had said goodbye to Mēni in the courtyard.

Mēni pulled her close suddenly, with the strength of someone accustomed to carrying water and lifting heavy pots. The embrace lasted just long enough for Analā to feel the familiar smell of smoke and rice flour in her clothes.

“Eat,” Mēni said into her hair.

“I will.”

“And don't let important people speak to you while you're hungry.”

“They may have to wait a long time.”

“Good.”

Mēni drew back and studied her face. Then she touched the blue cloth folded beneath Analā's arm.

“Keep it dry.”

Analā nodded.

Mēni turned away and lifted her son before Analā could see her cry.

Sēnan shook Analā's hand as if she were an adult who had come to discuss harbour accounts. She almost laughed.

“You made the lists better,” he said.

“You made them first.”

“Someone had to.”

She held his hand a moment longer.

“You kept us here,” she said.

“No,” Sēnan answered. “You all did that. We made room.”

He looked towards the children waiting beside the ship.

“Go and see what you need to see.”

Analā went to Ilā.

They had been side by side since sunrise. Ilā had brought the food bundles, criticised the knots, argued with a sailor about where the clothes should be kept, and complained that the ship was leaving on a day when the wind might change. Now they stood beneath the gangplank with nothing left to arrange.

Ilā had the little wooden boat in her hands.

“You forgot this,” she said.

“I left it with you.”

“I know.”

She turned it over. There was a pale seam where the girls had bound a crack with thread. It floated now, if one did not demand that it travel in a straight line.

“What if it sinks again?” Ilā asked.

“Fix it.”

“What if I can't?”

“Ask someone who can.”

Ilā smiled. It was the answer Analā had once given the children about their uncle's cleverest mind.

The boarding call came from the ship.

Neither moved.

Ilā looked up at Analā. She had been smaller when the boats arrived, with a scar beside her left eyebrow and a habit of deciding immediately whether a person was interesting. Now she had grown, though she still leaned forward a little when she wanted an answer quickly.

“Tell me one thing,” she said.

“What?”

“When you tell the king about this place, will you tell him we were good to you?”

Analā felt her throat close.

“I'll tell him you were terrible,” she said.

Ilā nodded solemnly.

“That's fair.”

“And that Mēni burned the rice twice.”

“Three times.”

“And that you steal my bread.”

“Only the good part.”

Analā tried to smile. Her mouth trembled.

“I'll tell him I was happy,” she said.

Ilā's eyes filled. She looked away towards the water as though the approaching tide required her attention.

“Then he'll know,” she said.

“Know what?”

“That you weren't alone.”

Analā pulled her close.

For a moment the harbour disappeared. There was only Ilā, the coarse cloth of her tunic, the thin shoulder beneath Analā's hand, the little wooden boat caught awkwardly between them. Analā had arrived on the island believing that every person who loved her had been left on the other side of the water. She had been wrong. She had learned that wrongness slowly, through a lamp left burning, a second bowl set out without being asked, a girl carrying rice up a hill because her friend had forgotten to eat.

Ilā held on fiercely.

Then she let go first.

“Go,” she said.

Analā looked at her. She wanted to promise something. She wanted to say that the road back was short, that the sea was small, that all the difficult questions awaiting her in Lanka would have answers she could carry home. She knew none of those things.

“I don't know,” she whispered.

“I know,” Ilā said.

It was not an answer to a question. It was permission not to lie.

Analā stepped onto the gangplank.

She did not look back until she reached the deck. Ilā was standing beside Mēni and Sēnan, holding the crooked little boat against her chest. All around them children and adults raised their hands. Some called names. Others waved without speaking.

The ship moved.

Analā lifted her hand.

Ilā lifted hers at once.

She kept waving as the harbour slipped behind the stern, growing smaller with each turn of the water. Analā watched until she could no longer tell the little boat from the folds of Ilā's clothes. Even then, the raised hand remained visible for a few more moments.

Once, on another boat, Analā had waved to her uncle standing on a cliff above a burning city. She had never known whether he saw her.

This time she knew.

It did not make leaving easier.

The wind held through the afternoon.

By evening the island had become a dark shape below the horizon and the ship was running north with a steady sea beneath it. Dev slept curled against his bundle. Two younger children whispered the verse about the king who stayed, stumbling over the lines Ilā had insisted they learn.

Analā sat near the stern with her blue cloth folded across her knees.

Māran came to ask whether she needed water. She thanked him. He looked towards the children and said they would make the harbour before the night was far advanced if the wind stayed kind.

“Will someone tell the king?” she asked.

“When we reach the harbour,” Māran said. “The sea would not let a messenger leave before us.”

“Then he doesn't know?”

“Not yet.”

Analā looked at the sea.

“He will be happy,” Māran said.

She did not answer.

The captain stood with her a moment, then left her alone.

Beyond the dark water waited a city of repaired wells and rebuilt markets, a brother who was alive, the unanswered fate of another, and a man whose name she had spent a year teaching herself not to need.

She was not returning because she had failed to make a life somewhere else.

She was going because there were questions she could no longer leave to the songs.

A lantern appeared ahead, then another. The entrance to Lanka's harbour came slowly out of the dark. Analā watched the lights without rising.

When the ship passed the outer marker, she put the folded blue cloth inside her bundle, rested her hands on her knees, and waited for the shore to come to her.
