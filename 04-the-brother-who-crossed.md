# Chapter Four

## The Brother Who Crossed

The eastern alarm stopped halfway through its third stroke.

The silence that followed was worse than the bell.

The king had just passed beyond the bronze screen of the women’s court. Shuka’s chair was being carried behind him. The first light of morning lay thinly across the passage, too weak to reach the floor. For a moment everyone waited for the iron plate to sound again.

Instead, an enemy horn answered from inside the wall.

It gave one long note. Nearer than it had any right to be.

The captain of the women’s guard turned towards the eastern arch. A servant carrying wet blankets stopped so abruptly that the woman behind her walked into her back. Somewhere in the garden, Mala continued shouting instructions about the rescued jasmine because she had not yet understood what the silence meant.

The king understood.

“Put Shuka down,” he said.

The guards lowered the carrying chair.

“Close the inner screen. No one leaves the women’s court by the eastern passage. Send two runners to the armoury and one to the river gate. I want to know which signal tower failed.”

The captain was already moving when a figure appeared beneath the eastern arch.

At first Shuka thought the man was carrying a red cloth against his stomach.

Then the cloth slipped.

The guard caught it with both hands and pressed it back into the wound.

He was very young. Salt water ran from his clothes and left a dark trail across the pale stones. The left side of his face was covered in fine black grit. One sandal was missing, and the sole of his bare foot had been sliced open from heel to toe. Something had struck the bronze plate on his chest hard enough to fold its lower edge inward.

He reached the king and attempted to kneel.

His legs failed before ceremony did.

The king caught him beneath the arms. Blood passed immediately through the king’s sleeves.

“Stay upright,” the king said.

The guard’s mouth opened twice before sound came.

“They are inside the eastern wall.”

No one in the passage moved.

The king lowered him carefully onto the floor.

“How many?”

“Forty. Perhaps sixty. More behind.”

“Through which gate?”

The guard shook his head. “No gate. Beneath the salt sluice.”

The captain knelt beside him and pressed both hands over the wound. Blood rose between her fingers.

“The salt road is underwater,” she said.

“Not at the third ebb,” the young man whispered. “There are stones. White stones beneath the surface. They knew where to step.”

The king’s face changed.

The guard reached beneath his armour. His hand returned holding a narrow wax tablet wrapped in oiled cloth.

“From their officer.”

The king took it.

A map had been cut into the dark wax with a fine stylus. It showed the eastern tidal channel, the two sluice houses and a maintenance path that no public map recorded. Small circles marked submerged stones beneath the water. A note beside the lower gate identified the hinge most weakened by rust. Another gave the precise interval between the second and third ebb.

At the bottom, in a different hand, someone had written:

*Avoid the workers’ quarter. Take the sluice house. Hold until the main force arrives.*

Shuka leaned forward in the chair.

“Could they have copied this from the harbour archive?”

The king looked at the map for several breaths.

“There were three surveys,” he said. “The harbour archive held one. It shows the channel and the sluices, but not the submerged stones.”

The captain’s hands remained pressed into the young guard’s abdomen.

“Who had the others?” she asked.

“One is sealed beneath the council chamber. The third belonged to the administrator who redesigned the eastern floodworks.”

Shuka knew the answer before the king spoke it.

“Vibhishana,” said the king.

It was the first time that morning he had used his brother’s name.

The wounded guard made a small sound. His body had begun to shake.

“Could someone have taken your brother’s copy?” Shuka asked.

“The copy would not be enough.”

The king touched one of the circles marking the stones.

“I added these after the eastern storm. I drew them on his map while he held the lamp. No scribe was present. No engineer saw the final page.”

Shuka looked at the wet footprints leading back towards the arch.

“Then there is no other possibility.”

“No,” the king said. “There is not.”

Another horn sounded beyond the eastern buildings. This one was answered by shouting, then by the hard uneven clash of metal in a narrow space.

The captain looked up.

“If they hold the lower sluice, they can drain the channel.”

“Not fully,” said the king. “The upper gate remains closed.”

“And if they take it?”

“Then three hundred men can walk beneath the eastern wall before the tide returns.”

The young guard’s fingers caught the king’s wrist.

“They killed everyone in the lower house.”

“You came from there?”

He nodded.

“How?”

“Under the gate chain. There was water.”

His eyes had begun to lose focus.

The king placed the wax tablet in Shuka’s lap and pressed his palm over the guard captain’s hands.

“Look at me,” he told the wounded man.

The guard tried.

“What is your name?”

“Ayan.”

“Ayan, the physician is coming. Until she arrives, you will continue being difficult.”

The guard’s mouth moved.

“Majesty—”

“That sounded like surrender. I forbid it.”

The physician ran through the bronze screen carrying a leather case. Trijata followed with linen, having apparently decided that restrictions on leaving the women’s court did not apply to women carrying bandages.

The king moved aside only when the physician touched his shoulder.

“Armour,” he called.

The old chamberlain appeared at the far end of the passage with two servants and a dark breastplate between them. He had also brought the green parrot. Nobody had asked him to bring the green parrot.

“The eastern armoury is being emptied,” he said. “This was in the ceremonial room.”

The king stared at the polished metal.

“Why is the shoulder clasp missing?”

The chamberlain examined it. “It was removed for cleaning.”

“Send someone to explain that to the enemy.”

One servant began fastening the lower straps while the other searched the wrapping cloth for the missing clasp.

Shuka watched blood dry across the king’s forearms.

“You cannot go into the channel,” he said.

“I have no intention of standing in it longer than necessary.”

“Send the eastern commander.”

“The eastern commander is either fighting at the sluice or beneath it.”

“Then send another officer.”

The king looked at him.

“They heard an enemy horn inside their wall. They need to see someone who has not yet become frightened.”

“Have you?”

The question escaped before Shuka could decide whether a spy was permitted to ask it.

The king pulled the breastplate into place.

“Yes.”

The honesty frightened Shuka more than denial would have done.

The chamberlain attempted to secure the right shoulder with a length of blue cord. The parrot watched from its cage and offered an opinion no one requested.

“Your aunt taught it that word,” the chamberlain said.

“My aunt blamed the bird.”

“She was royal. It was available.”

The cord held.

The king took the short sword returned to him at the women’s screen and exchanged it for the heavier blade carried by the captain. He tested the weight once.

Beyond the palace roofs, black smoke moved across the paling sky. The enemy horn sounded again.

At the king’s coronation, an old court seer had spoken one sentence no one included in the official record.

*You will not fall to an enemy who breaks your walls. You will fall when your own blood shows the beasts a road.*

The king had laughed then. The seer had been nearly blind and smelled strongly of fermented milk. The walls were unbroken. The sea could not be crossed by an army. His brothers had stood beside him in ceremonial white, one smiling, the other correcting the priest’s count of lamps.

For years the sentence had survived only as a joke told after too much wine.

Now men carrying monkey standards were walking beneath his eastern wall along stones only his brother could have shown them.

The king looked at the wax tablet in Shuka’s lap.

“My own blood has shown the beasts a road,” he said.

No one laughed.

A second group of guards arrived. Two had not finished fastening their helmets. One still carried a piece of breakfast bread in his hand.

The king pointed towards the eastern arch.

“The lower sluice is lost. We hold the upper house or we lose the road to the palace. Send archers to the potters’ roofs. No fire arrows. There are families beneath them. Open the overflow gate before we enter the channel.”

The guard captain rose from beside Ayan. “Opening it will flood our own men.”

“Warn them first.”

“How long?”

“Forty breaths.”

“That is not enough.”

“It is what the enemy has given us.”

He turned to Shuka.

“Remain here.”

Shuka looked down at his injured ankle. “Again?”

“You are becoming very experienced.”

“If you do not return—”

The king tightened the blue cord across his shoulder.

For one instant the unfinished sentence stood between them.

“No,” he said. “That sentence has ruined enough men. If I do not return, improvise.”

He left at a run.

The guards followed. The chamberlain followed the guards. The parrot, carried unwillingly among them, continued shouting until distance turned the word into something that might have been prayer.

Shuka remained in the passage with the wax tablet across his knees.

Behind him, the physician cut Ayan’s armour away. The young guard cried out once and then bit the linen Trijata placed between his teeth. The women’s court smelled of blood, salt, wet flowers and the sesame oil still warming in the garden.

The eastern alarm did not begin again.

***

They carried Shuka back to the western room because it had become the palace infirmary, council chamber and place where people left objects they did not know how to save.

The wounded filled the space behind the screens. The split pomegranate remained on the black table. Someone had removed the silver bowl but left three seeds pressed into the wood. The king’s breakfast chair still stood against the wall with its cracked leg.

Shuka ordered the guards to place his chair near the eastern window.

“The king said remain here,” one reminded him.

“This is here.”

“He meant the women’s court.”

“Then he should have chosen a less mobile chair.”

The guards looked at each other and left him beside the window.

From there he could see only roofs. Smoke concealed the lower eastern district. At intervals, men appeared along the covered walks carrying arrows, water or wounded companions. Each time a runner crossed the court below, Shuka searched the man’s face for news.

None looked up.

He placed the wax tablet on the table.

The copied lines were efficient. Whoever had made them understood that men entering cold water in darkness did not need history. They needed depth, distance, rust and time.

Beside the third submerged stone, the enemy scribe had cut a small mark indicating a sudden drop.

Shuka had seen that mark before.

Not upon a map.

Upon an old administrative report held in Vibhishana’s hand while he spoke of a road discovered when both brothers were young.

Shuka closed his eyes.

He had told the king that Vibhishana gave his name to the forest guards.

That was true.

He had told the king that the guards tied him before Vibhishana came closer.

That was also true.

He had not told him what happened after the northern prince ordered the ropes removed.

Truth did not always require a lie in order to be betrayed. Sometimes it needed only a careful stopping place.

***

Six nights earlier, Shuka had left the northern prince’s command tent with rope burns around his neck and both hands free.

The forest soldiers had been instructed to show him anything he had failed to inspect before sending him back across the causeway. Several appeared delighted by the order. One offered to show him the latrine pits. Another said this would be unfair because the pits were their strongest defence.

Shuka had been escorted past the grain stores, the medical tents and the racks where wet bowstrings hung beneath oiled cloth. He counted everything automatically. A spy continued working until blindness or death made the habit impractical.

At the edge of the camp, where torchlight thinned beside the mangroves, Vibhishana was waiting.

He wore no jewels. Mud darkened the hem of his clothes. His hair had been tied without a mirror and badly enough to prove it. The fresh cut across one knuckle had opened again.

The two forest guards stopped.

“He leaves from here,” Vibhishana told them.

“The prince said he may inspect anything.”

“He has inspected enough.”

One guard looked disappointed. “He has not seen the latrines.”

“Then some mysteries will survive the war.”

The guards withdrew.

Shuka and Vibhishana stood alone beside the dark water.

The causeway fires stretched behind them. Men moved over the new road carrying timber and stone. Far across the strait, the island lights climbed from harbour to palace, steady enough to pretend there was no army between them.

“You recognised me quickly,” Shuka said.

“You watched the officers rather than the food.”

“That is what I told your brother.”

“He will enjoy it.”

“He did.”

Vibhishana looked towards the island.

“When you return, do not carry this conversation as a message.”

“Then why have it with a messenger?”

“You are a spy.”

“A moment ago you argued I was protected as a messenger.”

“The law required one word. Honesty permits the other.”

Shuka rubbed the cord mark around his neck.

“What do you want?”

“For one person going home to know that I did not leave it easily.”

“You expect confidence from a man you exposed?”

“I expect judgment. Confidence would be more comfortable.”

Shuka studied him.

“How long have you hated your brother?”

Vibhishana’s eyes returned from the distant city.

“That is not why I crossed.”

“Then tell me why.”

“You will not believe me.”

“Belief is not required for memory.”

Vibhishana almost smiled.

Shuka added, “Begin wherever the lie becomes most convincing.”

The smile disappeared.

For a while there was only the sound of water moving beneath unfinished timber.

“The fire was not the beginning,” Vibhishana said at last. “Neither was the woman.”

“Where was it?”

“In a music room. We were children.”

The king’s brother had spent his life crossing rooms to stand on the other side of an argument. The boat was merely the first time anyone called it treason.

Before arguments, there had been music.

***

The old music room occupied a round tower above the sea. Its windows had no shutters because their mother believed salt air improved wooden instruments. It did not. Every third month a servant scraped white crust from the bronze fittings, and every winter two strings snapped during the night.

The elder boy loved the room.

He was thirteen and already too large for the low stool upon which their teacher made him sit. He played the veena with his eyes half closed, not to appear spiritual, as later painters would suggest, but because watching his own fingers distracted him. His left hand moved with unusual strength and precision. His right found rhythms before their teacher named them.

Vibhishana was nine.

He sat on the floor beside the oil lamp and turned the palm-leaf pages when instructed. He did not yet play well. His fingers hesitated. He understood structure more easily than sound and could explain why a phrase failed long before he could perform one that succeeded.

One evening the elder boy became trapped in an ending.

The composition began with a low phrase that climbed slowly, opened into something bright and returned upon itself. Every ending sounded either too final or insufficiently finished. He played six versions. Their teacher rejected five and fell asleep during the sixth.

Vibhishana had been listening to the rhythm beneath the melody.

“Begin the last phrase one beat earlier,” he said.

His brother opened his eyes.

“Why?”

“Because the answer is waiting too long.”

“Music can wait.”

“This part cannot.”

The elder boy tried it.

The phrase landed cleanly. The delay disappeared. The entire composition seemed to have been moving towards that ending from its first note.

His brother played it again.

Then a third time.

“That is better,” he said.

Vibhishana waited for more.

None came.

The following night their father held a gathering in the lower hall. Traders, officers, teachers and three neighbouring rulers sat beneath garlands of white flowers. The elder boy performed the composition before them.

The ending drew silence first, then applause.

Their father stood and placed a fresh garland over his eldest son’s shoulders.

“You hear what other men cannot,” he said.

Vibhishana sat behind the musicians holding a spare lamp.

His brother looked towards him once.

He did not mention the ending.

Later, when the guests had gone, the elder boy found him returning instruments to their cases. He removed the garland and placed it around Vibhishana’s neck.

“Why are you giving me this?” Vibhishana asked.

“You liked the ending.”

“I wrote the ending.”

His brother laughed.

He believed it was a joke.

That laughter remained longer than the garland.

It would have been easier if the elder boy had been deliberately cruel. Cruelty could be named and opposed. Instead he was generous with what he believed belonged entirely to him.

Anything placed near his brilliance eventually became part of its light.

Years later, Vibhishana would tell himself that this was the first wound.

Memory was rarely so orderly. It arranged old pain into beginnings only after the ending required one.

The elder brother also protected him.

Three years after the music-room performance, a group of boys from the military school discovered that Vibhishana preferred the records office to the practice yard. They called him Little Ledger. At first the name contained no violence. Then one afternoon they took his writing board, threw it into a drainage ditch and held him down when he tried to retrieve it.

The eldest was two years older than Vibhishana’s brother and broader across the chest.

This mattered for approximately half a minute.

The elder brother arrived carrying a practice spear. He did not ask what had begun the fight. He struck the largest boy behind the knees, broke another’s nose with the spear shaft and chased the third through the herb garden until both fell into a bed of medicinal nettles.

Their father was furious.

“You broke a minister’s son’s nose.”

“It was badly positioned,” the elder boy replied.

“Why did you attack three boys?”

“They struck my brother.”

He said it as though the sentence answered every question a reasonable universe could produce.

That night he cleaned the mud from Vibhishana’s writing board and pressed a cold cloth against the bruise beneath his eye.

“You do not need to become like me,” he said.

“I could fight.”

“You could. But one of me is already exhausting.”

Vibhishana laughed despite the bruise.

For years afterward, whenever resentment rose, this memory rose beside it: his brother kneeling upon the floor, repairing a cracked writing board with thread and glue, angry at the world for having touched what he loved.

Love did not prevent injury. It made injury difficult to classify.

***

The salt road entered their lives when Vibhishana was seventeen.

A cyclone had struck the eastern coast during the night. The sea crossed the lower embankment, flooded the dyers’ quarter and left three fishing boats inside a public kitchen. By morning the eastern sluices were blocked with timber, weed and part of a roof belonging to a tax collector.

The two brothers joined the inspection party.

The elder had already begun attending council. He rode at the front with officers and engineers. Vibhishana followed carrying survey tablets because everyone had agreed he possessed the safest hands.

At low tide he noticed a row of pale shapes beneath the brown water.

The engineers called them broken foundation stones. Vibhishana waited until the tide withdrew farther, tied a rope around his waist and entered the channel.

The stones continued beneath the eastern wall.

They were not a foundation. They formed an ancient maintenance path built when the first sluice house was constructed. Most records of it had been lost in an archive fire. From the outer mudflat, a repair crew could reach both gates without crossing the wall above.

From the outer mudflat, so could soldiers.

Vibhishana measured every stone.

He marked the sudden drop after the third, the place where weed concealed a broken edge and the exact interval during which the path remained passable at the lowest ebb. He proposed replacing two stones, marking the safe route beneath the surface and locking the outer chain from inside the upper house.

His report filled twelve tablets.

At the council presentation, he had reached the second when his brother interrupted.

“The path saves the eastern quarter during floods,” the elder said. “Repair crews can reach the gate without opening the city wall.”

The engineers began asking questions.

The elder brother answered them beautifully.

He had read the report once. He remembered the measurements, simplified the explanation and turned Vibhishana’s cautious proposal into a plan men wanted to fund. By the end of the meeting, the council was congratulating the future king for discovering how an old road could protect the city.

Vibhishana sat beside him holding the remaining ten tablets.

Outside the chamber, his brother struck him affectionately upon the shoulder.

“You found something useful.”

“They think you found it.”

“They approved the work.”

“That was not what I said.”

“Do you want the road repaired or your name painted upon every stone?”

Vibhishana had no answer that did not sound small beside houses saved from water.

The road was repaired.

During the next great storm, it allowed workers to clear the sluices before the lower district flooded. Families cheered the young prince who had ordered the improvements. Vibhishana stood beside him and accepted the portion of gratitude assigned to useful men who were not loved.

Years later, as chief administrator, Vibhishana commissioned three final surveys of the salt road.

One went to the harbour archive. One was sealed beneath the council chamber. The third remained in his private office.

The king added the hidden stones to that third copy himself while Vibhishana held the lamp.

*You will not fall to an enemy who breaks your walls. You will fall when your own blood shows the beasts a road.*

The prophecy had not yet been spoken.

The road was already waiting.

***

In the western room, a runner entered searching for the king.

His left sleeve had been burned away. He carried a broken helmet and shouted before the guards could stop him.

“The upper sluice house is surrounded.”

Shuka opened his eyes.

The music room vanished. The enemy camp vanished. Smoke filled the eastern window.

“Where is the king?” the runner demanded.

“At the channel.”

The man stared at Shuka. “He ordered the overflow opened.”

“Then open it.”

“Our men are still below.”

Shuka looked at the wax tablet. Forty breaths, the king had said.

“Has the warning horn sounded?”

“Twice.”

“Sound it a third time. Then obey him.”

The runner’s face tightened. “You are not in command.”

“No. The man who gave the order is standing where the water will go.”

That was enough.

The runner left.

Shuka listened for the warning horn.

One blast came, short and urgent.

Then another.

The third took longer to begin.

When it ended, a deep wooden crack travelled beneath the city, followed by the roar of released water.

The wounded men behind the screens fell silent.

No one knew who had escaped the channel.

Shuka returned to Vibhishana’s voice because the alternative was imagining the king beneath the flood.

***

“You think he stole your music and your road,” Shuka had said beside the northern mangroves.

Vibhishana looked almost offended. “I told you he used them.”

“You tell the difference with unusual care.”

“Because it is the difference between malice and nature.”

“Whose nature?”

“His. Perhaps mine.”

The causeway work continued behind them. Someone dropped a timber beam. Men cursed. A supervisor insisted the beam was unharmed, which no one had asked.

“He made you chief administrator,” Shuka said. “He placed you at his right hand.”

“He trusted me with every task for which nobody gives a garland.”

“That sounds like trust.”

“It was.”

“And affection?”

Vibhishana watched the island lights.

“He believed responsibility was affection.”

“Perhaps it was.”

“Then he loved me until I could no longer stand beneath the weight.”

Shuka heard the polish in the sentence. Vibhishana had repeated it before, if only to himself.

“You have practised this explanation.”

“I have had six days.”

“And before that?”

“A lifetime.”

The fire caused by the forest commander was not the beginning. It was the moment the private argument became public.

The morning after the southern market burned, Vibhishana walked through the damage carrying a list of the dead.

Seventeen names belonged to soldiers. Nine belonged to merchants, servants and people who had entered the street to help. One was a child whose family lived above the sandal shop. Two bodies could not yet be named. The youngest captain of the royal household appeared twice because the military clerk and palace clerk had each believed the other might forget him.

Vibhishana crossed out the second entry.

The act felt obscene. A boy had died, and the first useful thing anyone did was prevent him from becoming two lines in an account.

The king was in the burned guard court questioning the officer who ordered the prisoner’s rope set alight.

“I gave that order,” the king said.

The officer lowered his head. “Majesty, I chose the oil.”

“Then we have divided responsibility with impressive precision.”

Vibhishana waited until the officer left.

“You spared the envoy,” he said.

“At your insistence.”

“You obeyed the law.”

“And then found a lawful way to behave foolishly.”

The admission surprised him.

The king looked towards the ruined market.

“Do not appear so pleased. I have admitted an error, not crowned you wisdom itself.”

Vibhishana’s pleasure vanished because it had been seen.

That was another of his brother’s talents. He noticed weakness and turned it into humour before deciding whether to be kind.

“Return the woman,” Vibhishana said.

The king closed his eyes.

“There it is.”

“The fire proves the war has already entered the city.”

“The fire proves your envoy should have been searched more carefully.”

“He came because of her.”

“He came because the northern prince sent a spy.”

“Both are true.”

“You have become very fond of two truths whenever one inconveniences you.”

Vibhishana held out the list of dead.

“There will be more names.”

The king did not take it.

“I know.”

“Then release her.”

“And what do I tell my sister?”

“That mutilating her was wrong. That abducting another woman was also wrong. That one offence does not become lawful because it follows another.”

“You want me to send the northern princess back with gifts and an apology.”

“I want a formal settlement. Return her unharmed. Demand judgment concerning our sister. Demand compensation for the frontier dead. Invite neutral rulers to hear both claims.”

The king looked at him.

“A hearing.”

“Yes.”

“The northern prince builds a road across my sea, burns my market and gathers an army beneath my walls. You propose sending him an invitation to discuss procedure.”

“I propose giving him no reason to enter.”

“He has reasons. Men who bring armies always do.”

“Then remove the one reason other kingdoms will honour.”

The king took the list at last.

“You have prepared this argument carefully.”

“I have repeated it carefully. Preparation was no longer required after the tenth time.”

The king read the names.

“You want me to say you were right.”

“I want you to prevent a war.”

“That was not what I said.”

The phrase found Vibhishana like a thrown stone. It was a question he had used upon ministers, servants and occasionally his wife. Hearing it from his brother made him feel imitated and exposed.

“Would she be less free because I was pleased to be right?” he asked.

“No.”

“Would the city be less safe?”

“No.”

“Then do not confuse an imperfect motive with an incorrect decision.”

The king folded the list.

“And do not confuse correctness with purity.”

Neither spoke for a while.

Smoke moved through the court. A merchant searched the wet ashes of his shop with a garden rake. Every few strokes he stopped and looked at the rake as though disappointed by the object it had become.

The king placed the list inside his tunic.

“Bring your proposal to council tonight,” he said.

Vibhishana should have felt hope.

Instead he felt anticipation.

He disliked himself for knowing the difference.

***

The council met after sunset.

The room had been built for twenty people and contained thirty-four. Military commanders stood behind administrators. Harbour officers shared stools with tax officials. The king’s sister sat near the bronze doors with the scar across her face uncovered.

Vibhishana presented the settlement plainly.

Return the captive woman. Demand a tribunal. Preserve the island’s claims without preserving the act that had weakened them. Send the proposal before the enemy finished reinforcing the causeway.

Several ministers agreed with their eyes and opposed him with their mouths.

One praised the wisdom of negotiation before recommending continued resistance. Another said the proposal should be studied by a smaller committee, which was the administrative form of burial without witnesses.

The king listened.

When Vibhishana finished, his brother asked the military commander how long the northern wall could withstand the wooden towers.

“Two days,” the commander said.

“One,” Vibhishana corrected.

The commander’s face reddened. “My engineers—”

“Your engineers assume dry bowstrings and no fire behind the wall.”

“Then two days if the weather holds.”

“Weather has not signed your plan.”

The king lifted one hand.

“Enough.”

He turned to Vibhishana.

“If we return her and the army does not withdraw?”

“Then we fight with our law intact.”

“Laws do not stop arrows.”

“Neither does pride.”

The word changed the room.

The king’s sister looked at Vibhishana.

“Is that what you call my face?” she asked.

“No.”

“You ask him to return the only answer they understood.”

“She is not an answer. She is a person.”

“And I am what?”

“My sister.”

“That is not an answer either.”

Vibhishana looked at her scars.

“You were wronged.”

“Better.”

“What they did cannot be undone by keeping another woman behind a gate.”

“It can be remembered.”

“By whom? The woman who did not hold the blade?”

The sister’s expression closed.

The king leaned forward.

“A king who returns every insult unanswered becomes a clerk recording the disappearance of his country.”

Vibhishana recognised the shape of an argument his brother had repeated until it sounded inherited.

“And a king who answers every injury with a larger one becomes the reason his country disappears.”

The room went very still.

The king dismissed the council.

He asked Vibhishana to remain.

The bronze doors closed.

For several breaths the brothers listened to thirty-two people depart while pretending not to linger outside. Sandals scraped. Someone dropped a writing tablet. The king waited until the passage settled.

“You embarrassed the eastern commander,” he said.

“His estimate was false.”

“It was hopeful.”

“Hope becomes false when written beside troop numbers.”

The king rose and walked to the side table. A veena rested there beneath a length of blue cloth. One string had been removed for replacement.

“Do you remember the round music room?” he asked.

Vibhishana had expected anger. Memory was more dangerous.

“Yes.”

“The teacher who slept through my ending?”

“He slept through most things.”

“He claimed it improved his listening.”

The king lifted the cloth and touched the remaining strings. A low note moved through the room.

“You changed the final phrase,” he said.

Vibhishana looked at him.

“You remember.”

“Of course I remember.”

“You never said.”

“I gave you the garland.”

“Because I liked the ending.”

The king frowned, searching a memory that had never required examination.

“Was that what I said?”

“Exactly.”

For a moment the king appeared ashamed.

The sight brought Vibhishana less relief than he had imagined across thirty years.

“I was thirteen,” the king said.

“So was the wound.”

“You kept it alive very carefully.”

“No. I kept finding it in new places.”

The king covered the instrument again.

“This is not about music.”

“Nothing is only about where it begins.”

“You have spent your life waiting for me to admit you were right.”

“I have spent my life making your decisions survive contact with consequence.”

“I made you chief administrator because I trusted your mind.”

“You gave me every task for which no one gives a garland.”

“A kingdom is not governed with garlands.”

“No. It is governed by men whose work disappears beneath the king’s name.”

The king turned sharply.

“There. At last.”

“At last what?”

“The honest grievance beneath the moral one.”

Vibhishana felt heat rise into his face.

“The woman remains imprisoned whether or not I envy you.”

“Yes.”

“The army remains outside.”

“Yes.”

“Then my envy changes nothing.”

“It changes the man asking me to trust his judgment.”

Vibhishana stepped closer.

“And your pride changes the man asking the city to trust his.”

The king’s hand moved towards the table, then stopped. He had once been quick to anger and quicker to display it. Age had taught him that power made every movement louder than intended.

“You think the kingdom and I are the same thing,” Vibhishana said.

“No.”

“You think injury to you is injury to it.”

“Sometimes it is.”

“You think love of the city must look like loyalty to your decision.”

“And you think opposing me proves you love it more.”

The sentence landed because it was true often enough.

The brothers stood across the black council table. Between them lay the empty cup used by the military commander, a broken stylus and one pink flower carried in from the women’s court upon someone’s sleeve.

The king picked up the flower.

“Return tomorrow,” he said. “We will discuss the proposal after the eastern reports.”

“There will always be another report.”

“That is government.”

“This is delay.”

“This is a king refusing to dismantle his position because his brother has become impatient.”

“Your position is a woman behind a locked gate.”

The king’s fingers closed around the flower.

“Leave the room.”

Vibhishana did not move.

“Return her.”

“Leave.”

“Say once that you may be wrong.”

The king’s voice dropped.

“If their prince appears so righteous to you, cross the water and serve him.”

Silence followed.

The king looked away first.

He meant: leave the room before we say something that cannot be recalled.

Vibhishana heard: you no longer have a place beside me.

Both meanings were available. Each brother selected the one his pain required.

Vibhishana bowed.

Not deeply.

He left.

The king remained in the council hall holding the crushed pink flower.

Before sunrise, Vibhishana had begun to pack.

***

His wife watched from the bedroom doorway.

He placed two clean tunics, a legal code, three bundles of correspondence and the eastern flood survey into a travelling chest.

“Are you planning to defeat them through filing?” she asked.

“These establish the terms I proposed.”

“Then take another shirt. Your principles will not dry quickly at sea.”

He added a shirt.

She entered the room and lifted the legal code.

“Do they lack laws across the water?”

“They may lack ours.”

“That is generally why people invade.”

He took the code from her and returned it to the chest.

“Come with me.”

“With the children?”

“Yes.”

“And the servants? Their families? Your clerks whose names appear beside yours on every decree?”

“I cannot take everyone.”

“Then you are not taking the city. You are leaving it.”

Vibhishana closed the chest too quickly. The corner of a letter remained outside. He opened it again, folded the letter properly and shut it.

“I am trying to prevent its destruction.”

“By joining the men preparing to destroy it.”

“They will negotiate if someone they trust can guarantee terms.”

“And they will trust the brother of the king they are attacking?”

“They will trust what I can offer.”

His wife’s eyes moved towards the travelling chest.

“What can you offer?”

He did not answer.

She crossed to the window. The palace roofs lay dark beneath the last hour of night. Beyond them, fires marked the causeway.

“If they offer you his chair,” she said, “will you take it?”

“I am going to prevent a war.”

She turned.

“That was not my question.”

The phrase belonged to him. Hearing it from her made him understand why others disliked it.

“Someone must govern if he falls.”

“He has not fallen.”

“He is taking the city with him.”

“And you believe it would be safer in your hands.”

Vibhishana looked down at the closed chest.

“Yes.”

It was the first completely honest word of the night.

His wife sat on the edge of the bed.

“I will not come.”

He had prepared arguments for fear, duty and the children’s safety. He had not prepared for calm refusal.

“He may arrest you.”

“Will he?”

Vibhishana thought of his brother cleaning the mud from a broken writing board.

“No.”

“He may take the house.”

“He will not.”

“He may punish our sons.”

“Never.”

His wife’s face softened with something close to sorrow.

“You trust the brother you are leaving to protect the family you are leaving.”

He sat beside her.

For a while they listened to the sea.

“Come back before the morning watch,” she said.

“I cannot.”

“You can. You may decide not to.”

He reached for her hand. She let him hold it but did not close her fingers around his.

When he left the house, she remained beside the bed.

He carried the travelling chest himself.

Four men waited at the lower quay: an officer from the harbour guard, a legal clerk, an elderly physician who had served the family since before Vibhishana’s birth, and a quiet captain responsible for the southern storehouses. Each had chosen him for a different reason. None asked whether those reasons matched.

The boatman looked at the chest.

“That will make six passengers.”

“It is documents.”

“Documents weigh more when they belong to government.”

They removed one bundle of correspondence and hid it beneath an empty fish basket near the quay.

Vibhishana kept the flood survey.

The boat entered the channel before dawn.

The city receded slowly. First the lower houses merged into one dark slope. Then the harbour towers became pale marks against the sky. The palace remained visible longest because its copper roofs caught the earliest light.

Vibhishana sat facing it.

Inside his pocket was the key to his council office.

He had intended to return it. The palace registry closed at sunset, and the night clerk refused to accept keys without the proper tablet.

“Should I wake him?” Vibhishana had asked.

“Not for a key,” the clerk replied.

“I may not return.”

“Then bring it tomorrow.”

Bureaucracy possessed faith beyond religion.

Halfway across the strait, the boatman stopped rowing.

The tide moved them sideways. Behind lay the island. Ahead, the enemy shore.

“A patrol,” he whispered.

Three low boats passed south of them. The men inside wore monkey emblems painted upon their shields. Nobody called out.

When the boats disappeared into mist, the boatman lifted his oars.

“This is the last place to turn without being seen,” he said.

The harbour officer looked at Vibhishana.

The legal clerk looked down.

The physician appeared to be asleep, though later he denied it.

Vibhishana looked at the city.

He told himself that leaving the king was not leaving the kingdom.

He told himself a short war would save lives.

He told himself the northern prince needed someone who understood the island’s laws.

He told himself that a government must survive the man who temporarily embodied it.

Beneath these arguments lay another, smaller and more durable.

*I would govern better.*

“Continue,” he said.

The boatman rowed.

The city entered the mist.

***

When Vibhishana finished describing the crossing, Shuka interrupted him.

“You took the eastern flood survey.”

Vibhishana’s eyes remained upon the island.

“It was among my administrative records.”

“Why take flood maps to negotiate the return of a woman?”

“Because an army stood beside the water.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I gave myself.”

Shuka waited.

Vibhishana did not say what had happened to the survey.

At the time, Shuka heard a family memory, an administrator’s instinct and perhaps a precaution against flooding.

He did not hear soldiers walking upon hidden stones.

He heard what Vibhishana had taught him to hear.

***

The northern camp surrounded the five men before their boat touched mud.

Forest soldiers pulled them into the shallows, searched their clothing and opened the travelling chest. The legal code received particular suspicion.

“What is this?” one soldier asked.

“Law,” said the clerk.

The soldier turned several pages upside down.

“Is it dangerous?”

“Usually after interpretation.”

They were marched between rows of tents. Men came out to stare. Some shouted that the king’s brother had come to spy. Others suggested hanging him from the unfinished tower where the island could see.

The forest commander with the monkey stitched upon his shoulder arrived before anyone found rope.

He recognised Vibhishana.

“You argued that an envoy could not be killed,” he said.

“You remember.”

“I remember because I was the envoy.”

The commander looked at the four companions, the open chest and the flood survey lying among the documents.

“Are you an envoy now?”

“I seek protection.”

“From your brother?”

“From what he has decided.”

The commander considered him.

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

He ordered the soldiers to lower their weapons.

The decision was not welcomed.

At the command tent, the exiled forest king argued for arrest. His shoulders filled most of the entrance. He distrusted anyone who left a brother during war, perhaps because his own life had been shaped by brothers who did not leave soon enough.

“He comes with maps,” the forest king said. “He comes after seeing our road nearly complete. He expects his brother to lose and wants payment for arriving before the body is cold.”

Vibhishana stood without ornaments before men dressed for war.

“My brother lives.”

“Then return to him.”

“I will return when the city is no longer being governed through one man’s refusal.”

“And if we make you governor?”

“Then I will return sooner.”

The answer caused several men to exchange glances.

Vibhishana heard his wife’s question again.

The northern prince sat upon a folded blanket rather than a throne. His younger brother stood behind him. The commander with the monkey emblem remained near the tent pole, arms crossed.

“Why should I trust you?” the northern prince asked.

“You should not. Not yet.”

“Why should I protect you?”

“Because a man who asks protection without a weapon should not be killed while asking.”

The forest king made a dismissive sound.

The northern prince looked towards him. “If protection belongs only to men we already trust, it is not protection. It is preference.”

He returned his gaze to Vibhishana.

“You and your companions will not be harmed under my authority.”

Relief moved through Vibhishana so quickly that he nearly mistook it for righteousness.

The prince continued.

“That does not make you innocent. It makes you safe while we decide what you are.”

The commander with the monkey emblem spoke.

“He defended the law protecting an enemy messenger while his own guards lay dead. I am alive because he argued against his king’s anger.”

“And now he argues against the king,” said the forest ruler.

“Consistency is not always loyalty,” the commander replied.

The northern prince asked Vibhishana what he wanted.

“Return the woman,” he said. “Spare the city. Judge my brother for what he has done, not the people for living beneath him.”

“And afterward?”

“Preserve its laws and households.”

“Under whom?”

Vibhishana hesitated.

The hesitation answered before he did.

“Under a ruler who will keep the settlement,” he said.

The northern prince was silent for several moments.

Then he asked for seawater.

A soldier brought it in a bronze cooking cup because no ceremonial vessel had crossed the causeway. There were grains of sand at the bottom.

The younger prince dipped his fingers into the water.

“If we prevail,” the northern prince said, “the island will not be annexed. Its people will not become property of the northern kingdoms. You will govern it under the terms you have described.”

Vibhishana looked at the cup.

He could refuse.

He could say that no crown should be promised while his brother lived.

He could say he had come only for peace.

Instead he said, “First promise the city will be spared.”

“If its soldiers lay down their weapons, its homes and laws will remain.”

It was not an unconditional promise. Vibhishana accepted it as though it were.

The younger prince touched seawater to his forehead.

No drums sounded. No priests chanted. A cook waited nearby for the return of his cup.

The forest companies nevertheless bowed.

Across the water, a king still sat in his council hall.

On the shore, another had been named before the first had died.

Salt water ran beside Vibhishana’s nose and entered the corner of his mouth.

It tasted of home.


“So you accepted the throne,” Shuka had said.

They were still standing beside the mangroves. Beyond them, the enemy camp was beginning its evening meal. Smoke lay low over the water. Someone was frying onions in an iron pan, and the smell was offensively ordinary.

Vibhishana looked at him.

“I accepted responsibility.”

“That is a sentence officials use when they have accepted something they would rather not name.”

“You have been spending too much time in the palace.”

“I have been spending exactly enough time there to recognise the language.”

For the first time that evening, Vibhishana smiled. It was brief and tired. In it Shuka could see the younger brother who had once stood in a music room waiting for someone to say that the final phrase was his.

Then the smile went.

“Yes,” Vibhishana said. “I accepted it.”

“Before your brother is dead.”

“In the hope that he need not die.”

“Did they promise you that?”

Vibhishana looked across the water.

“No.”

It was the cleanest answer he had given.

Shuka let the silence remain. In the camp, a cook shouted at someone who had taken the wrong pot. A soldier laughed. The world had the indecency to continue arranging supper around treason.

“What did you give them?” Shuka asked.

Vibhishana’s eyes returned to him.

“Advice.”

“About what?”

“How to end the war quickly.”

“That can mean anything from drafting a letter to opening a gate.”

“I have opened no gate.”

It was true. Shuka heard the truth in it and, because he wanted the truth to be enough, did not examine its edges.

“Did you give them the harbour soundings?”

“No.”

“The cistern locations?”

“No.”

“The rotation of the watch?”

“No.”

“Then what knowledge ends a war quickly?”

Vibhishana touched the mud beside his sandal with the end of a reed.

“The knowledge that a city is not its king,” he said. “The knowledge that its people may surrender without dishonour. The knowledge that there are men inside the walls who do not approve of everything done in their name.”

He had answered a different question beautifully.

At the time, Shuka had admired the answer.

“You asked me how long I had hated him,” Vibhishana said. “Now you know I did not cross because I hated him.”

“You crossed because you loved the city.”

“Yes.”

“And because you believed you would govern it better.”

Vibhishana’s face tightened.

“Both things may be true.”

“That is inconvenient.”

“Most honest things are.”

Shuka watched the reed draw a narrow line through the mud. It curved between two mangrove roots and stopped at the water.

“Why tell me any of this?” he asked.

“Because you are going home,” Vibhishana said. “And I cannot.”

“You could come with me.”

“No.”

“You could stand before him and say all of this.”

“I did. He heard accusation where I meant warning.”

“Perhaps you placed some accusation inside the warning.”

Vibhishana considered that.

“Perhaps.”

He dropped the reed.

“When you return, do not carry this conversation as a message. I am not asking you to defend me. I am asking that one man know I did not wake one morning hungry for my brother’s chair.”

“Yet you accepted it.”

“Yes.”

Again, the clean answer.

Shuka had carried that answer home with him. He had carried the music room, the garland, the boys at the military school, the burned market and the wife who had refused to cross. He had carried the image of seawater drying upon Vibhishana’s brow.

But he had not carried them to the king.

He had told himself the conversation was personal. It had happened after his release, outside the work for which he had been sent. No order had required him to report a brother’s childhood sorrow. No military position had been disclosed. No gate had been opened.

And beneath every careful reason lay a simpler one.

Vibhishana had trusted him.

Shuka had wanted to deserve it.

He had also wanted to return to his own king without placing another wound at his feet.

Loyalty had not forced Shuka to lie. It had done something quieter. It had taught him which truths looked like home and which looked like betrayal. Faced with a story that could be either, he had folded it small and kept it inside himself.

Until the salt road opened.


The western room shook.

Dust fell from the carved lintel in a pale sheet. One of the oil lamps went out. From somewhere below came a deep, sustained roar—the sound of the upper sluice being opened.

Shuka was back in the palace.

For one confused instant he could still smell onions frying in the enemy camp. Then the room filled again with salt, lamp smoke and the sour odour of frightened men.

A palace guard appeared in the doorway.

He had lost his helmet. Blood covered one side of his face, though the cut itself was small. In both hands he carried a length of blue cord.

The cord had held the king’s missing shoulder clasp.

Shuka stood so quickly that the stool fell behind him.

“Where did you get that?”

“At the lower sluice.”

“Where is the king?”

The guard tried to answer. His mouth moved once without sound.

“Where is he?” Shuka repeated.

“The flood took the platform.”

The room became very quiet.

“Was he on it?”

“Yes.”

Shuka looked down at the wax tablet. The hidden stones ran beneath his fingers in a neat row. A child had once found them after a cyclone. A younger brother had mapped them. An elder brother had added them to the only private copy.

Outside, men were shouting from roof to roof.

“The eastern court has fallen!” someone cried.

Another voice answered, “No, the eastern court holds!”

A third shouted that the lower market was burning.

Three versions of the city existed before the first had finished echoing.

Shuka gripped the edge of the table.

“How many came through?” he asked.

“We do not know.”

“How many of ours remain at the sluice?”

“We do not know.”

“Did the flood close the road?”

“We think so.”

“Thinking is what men do before they know. Go and know.”

The guard did not move.

His eyes were fixed on the blue cord in his hands.

Shuka softened his voice.

“Give me that. Then go.”

The guard placed the cord on the table and ran.

Shuka remained standing.

He had spent years learning how to wait without appearing to wait. In foreign courts he had waited beside wells, in kitchens, under carts and once inside a grain chest occupied by an indignant family of mice. He knew how to let fear pass through the body without disturbing the face.

Now there was no foreign court to deceive.

His hands began to shake.

He thought of the prophecy.

Not an enemy who broke the walls.

His own blood showing the beasts a road.

He thought of the king fastening ceremonial armour with blue cord because the proper clasp had vanished. He thought of the ridiculous painted parrot on the breastplate. He thought of the king saying, *If I do not return, improvise*, as if death were an absent minister whose work could be distributed among the staff.

Shuka picked up the cord.

It was wet with salt water. One end was dark with blood.

The door curtain moved.

Shuka turned.

The king stood there.

For a moment neither man spoke.

The ceremonial breastplate hung crooked from one shoulder. The painted parrot had acquired a crack through its yellow beak, which improved its dignity. One forearm was wrapped in a strip torn from someone’s tunic. Salt water streamed from the edge of the armour and gathered around his feet.

There was blood on his cheek, blood at his wrist and blood across the gold plates of his chest.

Some of it was his.

Most of it was not.

“You have my cord,” the king said.

Shuka could not answer.

The king looked at the fallen stool.

“And you have murdered a piece of furniture.”

Shuka made a sound that might have been a laugh if fear had not broken it halfway.

The king stepped into the room. His right leg dragged slightly.

“The boy,” he said. “The one who brought the map. Is he alive?”

“The physicians have him.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“He was alive when they carried him away.”

“Find out.”

“I will.”

“And the lower houses?”

“The watch cleared most of them before the sluice opened.”

“Most?”

“Two families are unaccounted for.”

“Find them before you find me a dry robe.”

Only then did the king place one hand against the wall.

The movement was small. It told Shuka more about the wound than all the blood.

“Sit,” Shuka said.

“That sounded dangerously like an order.”

“Then punish me after you sit.”

The king lowered himself onto the surviving stool. Water ran from him in thin red threads.

Shuka fetched the basin. The water inside was clean for less than a breath. He pressed a cloth to the cut above the king’s hip.

The king inhaled sharply.

“The road?” Shuka asked.

“Closed. For tonight.”

“How many crossed?”

“Enough.”

“How did you escape the platform?”

“Without elegance.”

“That is not a military term.”

“It will be when I write the report.”

The humour landed between them and died there.

The king saw the wax tablet on the table.

He saw the blue cord beside it.

Then he looked at Shuka’s face.

“You were thinking of my brother,” he said.

It was not a question.

Shuka put down the bloodied cloth.

“Yes.”

“A reasonable night for it.”

“He spoke to me.”

The king’s expression did not change.

“In the enemy camp,” Shuka continued. “After they released me. He spoke to me alone.”

For several seconds the only sound was water dripping from the king’s armour.

“I asked you whether he spoke to you,” the king said.

“You asked before the council whether he came near while I was bound. I told you he gave his name to the guards and that the ropes were tied before he came closer.”

“All true.”

“Yes.”

“And arranged so that I would hear a no.”

Shuka lowered his eyes.

“Yes.”

The king leaned back. Pain crossed his face, but when he spoke his voice was level.

“Tell me now.”

So Shuka did.

He told him about the music room and the unfinished phrase. He told him about the garland. He told him about the boys at the military school and the elder brother who had protected the younger with a violence that had felt, to the younger boy, almost like another form of ownership.

He told him about the cyclone.

About the black stones visible beneath the emptied water.

About the map Vibhishana had drawn at seventeen.

The king closed his eyes.

“Did he tell you he showed them the road?”

“No.”

“Did you ask what knowledge he gave them?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“He said he gave them knowledge that might end the war quickly. He denied giving harbour soundings, cisterns or watch rotations. He said he had opened no gate.”

The king opened his eyes.

“He opened no gate.”

“No.”

“He merely showed them where the sea had made one.”

Shuka looked at the map.

“I did not understand.”

“You did not wish to.”

The words were quiet. That made them worse.

“I thought it was a family memory.”

“It was a military survey.”

“He told it as a story about being forgotten.”

“Because that was the version in which you would protect it.”

Shuka felt the tears before he could stop them. He hated them for arriving now, when the king had returned bleeding and the city had no time for the cleanliness of his remorse.

He wiped his face with the back of his wrist.

“I thought I was keeping a confidence.”

“You were.”

“I did not think it belonged to the war.”

“Everything a man tells the enemy’s spy in an enemy camp belongs at least partly to the war.”

“He did not ask me to carry a message.”

“He did not need to. He asked you to carry a version.”

Shuka’s mouth tightened.

“I wanted to believe him.”

“Why?”

There were several answers. None excused him.

“Because he was alone,” Shuka said. “Because he loved you. Because some of what he said was true. Because I had been taught to hear one version of our story as loyalty, and his version sounded like betrayal until I heard it in his voice. Then I was ashamed that I had never heard it before.”

The king watched him.

“And because,” Shuka continued, “I did not want to return here and place his childhood at your feet like stolen correspondence.”

“So you protected him from me.”

“Perhaps.”

“And me from him?”

Shuka could not make the answer noble.

“Perhaps.”

“A spy does not decide which truths his king deserves.”

“No.”

“You did.”

“Yes.”

The king reached for the cup on the table. His hand trembled once before closing around it.

“Did he accept their promise of my throne?”

Shuka looked up.

“How did you—”

“Because no northern prince receives a king’s brother and forgets to imagine a crown. Did he accept?”

“Yes.”

The king drank.

“Did he refuse until I was dead?”

“No.”

“Did he ask that I be spared?”

Shuka remembered the exact exchange.

“He said he hoped you need not die. He admitted they had not promised it.”

The king nodded once.

There was no rage in his face. Only recognition, which was harder to witness.

“How did they crown him?”

“They did not call it a coronation.”

“Of course not.”

“The prince touched seawater to his forehead. In a cooking cup. There was sand in it.”

The king looked down at the blood and salt drying on his own hands.

“My brother always did prefer modest ceremonies.”

The joke was so gentle that Shuka began to cry properly.

He turned away.

“I am sorry.”

“For which part?”

“For deciding I knew what mattered.”

“That is a large apology. It may take several reigns to complete.”

Shuka laughed through the tears and despised himself for it.

The king set down the cup.

“Look at me.”

Shuka did.

“Do you trust me?” he asked.

“That is not the question tonight.”

“It is mine.”

The king considered him for a long while.

“I do not trust you to decide which truths I deserve.”

Shuka accepted the sentence.

“Then why am I still here?”

“Because you have finally stopped trusting your own judgement.”

The king shifted on the stool and winced.

“That may make you useful.”

Shuka looked towards the door. Two guards waited beyond the curtain, near enough to hear an order for his arrest.

No order came.

“Will you forgive him?” Shuka asked.

The king gave him a tired look.

“You have mistaken me for a priest.”

“Do you believe he crossed to save the city?”

“Yes.”

The answer surprised Shuka.

“Do you believe he wanted your throne?”

“Yes.”

“Both?”

“Most honest things are inconvenient.”

Vibhishana’s words, returned in his brother’s voice.

The king looked at the map.

“He loved this city. He loved me. He believed I had endangered both. He also wanted, at last, to stand somewhere without my shadow falling over him.”

“Then is he a traitor?”

“To me, certainly.”

“To the city?”

The king’s fingers rested on the line of hidden stones.

“That is the question he crossed the water to avoid.”

Outside, the alarm changed.

Three low beats. A pause. Three more.

The salt road was closed. The eastern court still held. The enemy had withdrawn from the flooded channel.

For now.

A messenger entered and bowed.

“Majesty, their main force is moving towards the northern embankment. We have perhaps an hour.”

The king rose.

His injured leg nearly failed beneath him. Shuka reached out, then stopped, uncertain whether touch was still permitted.

The king noticed.

“Do not become ceremonial,” he said. “It is exhausting.”

Shuka took his arm.

Together they crossed to the window.

The eastern sky had begun to pale. Below them, the flooded quarter shone black and silver. Broken carts turned slowly in the current. A roof drifted past with a clay water pot still balanced absurdly upon it.

Beyond the walls, thousands of cooking fires were being stamped out.

The enemy was preparing to move.

“The prophecy was right,” Shuka said.

“Prophecies usually become right after enough people have worked to obey them.”

“Your own blood showed them the road.”

“Yes.”

“And the beasts entered.”

The king looked at the distant standards lifting in the dawn. From here, the carved animals on them appeared alive.

“Men become beasts very easily in another man’s song,” he said.

The first morning bell sounded from the temple quarter.

One brother had heard it six mornings earlier with seawater drying upon his brow.

The other heard it now with salt and blood drying between his fingers.

Between them lay a road that appeared only when the sea withdrew.

And for the first time in his life, Shuka understood that a hidden road did not become less real because everyone had agreed not to name it.
