# Chapter One

## The Returned Man

The king was deciding whether the western room needed airing when a guard told him the enemy had returned his spy alive.

He had not expected the man back. Spies returned in pieces, if an enemy wished to make a point, or not at all if he did not. To send one home alive was stranger. A dead spy carried a warning. A living one carried whatever the enemy had placed inside his mind.

The western room smelled of damp stone, old jasmine and the sharp vinegar the cleaners had used on the floor. Four flames burned in the five-branched lamp. The fifth wick had slipped beneath the sesame oil, leaving a thin black crescent above the wall niche. Sea air pressed against the wooden screens, but the screens had been shut for three days because one of their hinges had begun to pull away from the frame. Even in a palace faced with polished copper and pale shell, a loose hinge could spoil a wall if nobody attended to it.

“Open both screens,” the king said. “Put a stool beneath the broken one until the carpenter comes. Move the wounded boys from the southern passage in here before midnight; the roof there is leaking again. And have someone clean that lamp. If the wick drowns twice, the oil is dirty.” He looked at the guard. “Now bring me the man whom our enemy chose not to kill.”

The guard bowed and left with the relief of someone who had successfully delivered bad news.

Dharan, the chamberlain, remained by the door. He was a narrow old man with a white beard that divided neatly over his collarbone. He had served three generations of the royal household and carried a ring of keys large enough to anchor a small fishing boat. During the day, the keys announced him before his voice did. Tonight he had wrapped them in a strip of blue cloth so their noise would not wake the children sleeping in the rooms below.

“The wounded cannot be brought here while the carpet remains, Majesty,” he said.

The king looked down. The carpet had come from a western port whose name had changed twice since the merchants first unrolled it in the palace. Dark red vines ran through a field of blue. Deer no larger than a thumb drank from silver pools along its border. One corner had been burned by a brazier many years ago, and the repair was visible only to someone who knew where to look.

“Roll it.”

Dharan’s eyes moved briefly towards the rain tapping against the screens. “The storerooms are full.”

“Then put it in my music room.”

“Your music room is holding the records from the eastern archive.”

“Put the records in the old queen’s dressing room.”

“The old queen’s dressing room has no shutters.”

The king considered this.

“Then leave the carpet where it is and cover it with reed mats.”

Dharan bowed. It was a very small bow, reserved for occasions when the household had reached the answer he had suggested at the beginning.

Two servants entered carrying a bundle of mats between them. They were sisters from the potters’ quarter, alike enough that courtiers often confused them and different enough that neither forgave it. The elder, Mina, had a pale scar on her chin. Her younger sister had tied a yellow thread around her wrist to distinguish herself, though the thread had darkened almost to brown with work.

“Not over the whole carpet,” the king said. “Only from the door to the beds. It will smell of wet grass by morning if you cover it completely.”

Mina nodded. Her sister glanced at the king, then quickly lowered her eyes.

“What is it?” he asked.

The girl froze with the end of the mat in her hands.

“Speak,” Mina whispered.

“My brother is in the southern ward, Majesty.”

“Which brother?”

“Ketu. He came from the river gate this afternoon. A spear went through his shoulder.”

The king remembered a boy with wide ears who used to wait beside the kitchen drain for burnt pieces of flatbread. He had once fallen asleep inside an empty grain basket and remained undiscovered until a cook tried to fill it.

“He still steals from the kitchen?”

For the first time, the girl looked directly at him. “Only when he is hungry.”

“Then he has improved.” The king pointed towards the northern wall. “Put his bed beneath the shell panel. It is the driest part of the room. Tell the physician the king asked whether the wound was washed before it was stitched. Do not tell her I asked twice. She will become offended and spend the night writing me a lecture.”

The girl smiled before remembering that she was frightened.

“Thank you, Majesty.”

“Thank me if your brother keeps the arm. For now, lay the mats.”

They worked quickly. Dharan crossed to the lamp, pinched out the four good flames and lifted the warm brass bowl from its stand. The sudden loss of light changed the room. Gold withdrew into brown. The shell panels ceased to shine. In the dimness, the king could hear the palace more clearly: sandals passing in the corridor, a copper pot set down too hard in the kitchen court, the low groan of a man being lifted from one stretcher to another.

Farther away, beyond the outer walls, a dull knock repeated at slow intervals.

The enemy was building something in the dark.

The knock had continued since sunset. Sometimes it stopped for ten or twenty breaths, then began again with the same patience. The king had asked three engineers what it was. One believed they were driving posts into the wet ground. Another thought they were assembling platforms for archers. The third had said, with unusual honesty, that sound travelled strangely across water and it might be nothing more dangerous than men repairing a cart.

The king preferred the third man, though he had promoted the first.

Dharan returned the lamp with clean oil and five new wicks. He lit them from a small clay flame carried by a boy who guarded the household fire. The boy’s hair was flattened on one side from sleep. He tried to cover a yawn with his shoulder.

“Go back to bed after this,” the king told him.

“I am on duty until the second watch, Majesty.”

“Who told you that?”

The boy pointed at Dharan.

The chamberlain showed no concern at being accused.

“The older fire boy has a fever,” he said.

“Then find an adult who knows how not to set fire to a palace. This one can barely keep his eyes open.”

“The adults are carrying the wounded.”

“Then you guard the flame.”

Dharan looked at the king’s bare feet, the simple white cloth wrapped around his waist and the old shawl over his shoulders.

“Majesty—”

“I did not offer.”

The chamberlain accepted this distinction. He gave the boy a copper coin from his own sash and sent him away.

The king had worn no crown since the first alarm at dusk. The heavy band left a pain above his ears after several hours, and there was no one in the western room who needed reminding of his position. A narrow gold ring remained on his right hand. It bore the seal of the kingdom and had worn a smooth hollow against the finger beneath it.

He went to the open screen.

The rain was little more than mist now. It drifted in from the sea and settled cold upon his arms. Below, the palace descended through courtyards and pillared halls towards the city. Roofs shone black. Water ran from carved mouths at the corners of buildings and struck the drains in silver ropes. Lamps moved through the lower streets where families were leaving houses close to the northern wall. From this height they appeared as a patient line of fireflies, each carrying bedding, cooking pots and whatever could be lifted in two hands.

The wealth of the city was most visible at night. Copper roofs held the light. Warehouses stood in long orderly rows beside the harbour. The towers at the sea gate were faced with pale stone that glowed even beneath the clouds. Merchants from seven coasts had once complained that the harbour duties were too high and returned the next season with twice as many ships.

Tonight the harbour was empty.

The ships had been pulled into the southern inlets or burned at their moorings. The enemy’s watchfires stretched along the opposite shore and across the new road they had made through the sea. In daylight, the causeway looked rough and impossible: stone, timber, packed earth, broken boats, whole trees driven into the shallow bed. At night it was a black line laid across the water, with fires moving upon it.

The first reports had called it a miracle. The king had struck that word from every copy.

A miracle was what frightened men called engineering when they had not watched the labour.

Thousands had carried stone. Divers had marked the deepest channels with ropes. Men who knew the tides had worked waist-deep in water while archers guarded them from the shore. The road had not appeared because the sea obeyed a northern prince. It had been measured, planned and built.

That made it more dangerous, not less.

“He is here,” Dharan said.

The king turned.

The spy stood between two guards at the entrance. For an instant he looked shorter than the king remembered. Then the king saw that he was leaning to one side to keep his weight off his left foot.

Mud covered him from the knees down. His tunic had once been grey; it was now the colour of a road after rain. One sleeve had been torn away, and a strip of rough cloth had been wrapped around his forearm. The cloth was too tight. His fingers were swollen and darker than the rest of his hand. There was dried blood at the corner of his mouth and a clean line around his neck where a cord had recently been cut.

He wore no sandals.

The spy lowered himself to one knee.

“Majesty.”

His voice was steady. That was the first thing the king found reassuring.

“Stand up, Shuka.”

Shuka tried. His injured foot failed beneath him, and one of the guards caught his elbow.

“I said stand, not fall. Put him in the chair.”

The guards guided him towards a low chair beside the window. Shuka resisted until the king looked at him. He then sat with the careful obedience of a man pretending the decision had been his.

“Who tied that cloth?” the king asked.

“One of their healers.”

“Their healer dislikes your hand.”

“He disliked the rest of me as well.”

The king crouched beside the chair and loosened the knot. Shuka’s swollen fingers twitched as blood began to return.

“This will hurt.”

“It already hurts.”

“Then you are prepared.”

He unwound the cloth. The cut beneath it was shallow but filled with grit. Whoever had dressed it had stopped the bleeding and nothing more.

“Mina,” the king called.

The elder sister appeared from behind a screen with a folded mat in her arms.

“Bring boiled water, clean linen and the small jar from my travelling chest. Not the blue jar. The brown one.”

“The bitter one?”

“They are all bitter.”

Mina frowned, searching her memory.

“The one that smells of pine?”

“Yes.”

She left without bowing. The king preferred servants who forgot ceremony when there was work to do.

Shuka watched him examine the wound.

“You have physicians for this.”

“I have physicians for people who listen to them.”

“I would have gone to the infirmary.”

“You would have delivered your report first, lost the hand by dawn and called it loyalty.”

Shuka did not answer.

“Also,” the king said, “the infirmary is moving into this room, so you have saved yourself a journey.”

The younger servant carried in a brass basin. Water trembled against its rim. Behind her came Mina with linen and the jar. The king washed the cut until the water clouded. Shuka’s jaw tightened, but he made no sound.

“Has Ketu arrived?” the king asked.

“They are bringing him now.”

“Put him where I said. And ask the kitchens for rice water with ginger. Enough for twenty.”

“There will be more than twenty.”

Mina glanced towards the passage, where stretcher poles knocked softly against the wall.

“Then enough for forty.”

“The kitchen steward has locked the ginger store.”

“Why?”

The king’s hand paused above Shuka’s wound.

“He says the household accounts do not permit night distribution without his seal.”

The king held out his right hand. Mina looked at it, confused.

“Bring me a palm leaf.”

Dharan produced one from inside his robe, along with a small iron stylus. The king scratched a brief order into the leaf and pressed his ring into a square of warm wax at its edge.

“Give him this. Tell him it permits ginger. It also permits you to strike him once with a wooden spoon if he makes you come back.”

Mina studied the order.

“Is that written here?”

“It is implied by the seal.”

She tucked the leaf into her sash and left.

The king spread the pine-smelling paste over Shuka’s wound and wrapped it again, more loosely.

“Move your fingers.”

Shuka did.

“Again.”

The fingers closed with difficulty.

“You will keep the hand.”

“That is useful. I write with it.”

“You lie with it too.”

“Only when writing reports.”

The king smiled despite himself. He rose and washed his hands in the basin.

The guards were still standing by the door.

“Wait outside,” he told them.

They withdrew. Dharan remained.

“You too.”

The chamberlain looked around the room as though there might be one last object whose safety outweighed the privacy of kings.

“The wounded will begin arriving.”

“Let them. Put screens around the beds. No one bleeding into a mattress will be interested in state secrets.”

Dharan wrapped his keys more tightly and left.

For the first time since Shuka entered, the room became still.

The king took the chair opposite him. Between them stood a low table of black wood. A servant had set a silver bowl of pomegranates upon it earlier in the evening. One fruit had split. Its seeds showed through the skin like wet red stones.

Shuka stared at the fruit.

“When did you last eat?” the king asked.

“Yesterday.”

“Morning or evening?”

“I do not remember.”

The king pushed the bowl towards him.

Shuka reached for the split fruit with his good hand and stopped.

“May I wash?”

“You have crossed an enemy army, a flooded causeway and half my palace. At this point the pomegranate is in greater danger from the table.”

Shuka broke it open. Juice ran across his palm. He ate slowly at first, then with an attention that excluded everything else. The king waited.

It was one of the first lessons of command: hungry men answered the question they feared, not the one they heard.

Outside the screen, rain hissed against the copper roofs. The distant knocking stopped. In its absence the city seemed to hold its breath.

“How many?” the king asked when half the fruit was gone.

Shuka swallowed.

“On the northern shore?”

“Everywhere.”

“I could give you a number.”

Shuka watched him, waiting to see which kind of answer was wanted.

“If you give me the number they wanted you to see, I will send you back for another.”

“Then I cannot give you a number.”

“Good. Tell me what you saw.”

Shuka placed the fruit on the table. His fingertips were red.

“Their camp runs from the old salt landing to the eastern mangroves. The forest companies are arranged by banner, not by village. Each company cooks separately but receives grain from a common store. They have fewer carts than we believed. Most of their food crossed on men’s backs.”

“How many days of grain?”

“Five if they eat well. Eight if they do not.”

The king measured the distance to the city in days of grain rather than miles.

“They crossed an ocean road with eight days of grain?”

“They expect to eat ours after that.”

The king leaned back.

“Sensibly.”

“They have fishing nets.”

“Less sensibly. This coast will not feed that many.”

“Their commander knows. The nets are for the officers and the wounded.”

The king brushed a drop of pomegranate juice from the table with his thumb.

“Which commander?”

“The forest king.”

“The one who received his throne with northern help.”

“The same.”

The king looked towards the open screen. Somewhere beyond the rain stood a man who had lost a kingdom to his own elder brother, recovered it through a stranger’s arrow and paid for the favour with an army. Songs would make that arrangement sound like friendship. Ledgers would have called it debt.

“Are his people loyal?”

“To him? Some. To the northern prince? More than I expected.”

“Why?”

“Because he keeps winning.”

The answer irritated the king because it required no further explanation.

“Their banners?”

“Mostly clan signs. Boar, bear, langur, red-faced macaque. The companies paint the marks on their shields and sometimes on their bodies.”

“And do they have tails?”

Shuka looked up.

“No.”

“I ask because three reports have described an army of monkeys climbing my walls. One captain swore they speak to one another in cries.”

“They use bird calls in the forest. Across open ground they use drums.”

Shuka flexed his bandaged hand, watching the linen tighten over his knuckles.

“So men who fight beneath a monkey banner have become monkeys before reaching the city.”

“It makes them easier to kill in the telling.”

The king turned that sentence over in his mind.

“And perhaps easier to send against us,” he said.

Shuka lowered his eyes to the pomegranate.

“Perhaps.”

“The road through the sea?”

“Wider than yesterday. They are still reinforcing it. They built the first line along the ridge beneath the shallows. Timber cages hold loose stone where the current is strong. The deep channel has boats tied side by side beneath planks.”

The king glanced towards the dark beyond the screen.

“Who designed it?”

“A young engineer from the forest clans. Nala.”

The name meant nothing to the king, which made him uneasy. He knew the names of the northern prince, the prince’s younger brother, the exiled forest king, the old commander with the bear on his standard and the broad-shouldered envoy who had entered the city alone and left half a district burning. Yet a man he had never heard of had laid a road across the sea.

Kingdoms were rarely destroyed by the famous alone.

“How many men maintain the causeway?”

“More at low tide. Fewer when the water rises. They have marked the safe edge with white stones. Anyone who steps beyond them sinks to the thigh.”

“And their heavy weapons?”

“Not yet across.”

Another three blows came from the darkness, regular as a carpenter knocking at a door.

“Then the knocking?”

“They are assembling wooden towers on this side of the strait.”

The king listened. The sound had begun again, three slow blows followed by a pause.

“How high?”

“Higher than the northern wall if they finish.”

“They will not finish.”

Shuka’s face changed almost too slightly to notice.

“Say it,” the king told him.

“You have said that about the causeway for six days.”

The king rested his hands on the arms of the chair.

Any other minister would have softened the sentence before speaking it. Shuka had once done so too. Years in the king’s service had taught him that truth survived court only when delivered before fear could dress it.

“Yes,” the king said. “And I was wrong for six days. That does not make me wrong tonight.”

Shuka inclined his head.

It was neither agreement nor apology.

The first wounded men entered the room behind a folding screen. The servants had removed their armour and cut away the cloth around their injuries. Without helmets and weapons, they seemed younger. One had a beard that grew only along the left side of his jaw. Another kept asking for his mother in the embarrassed voice of someone who knew she was hundreds of miles away.

Mina’s brother came last. Ketu was conscious, though his skin had the grey shine of wet clay. The spear wound had been packed with cloth. He saw the king and tried to lift his good arm.

“Do not salute indoors,” the king said. “You will break something.”

Ketu’s mouth moved in what might have been a smile.

The servants placed him beneath the shell panel. The physician followed, already frowning at the use of a guest chamber as a ward.

“The wound was washed,” she said before the king could ask.

“Then I shall not ask twice.”

The physician’s frown deepened. “Who told you I said that?”

The king turned back to Shuka.

Behind the screen came the small sounds of work: water poured into bowls, linen torn into strips, a bronze instrument placed carefully on wood. The palace had always contained many worlds divided by thin walls. A treaty might be negotiated beside a room where a child was being born. A prince might learn that his father had died while servants argued in the passage about lamp oil. Tonight men discussed the survival of the kingdom within ten paces of a cleaner trying to remove mud from a carpet.

“Tell me how you were taken,” the king said.

Shuka rubbed the cord mark around his neck.

“Vibhishana recognised me.”

The name settled differently now. Vibhishana, his youngest brother, had once sat at his right hand in council and corrected figures no other man dared question. Six days ago, after their last argument over the woman, he had crossed to the enemy camp. Before sunset, the northern prince had publicly named him the man who would govern the island when the war was over.

The king’s gaze remained on Shuka’s face.

“Of course he did.”

“I was dressed as one of the forest soldiers.”

“Badly?”

“Very well.”

The king looked at the torn tunic and the mud worked into its seams.

“Then how?”

“He said I was watching the officers rather than the food.”

The king almost laughed. His brother had always noticed where people looked. As a boy he could tell which servant had stolen sweet cakes by watching everyone except the servant being questioned.

“Did he speak to you?”

“He gave my name to their guards. They tied me before he came closer.”

“Did he look ashamed?”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly to be softened.

The king rose and crossed to the window. Rain touched his face. The fires on the causeway had multiplied. He counted eleven between the island and the far shore, then lost the smallest behind a fold of mist.

“Continue.”

“The forest soldiers wanted to hang me where our watchmen could see. One had lost two brothers in the fire at the western gate. Another said spies were not covered by the laws of war.”

“He was correct.”

“The northern prince disagreed.”

The king turned back.

Shuka’s expression had altered again. It was not admiration, exactly. Admiration was warmer and easier. This was the look of a man who had found an object where his map showed empty ground.

“Describe him,” the king said.

“You know what he looks like.”

“I know what painters who have never seen him think he looks like.”

Shuka considered.

“Taller than me. Shorter than you. His skin is darker from months outside. His hair reaches his shoulders but is tied badly, as if he did it without a mirror. There is an old scar beneath his chin and a fresh cut across one knuckle. His clothes were clean at sunset. They were muddy when I saw him.”

The king made no sign that this ordinary description unsettled him more than a heroic one might have.

“Jewels?”

“None.”

“A crown?”

“No.”

The king waited a moment before asking the question that mattered least and would be repeated most.

“Divine light?”

Shuka met his eyes.

“A lamp stood behind him.”

The king smiled.

“That will become divine light by the time the story reaches the mainland.”

“Perhaps.”

“You saw no god?”

“I saw a tired man with a bow.”

The answer should have pleased him. It did not.

“What did the tired man do?”

“He asked whether I had completed my work.”

“Your work?”

“My count of his army. The positions of his commanders. The grain stores. The causeway.”

The king’s gaze moved briefly towards the fires on the water.

“And what did you say?”

“I thought it was a joke.”

“Was it?”

“No. He told his men to untie me. Then he said that if there was anything I had failed to inspect, someone should show it to me before I left.”

Behind the screen, the physician dropped something metal into a bowl. It struck the rim with a clear note.

The king returned slowly to his chair.

“He displayed his army to a spy.”

“Yes.”

“Because he wanted you to bring me fear.”

“That may be why.”

Shuka looked at the fresh bandage, as if mercy had left a second mark there.

“There is another reason?”

“He said a spy should not be killed for serving his king.”

“A memorable sentence.”

“Yes.”

The king turned his seal ring once around his finger.

“Especially when repeated in the palace of that king.”

Shuka picked a pomegranate seed from the table and pressed it between his fingers until the juice appeared.

“Mercy can be sincere and useful at the same time.”

“Most useful things can.”

“That does not make them false.”

The king studied him.

There had been a time when Shuka would have offered that thought as a question. Tonight he placed it between them like a cup from which either man might drink.

“You admire him.”

“I was his prisoner for less than an hour.”

“Men have fallen in love more quickly.”

Shuka did not smile this time.

“You asked what I saw.”

“I did.”

“Then do not punish me because it was not what you wished me to see.”

The king’s first response was anger. It arrived cleanly, before thought: a tightening in his throat, warmth behind the eyes, the old desire to make the room remember who held power in it.

Then he noticed Shuka’s injured foot resting crookedly on the mat and the red line left by the cord around his neck.

Anger was information. It did not have to become instruction.

“If I punished you for unwelcome facts,” he said, “you would have learned to bring me pleasant lies years ago.”

“Others learned.”

“Others were better students.”

Shuka’s mouth moved, almost smiling.

The king leaned forward.

“Did he threaten the city?”

“Yes.”

“Before or after the mercy?”

“After.”

The king nodded as though Shuka had confirmed a calculation.

“There. Even his kindness knows where to stand in a speech.”

“Your kindness does too.”

The words remained in the air.

From behind the screen came the smell of ginger as bowls of rice water arrived from the kitchens. Someone whispered that it was too hot. Someone else said wounded men could not wait for soup to cool. Mina’s sister began blowing across the surface of each bowl before handing it on.

The king looked towards the screen and then at the clean bandage around Shuka’s arm.

“Explain.”

Shuka did not look away.

“You dressed my hand before asking what I had learned. That was kindness.”

“And where did it stand in my speech?”

“Before the questions. Where I would remember it.”

The king sat back.

It would have been easy to dismiss the comparison. He had cleaned the wound because it needed cleaning. He had ordered food because an empty stomach made poor reports. He had cared whether Shuka kept his hand because Shuka was useful, loyal and, in some inconvenient place beyond those words, dear to him.

None of those reasons cancelled the others.

“So we are alike,” he said.

“I did not say that.”

“You placed our acts beside each other.”

“To understand one act. Not two men.”

The king lifted one eyebrow.

“Careful, Shuka. That almost sounded wise.”

“The cord deprived my brain of air. It may pass.”

The king laughed. It was a quiet laugh, but several people behind the screen stopped moving. The palace had heard very little laughter that week.

“What was his threat?” he asked.

Shuka wiped the pomegranate juice from his hand with the edge of his torn tunic.

“At first light, his full army crosses. The towers will move against the northern wall. The forest companies will attack the river gate and the eastern rise together. Their archers will target the roofs, not the battlements.”

“To start fires behind the defenders.”

“Yes.”

“The wind will be against them after dawn.”

Shuka glanced at the rain trailing from the edge of the open screen.

“Their weather men say it will turn.”

“Their weather men said the rain would stop before sunset.”

“It did.”

The king glanced at the mist beyond the screen.

“Eventually.”

Shuka continued. “He said the city could still be spared.”

“Cities are always spared in the message delivered before they are attacked.”

“He gave conditions.”

“Of course.”

“Open the northern gate. Stand down the wall companies. Allow Vibhishana and the forest king to enter with a small guard.”

The king’s mouth tightened at the name, but he did not interrupt again.

“And after they enter?”

Shuka looked towards the wounded men, though the screen concealed them.

“Return the woman.”

For the first time that night, the king became completely still.

The room did not change. Rain continued to touch the screens. Ginger and vinegar remained in the air. The lamp flames leaned together in a small movement of wind and then straightened. Yet everything that had seemed immediate—the causeway, the towers, the grain, the wounded boys—shifted around those three words as filings shift around a hidden piece of iron.

Return the woman.

Not the princess. Not the hostage. Not the northern prince’s wife. The woman.

It was either an insult or a refusal to permit the king any shelter inside titles.

“Those were his words?”

“Yes.”

“Exactly?”

“Exactly.”

Shuka rubbed his thumb over the stain the pomegranate had left on his palm.

“And if I comply?”

“The army halts outside the wall. The city is not sacked. Its laws and households remain under protection until terms are agreed.”

“My terms?”

“He did not say.”

The king’s face remained expressionless.

“My life?”

“He did not say.”

The king looked again at the causeway. The moving fires were clearer now. Men crossed the sea carrying timber on their shoulders. From this distance they were black strokes against orange light.

“He spared you so that you could place this offer in my room.”

“Yes.”

“And so that you could describe him as merciful before asking me to kneel.”

“Yes.”

The king listened to the answer while looking past Shuka towards the fires.

“You understand the performance.”

“I understand that it was also a performance.”

The king turned.

“Also?”

“He could have killed me and sent the same terms through a herald.”

“A herald would not have admired him.”

“Perhaps he wanted admiration.”

Weariness entered the king’s smile.

“Every king wants admiration.”

“Yes.”

There was no accusation in the word. That made it harder to resist.

Shuka bent to adjust his injured foot. His face tightened.

“Why did you come back?” the king asked.

Shuka looked genuinely surprised.

“You sent me.”

“My brother sent himself. Others have crossed during the night. The northern camp would have fed you, dressed your wound and found some honourable name for betrayal. Why return to a city you believe will fall?”

Shuka’s gaze moved around the western room: the shell panels, the reed mats, the servants’ shadows behind the screen, the magnificent carpet now covered to protect it from soldiers’ blood.

“Because it is my city.”

“That did not hold my brother.”

“Your brother believes he left in order to save it.”

The king’s hand closed around the seal ring.

Shuka saw the movement.

“I did not say he was right.”

“No. You have learned to stand beside dangerous thoughts without touching them.”

“That is also useful in a court.”

The knocking outside stopped once more. This time a horn sounded from beyond the wall: one low note, answered by another farther east.

The wounded men fell silent. Even those who did not understand the signal understood that it belonged to an army preparing to move.

The king crossed to the lamp and lifted the wick that had drowned earlier. It burned cleanly now.

“How long until dawn?” he asked.

Shuka glanced at the black sky.

“Four hours. Perhaps five with the cloud.”

“Enough time for a room to be cleaned badly twice.”

“Majesty—”

“You have delivered the message.”

Shuka straightened as far as his injured foot allowed.

“I have repeated his words.”

“There is a difference?”

“A messenger carries words in one direction. I am expected to carry an answer back.”

The king looked at him.

“Do you intend to cross the causeway again tonight?”

“If you give me an answer.”

“And if I do not?”

“Then that is the answer.”

Behind the screen, Ketu began to shake. The physician called for more blankets. Mina said there were none left in the western cupboards. Dharan’s muffled keys sounded in the passage as he hurried away to find some.

The king watched Shuka listen to all of it—the wounded boy, the servants, the horns outside, the soft crack of rain against the copper roof.

“You want me to accept,” he said.

Shuka took a long breath.

“I want the shaking boy behind that screen to wake tomorrow in the same city.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I trust tonight.”

The king returned to his chair, but he did not sit. He placed both hands on its carved back.

For years Shuka had brought him other men’s secrets: the price at which a harbour master could be bought, the name of a general’s mistress, the night on which a mountain road would be left unguarded. Information became power because Shuka removed himself from it. He had never before brought the king his own judgment without wrapping it in someone else’s words.

“You think this war is mine,” the king said.

“I think parts of it are.”

“Only parts?”

“I was not present at the beginning.”

The king’s fingers tightened around the carved back of the chair.

“You know what happened.”

“I know what each side says happened.”

“And you have decided their version is better?”

“I have decided that every version begins at the moment most useful to the person telling it.”

The king looked at the room he had ordered opened, cleaned and made ready for wounded men. He looked at Shuka’s bandaged hand. He looked beyond the screen towards a city built over thirty years from stone, trade, conquest, law, ambition and labour—his labour, and the labour of people whose names would never be cut into its gates.

Somewhere in the northern camp, another king waited for dawn and believed the story had already chosen its shape.

The king pulled the second chair closer to the table.

“Sit properly,” he told Shuka. “If we are to discuss the end of my kingdom, I refuse to watch you balance on one buttock.”

Shuka shifted with a wince.

The king poured water into two cups. The jug was silver, but one handle had been repaired with plain copper after a servant dropped it years ago. He gave the cup without the dent to Shuka.

“You have brought me his version,” he said. “Now you will hear mine.”

Shuka held the cup between both hands.

“Where does yours begin?”

The king thought of a woman arriving at his court with blood dried across her face and one hand pressed over the place where her nose had been.

“With my sister,” he said.

Shuka looked down into the water.

“That,” he said quietly, “is where you choose to begin.”
