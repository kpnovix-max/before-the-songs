# Chapter Seven

## The Shape of Ten Men

“Wake ten people,” the king said.

The old chamberlain stood beside him on the northern balcony, holding the cracked silver cup he had forgotten to leave behind.

Below them, enemy workers were filling the defensive ditch with the same timber frames that had crossed the sea. Purple water from the dye works moved around their knees. Behind overlapping shields, they lowered stone, tightened rope and made land where the city had carefully arranged that there should be none.

“Which ten?” the chamberlain asked.

The king watched another frame disappear into the ditch.

“The ten who will tell me I am wrong.”

The chamberlain considered this.

“That does not narrow the palace as much as Your Majesty may imagine.”

“Begin with the people I usually avoid.”

“That narrows it less.”

Shuka stood a little behind them, leaning against the parapet to spare his injured foot. The first grey suggestion of dawn had not yet reached the sea, but the darkness above the eastern roofs had thinned. Smoke held the city beneath a second, lower night.

The king turned from the balcony.

“The physician,” he said. “The northern captain. The woman commanding the inner guard. The water-master from the poisoned cistern. The chief cook.”

The chamberlain began counting upon his fingers.

“The harbour engineer,” the king continued. “A rope-maker from the western sheds. The old temple reader who quarrelled with me about the grain levy. You. Shuka.”

The chamberlain examined his hands.

“That is ten if Your Majesty does not intend to attend,” he said.

The king looked at him.

“I had assumed you were summoning ten people because you required ten people,” the chamberlain explained.

“Replace yourself with the chief cook,” the king said.

“You named the chief cook already,” the chamberlain replied.

“Then replace yourself with someone quieter,” said the king.

The chamberlain bowed.

“I shall search the city.”

He hurried away, the cracked cup still in his hand.

Shuka watched the king study the battlefield once more.

“Why those people?” he asked.

“Because generals have spent three days explaining why the enemy’s methods cannot work.”

“And now?”

“Now I would like to hear from people whose work is not improved by being correct too late.”

The king walked towards the council chamber.

His limp was more visible than it had been in the western room. The physician’s bandage restricted his hip. Kumbhakarna’s damaged clasp rested inside his robe, knocking softly against his ribs with each step.

Shuka followed.

They passed servants stripping silk hangings from the walls. The cloth was being cut into slings and bandages. Two boys carried a carved door between them towards the infirmary. A woman from the laundry walked behind, warning them that if they damaged the painted side she would personally return it to the king’s bedroom while he was asleep.

The king stepped aside to let them pass.

“That is not my door,” he said.

The laundry woman looked at the painted peacocks.

“It will be if they damage it.”

She continued towards the stairs.

Shuka glanced at the king.

“Your authority remains complete.”

“It has always weakened near the laundry.”

The council chamber smelled of damp maps, lamp smoke and the cardamom someone had spilled upon the carpet during an argument months earlier. Three walls were covered in painted plans of the city. The fourth opened through stone screens towards the harbour, where the sea breathed against the quay with the indifferent patience of something that expected to outlive every flag.

The great council table had been built for thirty-two men and the space required by their opinions.

The king ordered it removed.

Four servants stared at him.

“Where?” one asked.

“The passage.”

“It will block the passage.”

“Then everyone who encounters it will understand the evening.”

They lifted one end. The table did not move.

The king inspected the carved legs.

“It comes apart at the centre,” he said.

The senior servant shook his head.

“It has never come apart.”

“That is different from not coming apart.”

The king knelt with difficulty and reached beneath the central rail. His fingers found two wooden pins concealed behind a decorative lion. He pushed the first loose with the black enemy arrowhead from his belt.

The servant crouched beside him.

“How did you know?”

“I designed the room.”

“Why hide the pins?”

“Because the carpenter believed visible joints offended dignity.”

The second pin released. The table separated into two sections.

“The carpenter was right,” the servant said.

The king looked at the enormous pieces occupying most of the floor.

“Find him and promote him.”

“He died eleven years ago.”

“Then his judgement remains protected from administration.”

They carried the halves into the passage.

The king had mats placed in a circle upon the bare stone floor. In the centre he spread the salt-road map, the harbour chart and a hastily drawn plan of the northern ditch. He set clay cups at the corners to stop the papers curling in the sea air.

When the council assembled, its ten members did not resemble a war council.

The chamberlain remained among them. Either he had failed to find someone quieter or had decided the requirement was unreasonable.

The northern captain came in armour blackened at one shoulder. The captain of the inner guard wore a pale house robe beneath her breastplate and had braided her hair while walking. The water-master smelled faintly of lamp oil despite having washed twice. The rope-maker carried three different cords around one arm because no one had told him whether the summons concerned rigging, wells or executions.

The harbour engineer arrived barefoot. The chief cook brought a bowl of rice porridge and placed it in front of the king without asking permission. The physician came with her tray, saw that he was standing and pointed at the nearest mat.

He sat.

The old temple reader entered last, wrapped in a cotton cloth the colour of unpolished bone. His white hair stood out on one side where sleep had refused negotiation.

He looked around the circle.

“This is either the end of rank,” he said, “or the end of the kingdom.”

“We are attempting to keep one,” the king replied.

The chamberlain began introducing everyone.

The king stopped him.

“They know who I am.”

The rope-maker scratched his cheek.

“I have seen you at festivals.”

“Then we have both endured something.”

The chief cook pushed the porridge closer.

“Eat.”

The king took the bowl.

No herald announced the beginning. No priest blessed the maps. The northern wall struck the earth beneath them, and the council began because there was no longer time to pretend that ceremony caused decisions.

No one reached for the maps.

The rope-maker looked towards the stone screens as another impact moved through the room. A little plaster fell from the lintel and broke upon the floor between his knees.

“Majesty,” he said, “if my family should leave the western quarter, I would rather tell them before the road closes.”

The harbour engineer glanced at him, then at the king.

“The fishing boats can carry perhaps two thousand before the tide turns,” she said. “More if we abandon water and provisions.”

“To where?” the inner-guard captain asked.

“Away,” the harbour engineer said.

“That is not a place,” the inner-guard captain replied.

“It is sometimes enough of one,” said the engineer.

The chief cook kept her hand upon the porridge bowl.

“When the chamberlain woke me,” she said, “I assumed the generals had run out of answers.”

“They have not,” the king replied.

“Then why are we here?”

The cook’s question released the others.

The water-master asked whether the palace records were being packed. The northern captain wanted to know which gate had been reserved for civilian escape. The harbour engineer said the southern quay must be protected from crowds before panic made every boat useless. Even the physician, who feared very little that could be treated with boiled instruments, looked towards the ceiling when the wall was struck again.

The old temple reader listened until they stopped.

“People beyond the eastern market are saying the prince is Vishnu,” he said. “Not merely favoured by him. Vishnu himself.”

The king said nothing.

“They have seen the fire cross the sky,” the old man continued. “They know a road appeared upon the sea. Some are asking whether we are defending the wrong man from the right god.”

The northern captain’s jaw tightened.

“Those people are frightened.”

“Frightened people are still people,” the temple reader said. “And they are usually the first citizens of the next kingdom.”

The ten members of the council looked at the king.

Shuka understood what they believed the summons meant. Important men had failed. The enemy was at the wall. The king had gathered practical people not to save the city, but to divide what could still be carried from it.

The king placed the porridge bowl beside the map.

“I did not call you here because no one is in control,” he said. “I called you because the people in control have been asking the same questions of the same men and congratulating one another upon receiving the same answers.”

He touched the harbour chart.

“The northern prince crossed the sea because a fisherman was permitted to correct an officer’s map. A rope-maker contradicted an engineer. A child who knew the reefs moved a military marker. They did not become gods. They opened doors between rooms of knowledge.”

The king looked around the circle.

“We have spent generations building those rooms. We have harbours that outlive storms, drains beneath streets their army cannot name, kilns hotter than their fire baskets, storehouses measured for famine, women commanding gates, physicians who can order their king to sit and remain employed afterwards.”

The physician folded her arms.

“For the present.”

“For the present,” the king agreed.

His voice never rose. That made the certainty inside it harder to dismiss.

“They are inventive,” he continued. “Good. Let us respect that. But do not mistake one remarkable road for a greater civilisation. They brought timber, rope and courage to an island we have studied for generations. We possess all three, and the city beneath our feet.”

The rope-maker looked down at the maps.

The king leaned towards him.

“Your family will not flee the western quarter tonight. Not because I forbid it. Because by the time you return, you will know how we are going to break the road at the northern wall.”

“You are certain?” the man asked.

“No,” the king replied. “Certainty is what frightened people call a rumour that comforts them. I am telling you something more useful: they can be beaten.”

The harbour engineer stopped looking towards the sea.

The northern captain lowered herself closer to the map. The water-master picked up a clay cup and placed it over the poisoned cistern. Even the old temple reader, who had made a profession of distrusting royal confidence, turned the harbour chart towards the light.

Shuka felt it happen inside the room. The ten had entered believing they had been summoned to manage an ending. Now they began to examine the night as though it still contained choices.

He knew the ending fear had been whispering throughout the palace. He knew which man every rumour claimed the gods intended to survive it. Yet for one unreasonable moment, looking at the heads bent around the king, Shuka wondered whether dawn might find the bridge broken, the northern standards retreating and every confident prediction proved mistaken.

The king looked at the harbour engineer.

“Now,” he said, “tell me how their road can be broken.”

---

The harbour engineer studied the ditch plan.

“Burn the timber.”

“They expect fire,” the king said.

“Water, then,” the engineer replied.

“We have opened the dye works,” the king said.

“You opened one channel,” the water-master said. “There are three.”

The king looked at him.

“The second passes beneath the potters’ quarter,” the king said.

“The potters are already evacuated,” the water-master replied.

“The kilns are not.”

The water-master moved a clay cup upon the map.

“Open the second channel and the cold water strikes hot brick. The kilns crack. Half the street falls into the lower drain.”

“Taking their road with it,” the harbour engineer said.

“And twelve houses,” the inner-guard captain added.

“Eleven,” said the water-master. “One burned this afternoon.”

The captain looked at him.

“That is not an improvement.”

The king ate one spoonful of porridge.

It had no salt.

The chief cook watched his face.

“Salt is being kept for wounds and preserved grain,” she said.

“I made no complaint.”

“Your left eyebrow did.”

The king lowered it.

He asked the northern captain how many defenders remained between the ditch and the inner gate.

“Six hundred able to stand,” she said. “Two hundred more who will claim they can.”

“And the houses?”

“Empty if the lists are accurate.”

The physician spoke without looking up from the bandage she was inspecting through the king’s torn cloth.

“The lists are not accurate.”

The captain frowned.

“My officers cleared every marked house.”

“People do not always live where officers mark them,” the physician replied. “There are workers sleeping behind three of the kilns. A widow beneath the blue stair refuses to leave without six jars containing her husband.”

The king looked at the chamberlain.

“Move them.”

“The workers or the husband?” the chamberlain asked.

“Both,” the king said.

“The husband may object to the handling,” the chamberlain said.

“He has had several years to become flexible,” the king replied.

The chamberlain rose.

The king caught his wrist.

“Not you. Send someone. I need your memory.”

The chamberlain sat again and gave the order to a waiting runner.

The old temple reader watched the exchange.

“You have summoned ten advisers,” he said, “and already you are using one as furniture.”

“He is excellent furniture.”

“I have drawers,” the chamberlain said.

No one laughed for long. Another impact moved through the floor.

The cups upon the maps shifted.

The king placed his porridge aside.

“Open the second channel after the last civilian is clear,” he said. “Not before.”

The northern captain nodded.

“The road will sink,” she said. “They will build it again.”

“Yes.”

“Then we gain an hour.”

“An hour is not a small object tonight.”

The king looked at the rope-maker.

“How do they lift the frames?”

The man unwound the thickest cord from his arm.

“Four teams. Perhaps six. The weight is carried upon cross-lines so one broken rope does not release the frame.”

“You have seen them?”

“From the wall.”

“Can you reproduce their knots?”

The rope-maker made one upon his knee.

“This one. It tightens under load but can be released from the dry end.”

The king took the knot and turned it between his fingers.

“Can it be made to appear secure while failing under strain?”

The rope-maker’s expression changed.

“Yes.”

“How quickly?” the king asked.

“If I have people who know rope,” the rope-maker replied.

“You have sailors,” the king said.

“Sailors know how not to die,” the man said. “That is different from knowing rope.”

The harbour engineer looked offended.

“It is closely related.”

The rope-maker ignored her.

“Give me the women from the western sail shed,” he said. “And the old men who make temple cords.”

“Take them,” the king replied.

The temple reader cleared his throat.

“The temple cords belong to the gods.”

“The gods may submit a claim after dawn.”

The old man considered this.

“With interest.”

“Naturally.”

The king placed the deceptive knot upon the northern ditch.

The plan began growing around it.

They would allow the enemy to recover the first sunken frame. Island soldiers wearing captured cloth would help drag replacement ropes across the mud. The false ropes would hold until loaded. When the second layer of rubble arrived, the lines would release and pull two adjoining frames sideways.

The northern captain wanted archers on the roofs.

The physician asked where the wounded would fall.

The chief cook asked which streets must remain clear for water carts.

The harbour engineer shifted the flood timing by twenty breaths so the strongest surge would arrive after the frame tilted rather than before. The water-master objected, calculated again with pebbles and admitted she was right.

The king moved among them without rising.

One moment he was testing the rope against his own wrist. The next he was asking how many rice carts could be emptied without losing tomorrow’s food. He corrected the slope drawn upon the northern map, remembered that a drain beneath the blue stair had been narrowed during a fever year, and asked whether lime from the southern store could make wet stone too slippery to climb.

The physician forced him to finish the porridge while he calculated how long frightened men required to recognise a false signal.

Shuka watched the faces around the circle change.

At first they addressed the crown. Then they addressed the builder who remembered the drains. Then the quartermaster who knew the weight of grain, the musician who heard timing inside hammer blows, the scholar who asked what the enemy believed, and the older brother who touched the clasp beneath his robe whenever someone called a life expendable.

The people around him appeared to know each of these men.

Not equally.

The chief cook treated the king as a difficult patient who happened to own the kitchens. The northern captain watched the tactician. The harbour engineer argued with the builder. The temple reader seemed to address a former pupil who had become powerful without becoming correct. The chamberlain listened for all of them and prepared to repair whatever they forgot.

Shuka understood why some men left the king’s presence convinced they had met genius while others left certain they had survived ten separate arguments.

The king looked up.

“You are staring.”

“I am counting.”

“Our enemies are at the wall and you have discovered arithmetic.”

“It seemed a suitable night.”

The king returned to the map.

“Count the carts instead.”

---

The musician emerged because the enemy used drums.

The northern captain described three signals heard beyond the wall: a slow sequence that moved shields forward, a pair of rapid beats that brought stone teams, and one long roll after which the workers withdrew.

“First we learn what every rhythm commands,” the king said. “Then we reproduce it from our wall.”

The captain looked at him.

“You want to imitate their signals?”

“Exactly. When their officers call workers back, our drums will tell them to advance. When the road is weakest, we will summon their stone carts onto it. We will not merely make noise. We will issue orders to their army.”

The captain looked towards the distant drums.

“They may recognise the difference.”

“Then we understand the codes before we counterfeit them,” the king said. “Not approximately. Stroke for stroke.”

The king tapped the patterns upon the empty porridge bowl.

“Again,” he said.

The captain repeated them with her knuckles.

The king listened.

“The second is not two beats.”

“I heard two.”

“You heard two accents. There are six strokes.”

He tapped them more softly: three close notes, a space, then three more.

The chief cook tilted her head.

“The kitchen uses that rhythm when the rice must be turned.”

Everyone looked at her.

“What?” she asked. “Rice also requires armies.”

The king repeated the rhythm against the bowl. Beneath it he added another pulse with his heel.

Shuka recognised the structure of a composition he had heard years earlier in the autumn court. The melody had been played upon the veena while dancers crossed in opposing lines, each appearing to follow a different beat until the patterns met.

“Their commands sit inside the marching rhythm,” the king said. “The workers continue moving while the officers change the instruction.”

He looked at the chamberlain.

“Bring the drum master.”

The chamberlain hesitated.

“He left with your brother.”

Silence touched the circle.

The king’s fingers stopped upon the bowl.

Kumbhakarna had asked for drums so the frightened men following him would hear order behind them.

The king pressed his thumb against the rim.

“His apprentice, then.”

“She is fourteen,” the chamberlain said.

“Can she hold six strokes?”

The chief cook answered.

“She can hold seven while stealing mango pickle.”

“Bring her.”

The girl arrived carrying two drumsticks and the expression of someone expecting punishment for the mangoes.

The king made her repeat the enemy rhythms.

She learned them after hearing each twice.

“Now reverse the withdrawal and stone signals,” he said.

The apprentice frowned.

“That will sound wrong.”

“Only to a musician.”

“It will sound wrong to anyone with ears.”

The king gave her the empty bowl.

“Improve it.”

She sat at the edge of the circle, tapping possibilities against the clay. The king listened, corrected one interval and then accepted her correction of his correction.

No one announced that a fourteen-year-old kitchen thief had joined the war council.

There was no time to make the event unusual.

---

The painted cloth arrived before the plan was complete.

Two guards carried it between them, wet with mud and darkened along one edge by fire. It had been taken from an enemy signalling post abandoned when the dye water rose.

“We thought the markings might matter,” one guard said.

“Where was it hanging?” the temple reader asked.

“Above their grain station. The workers touched the prince’s painted feet before receiving food.”

The old man looked at the cloth without approaching it.

“It is not a signal,” he said. “It is propaganda.”

The chief cook frowned.

“A picture beside the grain?”

“The grain feeds the body,” the temple reader replied. “The picture tells the body what it is permitted to die for.”

The inner-guard captain crouched beside one blackened corner.

“Then we should make our own. Paint the northern prince standing upon his bridge with a hundred dead workers holding it above the water.”

“And his shining arrow entering the cistern,” the water-master added.

The physician looked at the ten-headed shadow visible through the folded cloth.

“Show the sister as she was before they cut her. Put the two images beside one another.”

The chamberlain was already considering logistics.

“The palace painters are dressing wounds in the eastern hall. The sign painters from the market may still be available.”

Suggestions crossed the room. Paint the buried labourers. Paint the poisoned child. Paint the forest clans as men and ask why their prince required them to wear animals. Several members of the council warmed to the idea. A false story had reached their wall; they wanted to answer it with a more useful one.

The king listened without joining them.

When the voices stopped, he looked at the northern map.

“A banner may persuade a frightened man to walk onto a sound road,” he said. “It will not prevent a false rope from breaking beneath him.”

The captain waited.

“Do we paint it?” she asked.

“After the road is broken. If we answer their picture before their army, our people will think we are defending ourselves from cloth.”

The king gestured to the guards.

“Open it. Let us see the lie they needed.”

The northern captain cleared a space.

The cloth was spread across the floor.

At first Shuka saw colour: red flame, blue water, yellow circles and the black shapes of men. Then the figures arranged themselves into a story.

On the right stood the northern prince, straight and golden beneath a white halo. His bow curved above him like the edge of the moon. His brother stood slightly behind, sword lifted in loyal anger. Between them was the pale figure of the stolen woman, modestly veiled and surrounded by flowering branches.

On the left stood a creature with ten heads.

Each face bore the same heavy brows, tusk-like teeth and blood-red eyes. Twenty arms held swords, hooks, clubs and cups that appeared to contain either wine or blood. Beneath its feet lay broken temples and small human bodies painted without faces.

Behind the creature sprawled a giant beside bowls of food. His stomach rose like a hill. His eyes were closed. A line of tiny soldiers beat drums against his ears.

Near the bottom, a woman with green skin reached towards the northern prince. Her breasts sagged to her waist. Her belly protruded. Curved teeth emerged from a mouth painted too large for her face. Blood ran from the place where her nose had been.

The king did not move.

The physician knelt beside the cloth.

“That is meant to be your sister,” she said.

“Yes.”

“They have made the wound accurate.”

“Cruelty often preserves the part that excuses it.”

The king crouched slowly. The bandage pulled at his hip, but he ignored it.

His fingers hovered above Kumbhakarna’s painted body without touching the cloth.

The giant’s mouth had been left open. A stream of food entered it from the hands of servants. Around his neck, the painter had placed a necklace made from human skulls.

The king’s thumb found the clasp beneath his robe.

“He fed his men,” he said.

No one answered.

“The rice was for the men.”

The chamberlain lowered his eyes.

The king moved to the ten-headed creature.

One painted face laughed. One snarled. One appeared drunk. Another licked its teeth. Though all ten were intended to be the same monster, the painter had given each a different expression.

The old temple reader leaned over the king’s shoulder.

“The fourth resembles you when the treasury report is late.”

The chamberlain examined it.

“The seventh is closer.”

The king looked at both men.

“We are discussing enemy propaganda.”

“It would be discourteous not to recognise the effort,” the temple reader said.

The rope-maker pointed at the upper-left face.

“That one looks constipated.”

The physician sat back upon her heels.

“The artist understands kings.”

Laughter moved around the circle.

It did not erase the ugliness. It made it possible to remain in the room with it.

The king studied the image again.

“Why ten?” Shuka asked.

The temple reader answered first.

“Old court praise. Ten minds, ten disciplines, ten men’s learning in one body. Poets have poor restraint.”

The chamberlain nodded.

“Your father used it when the king was young.”

“My father used it when he wanted other people to tolerate me,” the king said.

The old reader smiled.

“We needed considerable poetry.”

Shuka looked from the cloth to the people seated around the maps.

“And now they have made the minds into heads.”

“Minds are difficult to paint,” the physician said.

The king traced the air above the central face.

“A man who builds drains, writes music, studies medicine and commands an army is inconvenient. A creature born with ten heads requires no explanation.”

“Nor sympathy,” Shuka said.

“Especially not sympathy.”

The king looked at the painted northern prince.

The white circle behind his head had been edged with powdered shell, making it catch the lamplight.

“They have corrected the parasol,” he said.

Shuka remembered the wind striking the real prince behind the ear.

“It was less dignified on the beach.”

The king almost smiled.

Then his gaze returned to his sister’s painted teeth.

“Where was this displayed?” he asked the guards.

“Above their grain station.”

“For soldiers?”

“And the workers, Majesty. They touched the prince’s feet before collecting food.”

The king rose.

He did not order the cloth burned.

“Hang it on the wall,” he said.

The chamberlain looked at him.

“Here?”

“Above the maps.”

“Majesty, it depicts you with—”

“I can count.”

The guards fixed the cloth to the eastern wall.

The ten-headed figure looked down upon the ten people seated in council.

The difference between them occupied the room.

---

The counterattack began with soup carts.

The chief cook insisted that empty grain wagons would attract attention, while kitchen carts moved through every quarter without being questioned. Beneath covered pots, the rope-maker’s women carried false lines towards the northern ditch. The pots above contained actual lentil soup because the cook refused to waste a journey.

At the second gate, soldiers drank while receiving instructions on how to lose a road.

The apprentice drummer climbed the potters’ tower with two boys carrying the smallest festival drum. The enemy could see the tower from the ditch, so the instrument was hidden behind damp cloth.

The water-master waited beside the second channel with his hands upon the release lever.

The king took position in the old records room overlooking the northern street. It was the only chamber from which he could see both the tower signal and the bend where the lower drain entered the ditch.

The physician found him there.

“You should be lying down.”

“The enemy has shown little interest in my schedule.”

She touched the bandage through his robe.

“If the stitches open again, I will close the wound with sailmaker’s rope.”

“Use the enemy knot. Apparently it is excellent.”

The physician remained beside him.

“Why are you still here?” he asked.

“I am one of the ten people.”

“Your part is finished.”

“That is what kings say immediately before creating patients.”

Shuka entered carrying the captured animal standards.

There were three: a red monkey, a black bear and a long-beaked white bird. The king’s scouts had taken them from the abandoned signal post with the painted cloth.

The king examined the poles.

“The monkey moves first,” he said. “The bear follows after the false withdrawal. Keep the bird hidden unless their archers turn.”

Shuka distributed the standards to the inner-guard captain and two forest-born island scouts. In darkness and smoke, colour and movement mattered more than the face beneath the banner.

“If they recognise us?” the captain asked.

“Then run convincingly,” the king replied.

“In which direction?”

“Whichever preserves your professional future.”

She left with the standards.

The king looked towards the northern ditch.

Enemy workers had rebuilt half the sunken approach. Their shields overlapped like scales. Behind them waited carts of stone and timber. Fire baskets illuminated the pale mud beneath their feet.

The first false drum signal sounded.

It was imperfect.

The apprentice placed the middle stroke too close to the first. The king heard it and winced.

The physician noticed.

“Will it fail?”

“Not unless their commander teaches rhythm.”

Across the ditch, the red monkey standard lifted.

Enemy workers moved forward.

The island scout beneath the captured banner walked openly along the broken road and waved the stone crews towards the left-hand frame. The men obeyed because the signal arrived in the correct colour at the expected moment.

Kitchen carts rolled to the inner edge. The rope-maker’s teams threw replacement lines across the mud. Enemy hands caught them.

For several breaths, both armies held the same rope.

The king watched without blinking.

The false lines tightened.

They held.

Stone baskets moved forward.

The first frame sank deeper. Men shouted and added more rubble. The second false drum sequence crossed the field.

The black bear standard rose.

The water-master pulled the lever.

Nothing happened.

Shuka looked at the king.

The king continued watching the drain.

Still nothing.

The road filled with enemy workers. Carts crowded behind them. The false ropes stretched beneath the weight.

“The channel is blocked,” Shuka said.

“Wait.”

“If it does not open—”

“Wait.”

The king’s voice belonged to the musician now. He was counting a rhythm no one else could hear: the time water required to move beneath a quarter, strike a narrowed drain and rise behind the cracked kilns.

The apprentice’s drum sounded the withdrawal.

The king’s scouts turned and ran.

Enemy stone teams pushed forward, believing the defenders had broken.

Then the street moved.

The first kiln collapsed inward with a deep ceramic groan. Its brick floor disappeared into the drain. Water burst through the opening, carrying purple dye, ash, broken pots and the six jars containing the widow’s husband.

One jar struck an enemy shield and broke.

The king closed his eyes briefly.

“He objected to the handling,” Shuka said.

“I shall apologise if we meet.”

The flood struck the ditch.

The false ropes tightened under the load and released exactly as the rope-maker had promised. Two timber frames turned sideways. The stone carts behind them rolled into the opening. Men fell. Shields separated. Purple water climbed around their bodies.

The city’s archers rose from the roofs.

The king lifted one hand.

They waited.

Enemy workers struggled in the mud. Some had lost their weapons. Others dragged companions towards the dry edge.

“Now?” the northern captain shouted from below.

The king looked at the painted cloth visible through the records-room door. The northern prince stood in light while the ten-headed monster crushed faceless bodies.

“Target the carts and ropes,” he called. “Not the men in the water.”

The captain stared up at him.

“They will rebuild.”

“Then we will break it again.”

“Majesty—”

“Carts and ropes.”

The order travelled.

Arrows fell upon wheels, harnesses and timber stacks. Fire caught in the dry fibre behind the road. Enemy soldiers abandoned the trapped frames and pulled back beyond the ditch.

The defenders cheered.

The sound ran along the wall, through the potters’ quarter and into the palace. People who could not see the battle heard it and added their voices. For the first time that night, the city made a sound that was not alarm, grief or instruction.

Women leaned from infirmary windows and struck bowls with spoons. Wounded men lifted themselves from borrowed doors to hear more clearly. In the lower court, children who had spent the night learning the different meanings of bells heard adults shouting with joy and began shouting before anyone explained why.

The northern captain looked up at the records-room window.

“We broke it!” she called.

The rope-maker’s women repeated the words from the street. The cry travelled faster than an order.

**We broke it.**

For several minutes, the enemy was not an advancing certainty. It was an army standing on the wrong side of a ruined road.

Shuka saw the white halo disappear behind smoke. He saw animal standards withdrawing from the ditch. He had come into the council expecting to help an intelligent king lose with dignity. Now he found himself searching the darkness for signs of a larger retreat.

Perhaps the ending fear had rehearsed was not an ending at all.

Perhaps this was not the story he thought he had returned to tell.

The king did not join them.

He watched the retreating standards.

The enemy had been surprised only once.

The next attempt would not trust captured colours or familiar drums.

But the road lay broken. The northern ditch was open again. The city had purchased an hour, perhaps two.

The physician touched his sleeve.

Blood had reached the edge of his robe.

“Your stitches,” she said.

“They have served the kingdom.”

“They have served twelve minutes.”

She pushed him into the nearest chair.

Below, soldiers were still cheering while she opened his bandage and told him exactly what she thought of kings, wounds and decorative courage.

He listened with the patience of a man receiving praise.

---

The council returned to the map room beneath the painted monster.

The chief cook brought salted water, having apparently reconsidered the allocation. The apprentice drummer received half a mango from somewhere no one investigated. The rope-maker sat with both hands shaking now that they were no longer required to be steady.

The northern captain placed the red monkey standard upon the floor.

“They will not follow it again,” she said.

“No,” the king replied.

“Then we have delayed them.”

“Yes,” the king said.

“Not defeated them.”

“No,” he replied.

The room settled around the word.

Shuka looked at the ten-headed figure above them. Its painter had made victory simple. Light stood upon one side. Darkness stood upon the other. No drains, kitchens, cousins, errors, compromises or frightened children occupied the space between.

“If we are what you say we are,” Shuka asked, “why are they winning?”

The king looked up from the map.

“What do I say we are?”

“Older. Richer. A kingdom of laws. A place where a physician may insult her king and remain in possession of her head.”

The physician tied the last knot in his new bandage.

“For the present.”

Shuka continued.

“You say we are a civilisation. They arrived beneath animal flags. Why are they at our wall?”

The king leaned back.

“Because civilisation is not armour.”

“Then they are better than us.”

“At crossing water? Yes. At joining knowledge we kept separated? Yes. At making exhausted men believe their deaths serve something larger than themselves? Considerably.”

Shuka looked around the council.

“And we are better?”

“At some things.”

“Which of us is good?”

The king’s eyes moved to the painted sister with her green skin and curved teeth.

“Good and evil belong to acts,” he said. “Armies merely collect enough acts to argue about the total.”

Shuka waited.

The king placed one hand upon the map.

“We built hospitals and took a woman from her home. They crossed the sea through human genius and buried living men beneath their road. My sister offered affection. They answered with steel. I answered their cruelty with another cruelty and named it justice.”

“Then what are we defending?” Shuka asked.

“People.”

The answer came without hesitation.

“Not innocence,” the king continued. “People. The physician who contradicts me. The cook who rations my salt. The old woman who carries her husband through a flood six jars at a time. Children who believe squirrels understand bridgework. Men who will curse my decisions and still expect the drains to function tomorrow.”

The water-master looked down at his hands.

“And if they win?” Shuka asked.

The king turned towards the painted cloth.

“Then their cruelties will become difficult decisions. Ours will become our nature.”

Shuka pointed at the ten heads.

“Is that our nature?”

“No,” the king said. “That is their first draft.”

“And the last?”

The king returned his attention to the map.

“That depends upon whether we permit them to finish it.”

The room was quiet.

Then the old temple reader spoke.

“You have still taken a woman from her home.”

No one looked at him.

He continued.

“Breaking their road does not answer that.”

The king’s face hardened.

“Nor does cutting my sister answer desire.”

“No.”

“Then we return to the beginning.”

“Only because you prefer the beginning to the next act,” the old man said.

The northern captain shifted upon her mat. The rope-maker studied the cords around his wrist. The physician remained beside the king, close enough to intervene if anger became movement.

The king spoke carefully.

“What would you have me do?”

“Offer terms.”

The word entered the room more dangerously than an accusation.

“They demand surrender,” the northern captain said.

“Then offer something that requires them to reveal whether they want the woman or the kingdom,” the temple reader replied.

The king looked at the map of the northern camp.

Shuka saw another face arrive—not upon his body, but behind his eyes. The tactician withdrew. The judge considered language. The brother remembered blood. The king measured what pride could surrender without appearing to kneel.

“Transfer her to a neutral southern court,” he said.

The temple reader waited.

“Under her own guard,” the king continued. “She may speak there without either army present. Her account will be recorded. My sister’s mutilation will be heard in the same proceeding.”

“And the armies?” the harbour engineer asked.

“Withdraw beyond bow range during the inquiry. Their forest companies may return across the bridge without pursuit.”

The inner-guard captain studied him.

“Will the guest agree to leave?”

The king looked towards the garden quarter.

“That question should have been asked sooner.”

The physician folded the bloodied linen upon her tray.

“Will you accept her answer?”

The king did not reply immediately.

Above him, ten painted mouths offered ten easier answers.

“Yes,” he said.

The old temple reader watched him.

“Even if she chooses the northern prince?”

The king’s hand closed over Kumbhakarna’s clasp beneath his robe.

“She has always chosen him.”

That was not the same answer.

Everyone heard the difference.

The king looked at Shuka.

“You will carry the terms.”

Shuka’s injured foot throbbed at the thought of the road beyond the wall.

“They know my face.”

“Then they will recognise that I sent someone whose return matters to me.”

“Do I?”

The king’s expression did not change.

“Do not use wartime uncertainty to request affection.”

“It seemed efficient.”

“You matter sufficiently to be irritating.”

Shuka bowed from where he sat.

“I accept the honour.”

The chamberlain began preparing the formal tablet. The temple reader objected to the opening phrase. The northern captain demanded a safe-conduct signal. The rope-maker offered white cord. The physician said white cloth would be easier to see and removed a clean strip from the king’s unused bandages before he could object.

The council divided into arguments.

The king listened to all of them.

Outside, the enemy began cutting new timber.

---

Before Shuka left, he found the king alone beneath the painted cloth.

The ten advisers had dispersed into the city. The physician went to the infirmary. The captains returned to the wall and inner gate. The water-master descended to the drains. The cook reclaimed her carts. The rope-maker carried away the captured standards to study their lashings. The temple reader went to tell the guest that kings had finally decided to ask what she wanted.

The chamberlain remained in the passage, pretending not to guard the door.

The king stood before the ten-headed creature.

In one hand he held the terms of negotiation. In the other he held the enemy arrowhead he had used to open the council table.

“Which one are you?” Shuka asked.

The king did not turn.

“According to the painter?”

“According to you.”

The king examined the faces.

“Tonight?”

“Tonight.”

“The one who has run out of people to blame.”

Shuka moved beside him.

The central painted face had been given a crown of bone. Fire surrounded it. At its feet, the small bodies were still without features.

“Your people also say you have ten minds,” Shuka said.

“My people have watched me change my decision nine times.”

“They mean it as praise.”

“That is because they do not attend the meetings.”

Shuka looked at the council mats. Each retained the shape of the person who had occupied it. The cook’s bowl remained near the northern map. A strand of white temple cord lay beside the rope-maker’s place. One of the physician’s needles caught the lamplight where it had rolled beneath a cup.

“Were all ten minds yours?” he asked.

The king finally turned.

“No.”

He looked around the empty circle.

“That is what I failed to understand.”

From the northern quarter came the first testing beats of an enemy drum.

The king listened.

This time the pattern was different.

The prince had learned too.

Shuka tucked the peace tablet inside his robe. The white strip of bandage hung from his staff.

“Do you believe he will accept?”

“I believe he will understand the offer,” the king said.

“That is not what I asked,” Shuka replied.

The king looked towards the east, where the darkness had thinned another shade.

“No,” he said.

“Then why send me?”

“Because if he refuses a road away from war, I want the refusal witnessed,” the king said.

“For history?” Shuka asked.

“For me,” the king said.

The king took the painted cloth from the wall.

Shuka thought he intended to tear it. Instead, he folded it carefully: the halo inward, the sleeping giant inward, the wounded sister inward, until only the central face of the ten-headed creature remained visible.

He placed it upon the council table’s empty space on the floor.

“Take this also,” he said.

“To the northern prince?”

“No. Bring it back.”

Shuka frowned.

“Why?”

The king touched one painted head with the back of his finger.

“I want to see whether they improve the likeness after meeting me.”

The northern drums began again.

Ten voices had entered the room to tell the king he was wrong. They were leaving with ten parts of a decision to carry through the waking city.

The enemy painting remained behind.

It had ten heads and not one thought among them.
