# Chapter Six

## A Bridge Across the Water

Beyond the western screen, the last light had left the sea. Servants were lighting the palace lamps again. Someone had cleaned and refilled the table lamp while the king was at the wall; fresh wicks burned above the oil, and the blackened cotton from the previous night lay in a shallow dish beside it.

The palace floor moved beneath them.

It was not a large movement. The water in the silver cup trembled. A brass lamp rang once against its chain. Dust loosened from the painted beam above the western window and fell upon the black table, settling over the unsigned death order, the salt-road map and the broken clasp that had belonged to Kumbhakarna.

Then the sound arrived.

Wood struck stone somewhere beyond the northern wall. The blow travelled through streets, foundations and buried drains before reaching the room as a low, patient thunder. It was followed by the cheering of several thousand men who could not yet be seen.

Shuka rose too quickly. His injured foot reminded him of itself, and he caught the edge of the table.

The king did not move.

He still held his brother’s clasp inside his closed hand.

The floor shook again.

This time a thin crack crossed the cup.

Water gathered along the silver like sweat.

“That is not a ram,” Shuka said.

“No,” the king replied.

“A tower?”

“Too low.”

The king opened his hand and set the clasp beside the dried pomegranate. The metal had marked his palm with a red crescent.

“They are making a road to the wall,” he said.

Before Shuka could ask how a road could make the floor move, the curtain was pushed aside.

The physician entered carrying a shallow bronze tray. She had changed her bloodied outer cloth but not her expression. Three needles lay upon the tray beside folded linen, a clay jar of resin and the short black head of an enemy arrow.

The old chamberlain followed with a basin of hot water. A young servant came behind him with clean towels, realised that she had entered a room containing a grieving king and attempted to become part of the curtain.

The physician pointed to the chair.

“Sit.”

The king looked towards the northern window.

“The enemy is at the wall.”

“Your blood is already inside it,” she said. “Sit.”

Shuka expected resistance. The chamberlain expected resistance. The servant behind the curtain appeared to expect execution.

The king sat.

“You have become very commanding,” he said.

“You have become very perforated.”

The chamberlain placed the basin down and looked at the physician with something close to gratitude.

She cut through the bandage above the king’s hip. The linen had dried to the wound. When she pulled it free, his fingers tightened upon the chair, but he made no sound.

The physician cleaned the cut with hot water that smelled of neem leaves and boiled iron. Smoke from the northern quarter entered through the screens. It mixed with the sharp resin upon her tray, the bruised sweetness of the old pomegranate and the faint sourness of grief upon men who had not changed their clothes.

Wood struck stone again.

The physician did not look up.

“They say the northern prince has a circle of light behind his head,” she said.

The king turned towards her.

She pressed clean cloth into the wound.

“Who says?” he asked.

“Three of our wounded. Two of theirs. A potter from the eastern gate. One woman who claims she saw him from the roof of a grain store, although she cannot recognise her own husband from the same distance.”

“Then the matter is settled.”

“The husband is unusually forgettable,” the physician said.

Shuka looked from one to the other.

The physician selected a needle.

“They say his arrows turn in the air,” she continued. “They say he can strike a bird by watching its reflection in water. They say some of his arrows carry fire without being consumed by it. They say the sea refused him once, and that he stood before it until the sea reconsidered.”

The king’s mouth tightened.

“Has the sea provided a statement?”

“Not to the infirmary.”

She pushed the needle through his skin.

The king inhaled through his nose.

“There is more,” the physician said.

“There is always more when the first part is foolish.”

“They say he is Vishnu born as a man.”

The humour left the room.

The physician drew the thread through. Beyond the window, another blow landed against the northern earthworks.

The king watched her hands.

They were small, brown and entirely steady. Those hands had opened infected wounds, turned children in difficult births and once removed a fish bone from the chamberlain’s throat while he tried to give instructions. She believed in boiled instruments, measured doses and asking where the pain began. She had no patience for curses, auspicious sneezes or ministers who blamed fever upon disobedient planets.

That she had carried this rumour into his room gave it a weight it had not possessed in anyone else’s mouth.

“And you?” the king asked. “What do you say?”

The physician tied the first stitch.

“I say infection does not care who a man’s father is.”

“That was not my question.”

“It was the answer you needed.”

She reached for more thread.

The king waited.

The physician finally met his eyes.

“I do not believe a god requires engineers,” she said. “But I have treated men who saw that army cross the water. Each tells the story differently. None can explain it without eventually using the word impossible.”

The king looked towards Shuka.

It was not accusation yet.

That made it worse.

On the narrow shelf beside the western screen stood a plain black stone in a bronze bowl. It had no jewelled crown, no silver eyes and no priest assigned to it. A line of old ash crossed its surface. Beside it, a lamp had burned itself dry.

The king rose before the physician had finished tying the second stitch.

She caught the thread.

“Sit down.”

He lifted the lamp from the shelf.

“If I sit whenever you command it, the court will misunderstand the order of government.”

“The court has misunderstood it for years.”

He poured a little sesame oil into the lamp. His hand shook once. Whether from the wound or the clasp he had just released, Shuka could not tell.

The king rolled a fresh cotton wick between his fingers. He placed it in the oil, touched it to the nearest flame and returned the small light to the shelf.

He did not bow deeply. He did not speak a prayer. He simply wiped yesterday’s ash from the black stone with the edge of his own cloth and poured a spoonful of water across it.

The water ran into the bronze bowl.

The physician watched him.

“They say Vishnu has come as a man,” she repeated more quietly.

The king adjusted the wick.

“Gods who announce themselves through frightened soldiers,” he said, “usually belong to the soldiers.”

“Perhaps.”

“You sound unconvinced.”

“I am a physician. Uncertainty is the only honest room in which I work.”

He returned to the chair.

She resumed stitching.

“I served a god for half my life,” the king said. “The prince crosses one stretch of water and becomes one.”

The physician’s needle paused.

“Is that theology,” she asked, “or jealousy?”

The chamberlain became deeply interested in the basin.

Shuka studied the dust upon the table.

The king looked at the woman kneeling before him.

“You have mistaken continued employment for immortality.”

“No,” she said. “I have seen your wound.”

The king laughed once.

It hurt him. He pressed a hand to his side and laughed again because pain had made the first laugh undignified.

The physician finished the stitch.

“I do not admire the rumour,” she said. “I admire the difficulty of the thing beneath it.”

The king’s amusement faded.

“Shuka,” he said.

The spy looked up.

“Tell her what was beneath it.”

Shuka glanced at the chamberlain and the servant hiding badly near the curtain.

The king noticed.

“Stay,” he told them. “If the northern prince is a god, secrecy will not inconvenience him. If he is a man, we have already kept too many secrets.”

The servant stepped out from the curtain. She held the unused towels against her chest.

The floor moved again.

This blow was followed by a cracking sound from far below, and then by the bells of the potters’ quarter ringing without agreement.

Shuka lowered himself into the chair opposite the king.

“There was no bridge when I first reached the northern shore,” he said.

The physician began winding clean linen around the king’s waist.

“There were only arguments.”

---

The shore had smelled of salt, human waste, boiled millet and wet rope.

Shuka had expected an army camp. What he found resembled a market that had quarrelled with a shipyard and then spread for two miles along the coast.

Men from the northern plains slept beneath carts. Hill soldiers had raised shelters from leaves broad enough to roof a goat shed. Fishermen mended nets beside armourers. Cooks shouted at officers who crossed their fire pits. Physicians boiled cloth in pots that had previously contained rice. Everywhere there were animals, tools, sandals, baskets, broken shafts and people asking where something had been placed.

Nothing looked divine.

It looked badly organised, exhausted and very large.

Shuka entered wearing the coarse blue cloth of a salt porter. He carried a basket upon his shoulder and adopted the expression of a man who had been blamed for a delay he did not cause. It proved to be the most common expression in the camp and allowed him to travel almost anywhere.

At the water’s edge, several officers were shouting over a table made from two doors.

The sea lay beyond them, black-blue beneath the afternoon sun. Far to the south, the island appeared as a dark line that could have been cloud. Between the two shores, reefs and sandbanks disturbed the surface in pale, uncertain patches.

An elderly fisherman stood at the table with one bare foot upon a military map.

An officer told him to remove it.

The fisherman refused.

“Your map has put deep water where I have walked carrying my grandson,” he said. “My foot is the most accurate part of it.”

The officer appealed to a commander.

The commander looked at the map, then at the fisherman’s foot.

“Wash it,” she said. “Then leave it where it is.”

The first thing that impressed Shuka was not their knowledge.

It was whom they permitted to possess it.

In the island’s naval hall, admirals spoke first, engineers second and fishermen when summoned to confirm what had already been decided. Here, rope-makers corrected captains. Divers contradicted priests. A boy who collected shellfish was allowed to move a marker because he knew where the current cut beneath a reef after moonrise.

They argued constantly.

Then they measured.

Along the beach, men drove marked poles into the shallows. At fixed hours, scribes waded out and recorded where the water touched each pole. They marked the wind, the pull of the current and the shape of the moon. When two records disagreed, they did not choose the more senior scribe. They returned to the water.

Shuka watched a surveyor stretch a long rope across the sand.

Knots divided it into equal lengths. Three workers held the rope at different points while the surveyor shouted at them for moving.

“You are holding the fourth knot,” she told one man.

“You said the fourth.”

“I said four lengths. A knot is not a length. This is why bridges fall.”

The man moved his hand.

The three portions of rope formed a triangle: three measures along one side, four along another and five across the longest line. The surveyor pressed a peg into the angle between the shorter sides.

“True,” she said.

They repeated the shape farther down the beach, and a straight row of pegs began advancing towards the sea.

Shuka did not know the rule behind it. He only knew that whenever the three lengths met, the corner was the same.

“Did you report that?” the king asked in the western room.

The physician pulled the bandage tight.

Shuka looked at him.

“I reported survey lines, tidal poles and timber preparation.”

“And what did my council say?”

“That measuring the sea was not the same as crossing it.”

The king closed his eyes briefly.

“A sentence with the comfortable sound of wisdom.”

“You agreed with it,” Shuka said.

The physician secured the linen with a knot.

The king opened his eyes.

“Yes.”

He did not defend himself.

Shuka returned to the shore.

---

The second thing that impressed him was how often the northerners destroyed their own work.

Carpenters built short sections of wooden frame upon the beach. Each was only the length of six men lying head to foot. Workers loaded stones onto them while scribes counted baskets.

The first frame bent.

The second twisted.

The third held until a carpenter struck one joint with the flat of an axe. Then the entire structure leaned sideways and emptied its stones across the sand.

No one prayed over the broken wood.

They examined it.

They argued about where the fibres had split. They changed the crossing of the ropes, widened the base and built another frame.

Then they broke that one too.

Shuka spent an afternoon carrying stones for the tests because the work placed him close to the engineers. By sunset his shoulders burned and he had learned three useful facts: the frames were meant to disappear beneath the water, the engineers trusted failure more than confidence, and a basket of wet stone was heavier when carried for an enemy.

On the following morning, a new frame supported twice the previous load.

The workers cheered.

An engineer ordered more stones.

They continued until it failed.

“Why break the successful one?” a young porter asked.

The engineer looked genuinely surprised by the question.

“How else will we learn when it stops being successful?”

That evening Shuka sent a message across the water in the belly of a gutted fish.

He described timber boxes, stone loads and woven mats of coir.

The reply from the island arrived two nights later.

**No permanent structure of this kind can withstand the southern current. Continue observing.**

The statement bore the seal of the naval council.

Shuka read it twice.

Then he burned it over a cooking fire and used the ash to darken his porter’s cloth.

The council was correct.

The bridge could not be permanent.

The northern prince had never asked for permanence.

He had asked how long a structure must remain standing to move an army from one shore to another.

The answer was not a century.

It was six days.

---

The bridge began beneath the water.

Divers carried ropes between the shallow ridges. Men laid woven mats of coir and brushwood over soft sand so that the first stones would not vanish into it. Timber grids followed. Into these they lowered rock, broken coral and baskets of rubble.

The sea took much of it.

They returned with more.

From the shore it appeared that stones were being thrown into deep water and refusing to sink. The supporting frames were already submerged. The pale crests of the reefs were invisible beneath the stirred sand. Each new layer concealed the intelligence that held the next one.

By the third day, men were walking where the previous morning there had been only water.

That was when the stories began.

A cook told Shuka that the prince had whispered a name over every stone.

A soldier swore the sea had raised its floor to touch the prince’s feet.

A priest announced that the water recognised the purpose of a righteous man.

Nearby, six divers vomited seawater behind a cart.

No one announced that the sea recognised them.

“Did the prince encourage it?” the physician asked.

She had finished with the king and was washing his blood from her fingers. The basin water had turned the colour of weak tamarind.

Shuka considered the question.

“He did not begin the stories.”

“That was not what she asked,” the king said.

Shuka looked towards the little lamp beside the black stone.

“No,” he said. “He did not correct them.”

The king leaned back carefully.

“Why would he?”

The physician dried her hands.

“Because they were untrue.”

“Truth is one instrument of command,” the king replied. “Not always the loudest.”

The physician studied him.

“You sound as though you approve.”

“I sound as though I recognise it.”

Outside, the bells stopped.

For a few breaths the city became strangely quiet.

Then the wooden road struck the northern earthwork again.

The servant behind the curtain flinched.

Shuka continued.

---

The smallest workers came at low tide.

They were children too young to carry stone, old people whose backs rejected baskets and women recovering from wounds that prevented heavier work. They sat in long rows beneath palm-leaf awnings, twisting coconut fibre into rough bundles.

The bundles were soaked in a mixture of shell-lime, fine sand, boiled tree resin and something that smelled powerfully of spoiled milk.

Once coated, they were rolled through dry sand and carried into the shallows. Workers pushed them into the spaces between larger stones, where the fibres trapped smaller material and slowed the water that tried to pull it free.

The children wore three lines of pale clay across their backs so supervisors could identify which group had completed each section.

When they bent over the sand, the marks resembled stripes.

One little boy soaked his entire tunic in the binding mixture, rolled accidentally down a pile of sand and emerged coated from shoulder to ankle.

An old woman looked at him and said, “A squirrel would have carried more and complained less.”

The workers laughed.

By supper, the children’s group had become the squirrel company.

By the following morning, men farther along the shore were saying that squirrels had come from the trees to help the prince.

One soldier claimed to have seen them wet their bodies before rolling in sand.

Shuka asked how many.

“Thousands,” the soldier said.

“Were they organised?”

“Perfectly.”

“Who commanded them?”

The soldier considered this.

“A larger squirrel.”

Shuka wrote the answer upon a scrap of bark because some foolishness deserved preservation.

In the western room, the young servant laughed before she could stop herself.

She covered her mouth with the towels.

The king looked at her.

“Do not apologise,” he said. “It may be the last accurate response available to us.”

The servant lowered the towels.

“My grandmother told me squirrels carried the sand,” she said.

No one asked how a story from the enemy shore had already reached the palace kitchens.

That was the nature of stories. Armies required roads. Stories crossed without them.

“She said the prince stroked one across the back,” the servant added, “and that is why they have stripes.”

The physician glanced at the king.

“There is your medical evidence.”

“Dismiss her immediately,” he said.

The servant’s face emptied.

“Majesty?”

“Anyone whose grandmother knows more than the naval council is required elsewhere.”

The chamberlain rescued the girl with a small movement of his hand.

“He is joking,” he told her.

The king looked offended.

“I was considering promotion.”

The servant smiled uncertainly.

The floor shook again.

Her smile disappeared.

---

The men called monkeys were easier to understand once Shuka stood among them.

They came from forested hills and warm river valleys. Their companies carried long banners painted with animals: monkey, bear, boar and bright-beaked birds. Some wore cords at the back of their belts to show clan and rank. From a distance the cords resembled tails.

They climbed timber frames without ladders. They moved along ropes with bare feet and laughed at plainsmen who required both hands. Their officers used whistles, hand signs and calls that carried above surf better than shouted words.

To soldiers who did not speak their language, the calls sounded like animals in the canopy.

The plainsmen began calling them monkeys.

Some of the forest soldiers accepted the name because insults become less useful when worn willingly. Others hated it. One broad-shouldered foreman broke a man’s nose for offering him a banana.

Shuka met the foreman while unloading timber.

The man carried pickled lime in a pouch and ate it between instructions. His teeth had been stained yellow by spices. He could calculate rope lengths by looking at a span and was wrong less often than the scribes who measured it.

“You island men use timber differently,” the foreman said.

Shuka nearly dropped his end of the beam.

“I am from the western salt flats.”

“Then the western salt flats teach the island knot.”

The foreman held Shuka’s gaze.

Around them, men carried wood into the water. No guard appeared to have noticed.

“Are you going to call one?” Shuka asked.

“If I call a guard, this beam remains here.”

The foreman shifted his grip.

“Lift.”

They carried it together.

At the end of the platform, the foreman spat a lime seed into the sea.

“When you report this,” he said, “tell your king we are not monkeys.”

Shuka’s hands tightened upon the timber.

“What are you?”

“Underfed.”

The foreman took another piece of lime from his pouch.

“And presently busy.”

He walked away.

Shuka never learned whether the man had recognised a knot, an accent or fear.

Years later, songs would remove his arithmetic, his judgement and his refusal to delay work for an arrest. They would leave him fur.

It was easier to believe that an army of animals built the impossible road.

Animals did not require wages, names or graves.

---

On the fourth morning, a great bird rose above the shore.

Its wings were made from bamboo ribs and tightly woven cloth. A painted beak extended from the front. Ropes as thick as a man’s wrist ran from its body to four teams upon the beach.

Beneath the wings, a narrow wicker cradle held a scout.

The sea wind lifted the structure in violent movements. Men ran with the ropes. The scout rose above the palms, kicking once in terror before remembering the dignity required by those visible to an entire army.

The painted wings steadied.

From that height he could see the broken pale line of reefs, the deeper channels and the southern shore. He released strips of coloured cloth to signal changes in current and enemy movement.

The camp called the device Garuda.

By noon, men who had not witnessed its launch said a giant eagle had carried a warrior into the sky.

By evening, the warrior had become the prince.

Shuka watched the cloth bird until his neck hurt.

He did not know whether to be impressed by the men who had raised it or alarmed by how quickly the truth had become insufficient.

The scout descended badly. The wicker cradle struck the beach, rolled twice and deposited him beside a cooking fire.

He stood with sea grass in his hair while the entire shore cheered.

“What did you see?” an officer shouted.

The scout looked towards the sky device collapsing behind him.

“My ancestors,” he said.

The cheer became laughter.

The following launch used a wider cradle.

The physician smiled when Shuka described it.

The king did not.

He was looking at the arrowhead upon her tray.

Its black surface was hollowed by a narrow groove. Resin remained inside it.

“The arrows carrying fire,” he said.

The physician handed it to him.

“Recovered from a man at the eastern storehouse. The shaft held wrapped fibre behind the head. Oil and resin, perhaps saltpetre. It continued burning after it entered the wound.”

The king turned the arrowhead beneath the lamp.

“Not fire summoned from the sky.”

“No.”

“Only careful cruelty.”

The physician’s expression changed.

“Care is not always mercy.”

The king set the arrowhead down beside Kumbhakarna’s clasp.

Black iron and scratched bronze touched upon the table.

Shuka returned to the bridge.

---

The northern prince did not design the frames.

He did not calculate the tides, twist the ropes or discover the shallow ridge. Men who would receive shorter songs did those things.

His talent lay elsewhere.

He made knowledge stand in the same place.

The fisherman, the surveyor, the forest foreman, the quarry master and the officer responsible for grain were required to look at one map. None was permitted to leave merely because another had insulted him. When the rope-makers said the timber schedule was impossible, the carts carrying ceremonial cloth were stripped and their axles reassigned. When priests objected to cutting certain trees, the prince asked them to identify others that would bend without splitting.

He listened quickly.

He decided faster.

And once he decided, the suffering required by the decision became someone else’s task.

Shuka saw him clearly only twice.

The first time, the prince stood upon a raised timber platform at sunrise. A pale parasol had been fixed behind him to shield the map table. Its polished inner surface caught the eastern light.

From the beach below, the brightness formed a clean circle around his head.

Men stopped working to look.

The prince noticed the silence and turned. For a moment even Shuka understood how the rumour had begun. The man appeared cut from the morning: straight-backed, dark-haired and still while everything around him moved.

Then the wind shifted.

The parasol struck him behind one ear.

He swore and ordered it tied more securely.

The god vanished.

The commander remained.

The second time Shuka saw him, thirty-one men were beneath the water.

A section of the causeway had folded where a deep channel cut between two reefs. The timber frame twisted. Loaded stone dragged it sideways, pulling the next section with it. Workers disappeared into brown water filled with rope, baskets and broken wood.

The camp ran towards the collapse.

Shuka ran with them.

He entered the water before remembering that a spy was not required to save enemy labourers.

The sea reached his chest. A rope moved beneath the surface and wrapped around his ankle. He cut it with the small blade hidden in his belt.

A hand appeared between two beams.

Shuka caught it.

The man beneath the wood pulled once, then stopped. Shuka shouted for help. A forest worker climbed across the frame, plunged both arms into the water and found the trapped man’s shoulder.

Together they lifted.

The beam did not move.

More stones rolled from the damaged section. One struck the forest worker’s back. He grunted but kept his grip.

The trapped man’s face surfaced between them.

He was young. Mud filled one ear. He inhaled once and said, with extraordinary irritation, “My foot.”

Shuka reached beneath the beam.

The foot was caught inside a woven mat that had folded around it. Shuka cut the fibres. The young man came free so suddenly that all three fell backwards into the water.

They dragged him to the shallows.

He vomited upon Shuka’s knees.

“Thank you,” he said when he could breathe.

“You are welcome,” Shuka replied.

Neither asked the other’s name.

Farther out, men were still shouting from inside the collapsed frame.

The tide was rising.

Engineers gathered around the prince. Shuka remained close enough to hear because no one examined a salt porter covered in blood and vomit.

“We must lift the outer grid,” the chief engineer said. “There are men beneath it.”

“How long?” the prince asked.

“Two hours if the ropes hold.”

The surveyor looked towards the marked poles.

“We have less than one hour before the current turns.”

“Then brace the next section and lift from the northern side,” an officer said.

The engineer shook his head.

“If we brace it, the trapped space closes.”

The prince looked towards the broken causeway.

Hands appeared and disappeared between the timbers. A man shouted for his brother. Another voice called upon his mother with the simple confidence of a child who believed she could still reach him.

“How many?” the prince asked.

“Perhaps twelve alive,” the engineer said. “Perhaps more.”

“And if we dismantle it?”

The surveyor answered.

“The current takes both sections. We lose a day, perhaps two.”

The prince looked north along the shore.

Thousands of men waited beside piles of stone. Behind them, carts extended beyond sight. The army’s food would not last indefinitely. Storm clouds had begun gathering beyond the western headland.

The prince’s younger brother stood beside him.

“We can recover them,” the brother said.

The prince did not look at him.

“Can we recover them and preserve the crossing?”

No one answered.

The trapped men continued shouting.

The prince closed his eyes.

Only for one breath.

Then he opened them.

“Brace it,” he said.

The engineer did not move.

“Majesty—”

“If we open the frame for twelve men, the sea closes it against twelve thousand.”

The prince turned towards the waiting crews.

“Brace it.”

Hammers began.

The sound travelled through the timber and into the water.

Men drove wedges beside the places from which voices still came. Stone followed. Basket after basket was emptied into the broken grid.

The calling beneath it became harder to hear.

The young labourer Shuka had pulled free tried to stand.

“My cousin is there.”

A physician forced him down.

“Your ankle is broken.”

“My cousin is there.”

The physician held his shoulders while the road was closed over the living.

The prince remained until the work was finished.

He did not turn away.

Shuka hated him slightly more for that.

It would have been easier if the order had cost him nothing.

At sunset, the repaired section held.

The army cheered when the first loaded cart crossed it.

No one could hear the men below.

That night, priests placed lamps along the completed road. From the beach, the flames appeared to lie upon the sea. Soldiers said the ocean had accepted the prince’s purpose.

Shuka sat alone beside an overturned basket and washed another man’s blood from his hands.

The young labourer found him by hopping upon one foot.

His broken ankle had been tied between two pieces of wood.

He offered Shuka half a millet cake.

“For pulling me out,” he said.

Shuka accepted it.

The labourer looked towards the lamps.

“He had no choice.”

Shuka knew whom he meant.

“There is always a choice.”

“Not for kings.”

“Especially for kings.”

The young man broke the remaining half of the cake into smaller pieces.

“If he had opened it, the work of the dead would have been lost.”

“Some were not dead.”

The labourer stared at the sea road.

“They are now.”

He ate one piece of cake without tasting it.

“Tomorrow I go back.”

“With that foot?”

“I can twist fibre sitting down.”

Shuka looked at him.

“Why?”

The labourer seemed surprised.

“Because we must cross.”

Faith, Shuka realised, did not always look like prayer.

Sometimes it looked like a man returning to the work that had killed his cousin because the man who ordered it had made the destination feel larger than the dead.

---

The western room had become completely still.

The physician held the bloodied bandage she had removed from the king. The servant’s towels had lowered to her waist. The chamberlain stood beside the basin with his eyes upon the floor.

The king looked at Kumbhakarna’s clasp.

Wood struck stone beyond the northern wall.

Once.

Twice.

The king spoke without lifting his head.

“Was the road completed?”

“Yes,” Shuka said.

“Did the twelve thousand cross?”

“More than twelve thousand.”

“Then his calculation was correct.”

The physician placed the old bandage on her tray.

“A correct calculation can still contain a murder.”

The king looked at her.

“A physician has the privilege of treating one body at a time.”

“A king has the privilege of calling bodies numbers.”

“No,” he said. “The duty.”

The physician’s eyes moved to the broken clasp.

“Your brother had a name.”

The king’s face changed.

The chamberlain stepped forward, but Shuka spoke first.

“So did the men beneath the bridge.”

The king turned towards him.

For a moment the old force returned to his face—the force before which councils had fallen silent, ambassadors had reconsidered their language and brothers had learned to carry wounds without showing them.

Shuka did not look away.

The king’s anger passed.

What remained was older and more tired.

“You believe we are alike,” he said.

“I believe you recognised his choice before I finished describing it.”

The king looked towards the northern smoke.

“He buried labourers beneath a road.”

“You sent your brother beneath an army.”

“My brother chose.”

“After you placed the choice before the one man whose love would not permit refusal.”

The words entered the room softly.

Nothing in them was new.

That was why they hurt.

The physician gathered her instruments.

“The northern prince may be a god,” she said. “But if he is, he has learned the arithmetic of kings.”

The king watched her place the black arrowhead beside the needles.

“And if he is only a man?”

She lifted the tray.

“Then men are enough.”

She left the room.

The servant followed with the basin. The chamberlain remained long enough to replace the cracked silver cup with a clay one.

“The silver can be repaired,” he said.

“Everything can,” the king replied.

The chamberlain looked at him.

The king touched the broken clasp.

“That was not an instruction.”

The old man bowed and went out.

---

The bridge did not rise in a single miracle.

It advanced by measurements, failures and men carrying things heavier than themselves.

Each morning, the sea removed part of the previous day’s certainty. Each afternoon, the workers returned it in another form. They widened sections that shook. They opened channels where trapped water pushed against the frames. They changed the angle of the outer stones after watching waves break across them. They learned not to oppose the whole sea when it was sufficient to persuade a small part of it to pass elsewhere.

No one person understood the entire structure.

That was its greatest strength.

The prince understood people.

He appeared wherever work had begun slowing. He praised one company before another. He ate the same grain served to the labourers, but from a clean leaf and beneath a guarded awning. He learned the names of injured men and sometimes confused them. He touched shoulders. He listened to complaints until the speaker felt heard, then returned to the original order.

Men left his presence believing they had persuaded him.

Usually they had merely explained to themselves why they would obey.

On the fifth day, rain struck the coast.

The bridge disappeared behind grey water.

Workers tied themselves to the frames and continued. Lightning opened the horizon. Wind tore three shelters from the beach and deposited one inside the officers’ latrine, an event the army interpreted as divine judgement upon administration.

The great cloth Garuda could not be raised. Surveyors crawled along the causeway with poles. Forest climbers carried ropes into places where carts could no longer move.

The so-called monkeys saved the centre section.

The squirrel companies filled the gaps behind them.

Fishermen shouted the rhythm of the waves.

Carpenters struck between each surge.

At sunset, the storm passed.

The bridge remained.

Men stood upon it in rain-darkened clothes and looked back at what they had made. It stretched across the water in an uneven line of timber, stone and mud, too narrow in some places, submerged in others, already requiring repair behind them.

It was not beautiful.

It was not permanent.

It was enough.

The army began crossing before the sky cleared.

From the island watchtowers, it must have appeared that men were walking upon the sea beneath animal banners while a divine prince followed a path built for him by obedient nature.

Shuka knew better.

He had seen the test frames split. He had smelled shell-lime upon children’s hands. He had heard men beneath the water. He had watched a fisherman’s dirty foot correct a royal map.

The truth was less miraculous.

It was also more frightening.

No god had built the road.

People had.

A messenger reached the western room at a run.

He stopped at the curtain, saw the king and forgot the first half of his bow.

“Majesty. The northern embankment has reached the wall.”

The king rose.

Pain caught him at the hip. His hand found the chair, steadied and released it before the messenger could pretend not to notice.

“What are they using?” he asked.

“Timber grids filled with rubble. They lay them across the ditch and bring the next upon the first. Our arrows slow them, but their shields cover the workers.”

Shuka understood the sound beneath the floor.

The same modular frames that had crossed weak seabed were now crossing the defensive ditch. The enemy had carried the bridge with them—not as a road, but as knowledge.

“The outer face?” the king asked.

“Holding. The western drain has cracked.”

“Open the drain fully before it collapses. Flood the ditch from the dye works.”

The messenger hesitated.

“The dyers are sheltering below the vats.”

“Move them through the temple lane. Use the palace carts.”

“The palace carts are carrying wounded from the brickworks.”

The king closed his eyes for the length of one breath.

“Then empty the ceremonial stable.”

“Those horses were sent north with—”

The messenger stopped.

With Kumbhakarna.

The king completed the sentence.

“My brother.”

The messenger lowered his head.

“Yes, Majesty.”

The king looked around the room.

There were no carts left in it. No hidden company of rested soldiers. No god waiting behind the screen.

Only a spy with a damaged foot, a cracked cup, an old map and a lamp burning before a black stone.

“Take the musicians’ wagons,” he said.

The messenger blinked.

“Majesty?”

“The large ones used for the drums. Remove the painted sides. Put the dyers in those.”

“And the instruments?”

The king looked towards the veena case standing beneath the western shelf.

“Instruments have survived worse rulers.”

The messenger bowed and ran.

Shuka stood.

“You should not go to the wall.”

“That sentence has had a very unsuccessful day.”

“The physician will blame me.”

“She possesses imagination. She will find reasons.”

The king fastened his outer cloth over the new bandage. His fingers stopped at the table.

He picked up the black arrowhead.

Then he picked up Kumbhakarna’s clasp.

He placed the arrowhead in his belt and the clasp inside his robe.

“Do you still believe he is not a god?” Shuka asked.

The king turned towards the small shrine.

The wick had settled into a steady flame. Oil shone upon the black stone. Water remained in the bronze bowl beneath it, holding a small reflection of fire.

“I believe divinity should require more than winning,” he said.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have.”

They walked from the western room.

The corridor outside had been stripped of carpets for bandages. Servants carried doors towards the infirmary to be used as beds. A cook passed with a pot of thin lentils and shouted at two soldiers to move because soup respected no rank. Somewhere in the lower court, a child was singing the squirrel story badly and being corrected by another child who knew an even less accurate version.

The palace smelled of resin, smoke, boiled cloth and rain approaching from the sea.

At the northern balcony, the city opened beneath them.

Fires marked the broken districts. Temple courtyards had become infirmaries. Lines of people moved through the streets carrying water, grain and those who could no longer carry themselves.

Beyond the wall, enemy standards filled the dark.

At the ditch, hundreds of men laboured beneath overlapping shields. Timber frames advanced through mud coloured purple by the opened dye works. Stones followed. Ropes tightened. Officers signalled with lamps.

From the height of the balcony, the movement appeared almost effortless.

A road was forming where no road had been.

The king gripped the parapet.

“There,” Shuka said.

Upon a distant rise stood a pale circular canopy lit from below by fire baskets. A dark figure occupied its centre. At that distance there was no face, only stillness surrounded by light.

The physician’s halo.

An arrow rose from somewhere beneath it.

The shaft travelled too far for Shuka to follow. A moment later, fire appeared upon the island’s outer wooden screen.

The soldiers beyond the ditch cheered.

The king watched the flame spread.

“A god?” Shuka asked.

“Oil, resin, a hollow head and an archer who has measured the wind.”

The king’s voice held contempt.

It also held admiration.

Below them, the enemy workers set another timber frame into place.

“Magic,” the king said, “is what remains when no one remembers the measurements.”

The northern screen collapsed in sparks.

For a moment the road beyond the wall shone clearly: wet rope, stone, timber, bent backs and hundreds of ordinary hands.

Then smoke covered it, and it became impossible again.
