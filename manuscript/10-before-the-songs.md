# Chapter Ten

## Before the Songs

Surpanakha required larger teeth.

The dead king’s sister had already been made green, broad-bellied and almost naked, but the master of ceremonies remained dissatisfied. Her reaching hand looked frightened rather than lustful. The blood beneath her missing nose appeared painful. Neither quality was suitable above a throne upon a morning devoted to the restoration of righteousness.

“Make the teeth visible when her mouth is closed,” the master said.

The painter sat back upon his heels.

“Teeth generally require an opening.”

“These are not ordinary teeth.”

The painter considered the woman taking shape upon the cloth.

“Was she born with them?”

“She was a rakshasi.”

“So I am told. I am asking about the teeth.”

The master of ceremonies lowered his voice.

“The picture must explain what happened to her.”

The painter understood.

He lengthened the two side teeth until they curved over her lower lip. He widened the mouth around them, darkened her eyes and added nails like small knives to the hand reaching towards the handsome prince.

The master nodded.

Surpanakha’s mutilation now looked less like something done to a woman and more like something prevented.

Sunlight entered through the eastern arches.

It was clear, warm and astonishingly yellow.

Throughout the long night, the palace had belonged to lamps. Its pillars had risen out of shadow. Its floors had reflected fire. Doors had opened upon smoke, arguments and people carrying things that bled.

Now every door stood open.

Servants had washed the audience hall with water scented by crushed lemongrass. Fresh white cloth concealed the cracks in two pillars. Garlands of jasmine and orange trumpet flowers hung from beams blackened by the archive fire. Someone had placed bowls of rosewater beneath the windows, where they performed bravely against the odours of wet ash, medicinal oil and bodies being removed through the western court.

The blood upon the third stair was gone.

Cold water had worked.

Musicians tuned beside the treasury doors. Cooks carried sweetened rice into the courtyard. Priests in new garments discussed the correct direction from which a restored kingdom should receive its blessing. A carpenter repaired the dais upon which the new king would sit, striking each peg with a cheerful sound.

Above him, the old king acquired his heads.

The painter had fitted four on the left, three on the right and one above the central face. The arrangement left an empty space near the right shoulder.

“Nine,” the painter said.

The ceremonial poet looked up from the victory hymn.

“Ten.”

“Ten will not fit.”

“He possessed ten.”

“At the same time?”

The poet frowned.

“What other arrangement do you propose?”

The painter turned his brush between his fingers.

“Perhaps one principal head and nine suggested behind it.”

“The audience may fail to count suggestions.”

“The audience is expected to count during the hymn?”

“Children will count them for centuries.”

The painter shifted the upper row and created room for the tenth.

“What expression?” he asked.

“Vile.”

“All ten?”

“Vile in different ways.”

The painter gave one face drunkenness, one rage, one hunger and one cowardice. He made another laugh while surrounded by dead men. The central head received intelligence, because evil became more satisfying when it understood what it was doing.

At the monster’s feet he painted Kumbhakarna.

The giant lay beside seven bowls of food with his eyes closed and mouth open. Tiny soldiers struck drums beside his ears. A stream of rice descended towards him from frightened servants.

Shuka stood near the final pillar.

No one had invited him. No one had yet remembered to remove him.

His robe was grey with the dust of the northern gate. Dried blue water marked its hem. In one hand he held the folded enemy cloth from which the new painting had taken its arrangement. The other remained closed around a small metal plate scratched with the positions of veena notes.

“The rice was for his men,” Shuka said.

The painter looked down from the platform.

“Whose men?”

“Kumbhakarna’s.”

The ceremonial poet recognised the name.

“The giant ate entire carts of rice.”

“He carried the carts for the soldiers following him.”

The painter examined the space around the sleeping figure.

“Should I include the soldiers?”

The master of ceremonies looked at the crowded cloth.

“There is no room.”

The painter added another bowl.

Outside, conches sounded.

Everyone in the hall turned towards the eastern doors.

A herald entered wearing white. He walked to the centre of the newly washed floor, lifted a staff decorated with forest leaves and waited until the musicians stopped tuning.

His voice travelled easily in daylight.

“Rama, eldest son of Dasharatha, guardian of dharma and rightful heir of Ayodhya, has slain Ravana, the ten-headed king of the rakshasas.”

The names filled the palace as though they had always lived there.

Rama.

Ravana.

One arrived carrying righteousness. The other arrived already dead.

The servants repeated the announcement in the courtyards. The gate captains sent it down the streets. Drummers carried it towards the harbour. By the third repetition, it no longer sounded like news. It sounded like something the world had remembered.

At dawn, Rama had killed Ravana.

This was known before anyone found the body.

---

They found several kings at the northern gate.

One body wore a field officer’s shoulder covering. Another had died beneath a fallen standard. A third was so badly burned that rank could be inferred only from the metal scattered around him. Men who had fought through the smoke disagreed about where Ravana had last stood.

One soldier had seen Rama’s arrow enter his chest.

Another said the island king continued fighting after three arrows struck him.

A forest archer insisted that every severed head had grown again until Rama invoked a weapon taught by the gods.

The archer had not been close enough to see Ravana’s face, but distance improved his account.

The northern captain was not found.

Neither was the sword the king had carried.

Near the broken gate, searchers discovered blackened bronze beneath a dead man’s neck. The clasp was plain, heavy and hooked at one end.

Vibhishana held it in his palm.

He had seen the same clasp fastened at Kumbhakarna’s throat when they were young. Later he had seen it beneath his elder brother’s robe. The metal before him had passed through fire. One edge had melted enough to make certainty difficult.

Behind Vibhishana waited Rama, the northern prince.

Morning made the prince appear younger. Mud dried upon his legs. His hair had been tied back after the battle. A white parasol shaded him, creating a pale circle behind his head whenever the bearer stood in the proper place.

Lakshmana, his younger brother, remained slightly behind his right shoulder.

Hanuman stood to the left—the forest commander whose torn monkey emblem had once been carried into this palace as evidence against him. One forearm was wrapped where the road had collapsed beneath him. Without the animal standard, he looked like a powerful forest commander with tired eyes and a cut across his chin.

No one called him a monkey while asking him to identify bodies.

Rama looked at the covered figure upon the ground.

“Is it your brother?” he asked.

Vibhishana closed his hand around the clasp.

The body was the correct height. The armour had been made for a broad chest. The face could no longer contradict anyone.

“It is my brother,” Vibhishana said.

Lakshmana exhaled.

Hanuman lowered his head.

Rama stood in silence before the covered body. He had refused to promise withdrawal while the king lived. He offered honour easily now that honour could no longer alter the war.

“He was a great warrior,” Rama said.

Vibhishana looked towards the breached gate.

“He was several other things.”

Rama heard him.

“You will perform the rites due to a king and a brother.”

“Will your soldiers permit that?”

Rama’s face changed—not with anger, but with the small tightening of someone reminded that victory also required administration.

“They will obey.”

The funeral pyre was built before noon.

Sandalwood had become scarce, so ordinary timber formed its centre. Priests placed fragrant branches along the outside where witnesses could see them. The body remained covered. Vibhishana walked around it with the black clasp in his hand.

When the fire rose, no one asked for a second identification.

A missing king invited questions.

A cremated king left ash.

---

By the time Vibhishana returned to the palace, the tenth head had dried.

The painter was working upon Rama’s halo. Powdered shell made the white circle catch sunlight when viewed from the courtyard. Lakshmana’s sword had been lengthened to reach Surpanakha’s face without requiring the prince to bend.

Vibhishana stopped beneath the painting.

“She did not look like that,” he said.

The painter glanced at the master of ceremonies.

Rama entered behind the new king.

The hall adjusted itself around him. Musicians stood. Priests lowered their conversations. Servants moved trays out of his path before discovering they had done so.

Vibhishana pointed towards Surpanakha.

“My sister did not possess tusks.”

Rama studied the painted woman.

“No,” he said.

“Then why permit it?”

Rama answered without lowering his voice.

“Would you prefer the painting show my brother cutting an ordinary woman?”

Lakshmana stood near the entrance.

He had entered in time to hear. His face remained still, but his right hand closed over the other wrist.

Vibhishana looked at him.

For a moment, the clean hall contained a forest clearing, two amused young men and a woman learning that attraction could become a public punishment.

Then the musicians resumed tuning.

Vibhishana moved to Kumbhakarna.

“The rice was for his soldiers.”

“So I have been told,” Rama said.

Vibhishana turned.

“By whom?”

The painter pointed with his brush towards the final pillar.

The space was empty.

Shuka had been standing there moments earlier. The folded cloth was gone. So was the tuning plate. No guard remembered seeing him cross the courtyard. No servant recalled opening a door for him.

The master of ceremonies sent two soldiers to search the western passages.

They returned without a prisoner, a body or a useful explanation.

The coronation list later recorded every surviving officer of the former court who accepted the new king’s authority.

Shuka’s name was not upon it.

It appeared in no palace record again.

The painter waited.

“Shall I remove a bowl?” he asked.

Vibhishana looked at the sleeping giant.

“No.”

The painter dipped his brush.

Rama walked towards the ten-headed figure.

“He did not look like that either,” Vibhishana said.

“No.”

“He had one head.”

“Most men do.”

Vibhishana almost smiled.

The smile carried the memory of an elder brother who could conduct three arguments while tuning a string and eating food stolen from someone else’s plate. It lasted only until Vibhishana looked at the throne being repaired for him.

“Then why ten?” he asked.

Rama touched none of the wet paint.

“Your people must understand why you replaced him.”

“And yours must understand why they continued the invasion after Sita was free.”

The hall became quiet again.

Rama turned towards Vibhishana.

Nothing in his expression denied the sentence.

“My men crossed because a king took her,” Rama said. “Her release did not erase the taking.”

“Nor did his death restore the men beneath your road.”

Lakshmana looked down.

Rama’s voice remained calm.

“No kingdom can begin with ten explanations for the man it has removed.”

Vibhishana looked at the ten painted faces.

“He would have appreciated the number.”

Rama’s mouth moved slightly.

It was the first joke permitted between victor and successor.

The master of ceremonies approached with the official proclamation.

“We require the former king’s description,” he said. “The city copies will be read after the coronation.”

Rama gave the tablet to Vibhishana.

The text was brief. Ravana had been powerful, learned in forbidden arts, consumed by desire and finally destroyed by the righteousness he opposed. His brother Vibhishana, devoted always to dharma, had abandoned evil and been restored to his rightful place.

There was no garden gate.

There were no peace tablets.

There was no archive, no flying frame, no captain at the blue stair and no king waiting in fear while the ram counted towards him.

Vibhishana read it twice.

“What would you have them say?” Rama asked.

Through the open doors came the sounds of his coronation: flower sellers bargaining, carpenters hammering, priests disagreeing about water and cooks rescuing sweet rice from children who considered regime change an inadequate reason to delay breakfast.

Vibhishana could correct the proclamation.

He could add his brother’s music, scholarship and restraint. He could record the unconditional opening of Sita’s gate. He could state that the final offer had been refused. He could preserve Surpanakha’s face before the painter altered it.

Then he would sit upon his brother’s throne and ask the city to believe the brother had nevertheless required removal.

Vibhishana returned the tablet.

“Say that righteousness defeated evil.”

Rama nodded.

The master of ceremonies carried the words away.

Before memory made its god and monster, two men had chosen what could still be said.

---

The coronation was beautiful.

This was not entirely propaganda.

The city had survived the night. The northern gate stood open instead of burning. Water reached the lower streets again. Grain stores were unsealed. Mothers discovered sons among the wounded. Children who had learned to distinguish alarm bells were permitted to misunderstand the temple drums.

People needed flowers.

They covered Vibhishana with them.

He sat upon the repaired dais wearing a crown that had been cleaned but not reshaped. It settled differently upon his head. A priest poured water gathered from the island’s rivers and the northern sea. Another placed rice upon his brow. Rama stood beside him in white. Lakshmana held the royal parasol. Hanuman watched from among the forest captains, uncomfortable in a ceremonial cloth too narrow for his shoulders.

Women filled the galleries.

They carried garlands, lamps, food and children. None sat at the council table beneath the new throne. The physician stood briefly near the eastern door, still wearing yesterday’s blood upon one sleeve. An official asked her to change before joining the ceremony.

She looked at him until he found another duty.

The northern captain’s chair had been removed.

No one knew whether she still required it.

When the priest declared Vibhishana king, the hall cheered.

Vibhishana smiled.

Rama smiled beside him.

The painter upon the side platform studied both faces. Each man held the expression while the first lines were drawn. Rama’s smile suggested compassionate victory. Vibhishana’s suggested grateful restoration.

The painter lowered his board.

Rama’s smile disappeared first.

He rubbed the place where his bowstring had opened the skin of his forefinger. Vibhishana looked towards the corridor leading to his brother’s shrine.

Then the painter raised the board again.

Both men smiled.

The hall applauded the beginning of peace.

---

Hanuman carried the news to the garden.

He entered through the gate that remained open.

Sita, the woman the palace had called its guest, sat beneath the ashoka tree with pink petals caught in the folds of her pale robe. Her two attendants stood behind her. Upon the low table lay the tablet containing Rama’s refusal to promise withdrawal. A flower had dried against its edge.

Hanuman bowed deeply.

“Rama has won,” he said.

The words were everything she had waited to hear.

Sita closed her eyes.

Her breath left her in a sound that might have become laughter or weeping if she had permitted either. One attendant covered her mouth. The other began crying without attempting dignity.

“And the king?” Sita asked.

Hanuman hesitated.

“Ravana is dead.”

The name entered the garden after the man had left it.

Sita looked at the open gate.

She remembered him giving her the key because turning it pulled at the stitches in his side. She remembered the sentence he had finally spoken without defence.

Taking you was wrong.

She did not mourn him.

She also did not feel the clean relief expected by the announcement.

“Did Rama kill him?” she asked.

“That is what the army says.”

Sita looked at Hanuman.

He had chosen each word carefully.

“What do you say?”

The commander lowered his eyes.

“I saw Rama loose an arrow. I saw the gate disappear in smoke. I saw men carry a body after dawn.”

“Was it his?”

Hanuman answered as honestly as victory allowed.

“It has received his funeral.”

Servants arrived carrying water, fragrant oil and folded garments. Vibhishana had ordered that Sita be brought before Rama dressed according to her rank.

The older attendant touched the fine cloth.

“You are already dressed.”

The palace servant looked embarrassed.

“The ceremony requires brighter colours.”

Sita almost laughed.

Even freedom had a costume.

They bathed her hair in water warmed with vetiver. They rubbed sandalwood along her arms and placed red silk around her body. Gold returned to her wrists. Her attendants threaded small white flowers through her hair. The woman in the bronze mirror looked less like someone who had survived a siege and more like the conclusion of one.

When Sita stood, the garden seemed suddenly smaller.

She walked through its open gate for the second time.

This time trumpets waited outside.

---

The reunion took place in the eastern court.

Sunlight covered everything the night would have hidden.

Rama stood beneath a white parasol wearing a fresh robe. Lakshmana was beside him. Hanuman took his place among the commanders. Rows of soldiers filled the court: plainsmen, forest companies, bridge builders and archers who had crossed the sea because they had been promised that this moment would explain the blood behind them.

Sita entered through flower petals.

The crowd smiled.

Rama smiled.

For one unguarded instant, Sita smiled too.

He was alive.

Whatever else the war had become, the man she loved stood before her. She saw the old tiredness around his eyes, the small scar upon his arm and the way his left shoulder lowered when he had slept badly. Months collapsed. The forest returned. A cooking fire, wet bark, an argument over whether rice had burned. Ordinary things came back with such force that she nearly ran to him.

Then she noticed the distance left open between them.

A court painter waited at the side with a board upon his knees.

Rama held the smile while the painter marked the line of his face. Sita took her place beside him. The parasol bearer adjusted the white circle until it framed Rama’s head. Flower girls gathered near Sita’s feet.

The painter looked up.

The husband and wife smiled together.

He looked down.

Rama’s face became tired.

Sita’s became watchful.

The painter looked up once more.

They smiled.

Behind the soldiers, someone whispered.

“The island keeps its prisoners comfortably.”

Another man answered, “Perhaps she did not remain a prisoner for long.”

A few soldiers laughed.

Lakshmana heard them. He said nothing.

Rama heard them too.

His eyes moved over Sita’s clean hair, the red silk, the gold upon her wrists and the healthy colour the garden attendants had struggled to preserve in her face.

“My messenger said you were kept in a garden,” Rama said.

Sita looked at Hanuman.

“Then why are you asking me?”

Rama glanced towards the soldiers.

“He saw you once.”

Sita understood what courtesy had been asked to conceal.

“Ask whether the king touched me,” she said. “Do not make courtesy carry cowardice for you.”

The court became still.

Rama’s smile was gone now. The painter continued working because history often required the expression that had existed before the question.

“Did Ravana touch you?” Rama asked.

“No.”

“Did he enter the garden?”

“Yes.”

“Did he speak with you alone?”

“Sometimes.”

A soldier behind Lakshmana muttered, “And we are to trust the dead demon’s restraint?”

Rama did not silence him.

Sita looked at the army that had arrived to restore her dignity and now waited for her to prove that it remained intact.

“He confined my body,” she said. “You receive me by asking me to defend it.”

No one smiled.

The painter looked up at precisely the wrong moment.

“Please,” he said softly. “The court has commissioned the reunion.”

It was an absurd request.

It was also an honest description of the morning.

Rama looked at Sita.

Love remained in his face. So did doubt, fatigue, political calculation and the knowledge that thousands of men had made her body the public measure of his honour. He had crossed the sea for her. He had also arrived surrounded by people to whom he now owed a version of her.

He held out his hand.

Sita could refuse.

The garden gate had taught her what such freedom meant.

If she rejected him, the army would call captivity corruption. If she defended Ravana’s restraint, they would use the defence as evidence of affection. If she accused Rama, her pain would become ingratitude. If she smiled, everyone could go home.

Sita placed her hand in his.

She smiled because she loved him.

She smiled because the war was over.

She smiled because thousands of people were waiting to discover what her face would permit them to believe.

The painter captured it.

Conches sounded. Flower petals rose and fell. Soldiers cheered as Rama and Sita stood together beneath the white circle of shade.

Righteousness had recovered what evil stole.

The picture was beautiful.

---

The problem of Sita’s purity did not disappear simply because the painter had finished.

It moved into the afternoon ceremony.

Priests brought a brazier to the court. Sita stood before it and repeated that no man had possessed her body or altered her heart. She held her hands above the flame until heat reddened her palms. Rama watched. The soldiers watched him watching.

A gust from the sea lifted the fire sideways. Sita’s red veil entered the flame.

Hanuman struck it down with his wrapped arm before it caught. An attendant poured rosewater across the cloth. Steam rose around Sita. For a moment she stood inside fire, water and white vapour without moving.

The court gasped.

By evening, the story had improved.

Sita had entered a great pyre. Fire itself had examined her. Agni, the god of flame, had returned her untouched to Rama’s arms. Hanuman’s burned bandage, the attendant’s rosewater and the dangerous edge of a veil were unnecessary details.

The miraculous version was kinder in one respect.

It allowed a god to believe Sita before men did.

---

The first complete song was performed at the victory feast.

It began with Rama’s birth beneath an auspicious sky. It followed his exile, Sita’s devotion and Lakshmana’s perfect loyalty. When Surpanakha entered, the singer changed his voice and scratched at his body until children laughed. Lakshmana’s blade flashed in a comic gesture. The musicians struck a playful rhythm as the ugly rakshasi fled.

No one described the blood.

Ravana appeared with ten faces, twenty arms and a laugh large enough to make cups tremble upon the tables. He carried Sita through the sky. She cried Rama’s name while forests passed beneath her.

Hanuman grew a tail before the second refrain.

The real commander sat beside the other captains, eating sweet rice with his uninjured hand. When the singer described him leaping across the ocean, the soldiers shouted in approval.

Hanuman leaned towards Lakshmana.

“I used a boat for most of it.”

Lakshmana kept his eyes upon the performance.

“The boat has poor stage presence.”

When the singer reached the bridge, squirrels rolled in sand and carried it upon their backs. Stones floated because Rama’s name had been written upon them. No engineer argued. Most were asleep outside after working to reopen the road for the return journey.

Kumbhakarna woke to the smell of food, consumed three carts and entered battle because thought required energy he preferred to spend chewing.

The hall laughed.

Vibhishana did not.

The singer gave Ravana ten deaths before allowing the final arrow to work. Each head fell and returned. Rama remained calm. The gods watched from the sky. At last Vibhishana revealed the secret place where his brother could be killed.

Vibhishana turned towards the ceremonial poet.

“I revealed no such place.”

The poet bowed from his seat.

“The audience requires your decision to affect the battle.”

“I gave them roads, culverts and the habits of his mind.”

“That is difficult to sing.”

“So you placed a secret inside his body?”

The poet spread his hands.

“Bodies fit the metre.”

Rama heard the exchange.

He said nothing.

The song reached Sita’s rescue. She emerged pure, smiling and grateful. Rama received her without hesitation. The brazier became a golden fire that opened like a flower and returned her to him.

Sita listened from behind a thin ceremonial screen.

The singer described Ravana attempting to violate her.

Her smile changed, but did not disappear.

She could correct the song and invite another public examination of her body. Or she could allow a dead man’s crime to grow large enough to protect her from suspicion.

She said nothing.

The singer mistook her silence for approval.

The final refrain declared that righteousness had defeated evil, restored the rightful king and made the world orderly again.

The music was excellent.

That mattered.

A complicated account required attention. The song required only memory and a voice. Its rhythm carried grief without forcing anyone to examine responsibility. Its demon was safely inhuman. Its hero never needed to calculate. Its rescued woman never made the celebration uncomfortable.

The ceremonial poet looked towards the final pillar, perhaps expecting the grey-robed spy to interrupt again.

The pillar stood empty.

“Continue,” Rama said.

The singer continued.

---

The new king’s men recovered the flying machine from the southern island three days later.

Its pilot had broken one arm during landing. Both children survived. The channel chart and signal lamps lay beside the damaged frame. The medicines and rescued records had travelled separately in the evacuation boats.

The flight drawings had burned.

Vibhishana’s engineers brought the craft back by boat. They repaired its torn silk and replaced the damaged ribs. Without the balancing tables, they understood only part of what they were repairing. The harbour engineer who had known the frame best was not among the survivors who presented themselves to the new court.

Rama walked around the machine.

Its wings opened like the petals of a pale flower.

“What is it called?” he asked.

Vibhishana looked at the treated silk.

“My brother called it several things, usually after whichever part had failed.”

The ceremonial poet touched one flower-shaped wing.

“Pushpaka,” he suggested.

The name pleased the court.

The pilot explained cliff wind, balance and the danger of carrying excessive weight. She recommended a short flight along the coast before the craft was dismantled and transported north.

Rama listened.

The song had already decided otherwise.

In it, Pushpaka became a golden palace of the sky, once stolen by Ravana and now restored to righteous hands. It carried Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, Hanuman, Vibhishana and an accommodating number of soldiers directly to Ayodhya. It expanded whenever a passenger approached. It required no wind, no launching rail and no engineer.

The physical machine made one brief flight above the harbour.

The miraculous one never landed.

They remembered that it flew.

They forgot everyone who had taught it how.

---

Rama and Sita returned north beneath a sky filled with flowers.

That was how the painters showed it.

In the physical journey there were boats, carts, sore feet, spoiled grain and an argument lasting two days about which timber sections of the sea road should be recovered. Rain entered Sita’s carriage. Hanuman’s arm became infected. Lakshmana developed a fever and denied it until he fainted beside a water cart.

The song removed these inconveniences.

Ayodhya received Rama in gold.

The streets were washed. Lamps lined the roofs. Women made coloured designs before their doors. Children climbed trees to see the returning prince. Brothers embraced. Mothers wept. Conches, drums and bells crowded the air until no individual grief could be heard beneath them.

Rama was crowned.

Sita sat beside him.

The court painter arranged them beneath a parasol. Rama wore the expression of a ruler whose suffering had proved his virtue. Sita wore the smile from the island court. Lakshmana stood in devoted profile. For Hanuman, kneeling at Rama’s feet, the painter supplied the face of a monkey and kept the shoulders of a man.

The painter looked up.

Everyone smiled.

He looked down.

Rama asked how much longer they must remain seated. Sita loosened one anklet where it had cut her skin. Lakshmana closed his eyes. Hanuman shifted his injured arm.

The painter looked up.

They became the picture again.

---

The names travelled farther than the people.

Children were called Rama in the hope that they would grow righteous. They were called Ramachandra, Raghava and Ram because goodness benefited from several affectionate forms. Brothers received the name Lakshmana. Devoted sons received Hanuman. Kings borrowed the names of Rama’s ancestors when blood alone did not provide enough legitimacy.

Ravana’s name travelled differently.

Parents used it for stubbornness, pride and disobedience.

“Do you want people to call you Ravana?” a mother asked when her son refused to share sweets.

The child surrendered the larger piece.

No one asked whether the old king had shared his grain.

Kumbhakarna became the name given to anyone who slept beyond sunrise. Men laughed over large meals and called one another Kumbhakarna without remembering that he had walked towards death fully awake.

Surpanakha became a face used to frighten girls away from desire. Her teeth lengthened with every generation. Her stomach grew. Her age changed according to what the storyteller wished to punish.

Sita became purity.

The transformation sounded like praise.

It left increasingly little room for the woman.

---

Rama’s birthplace became sacred.

At first, people marked the neighbourhood. Then they marked a courtyard. Later generations identified a particular foundation stone. Rulers repaired it, enlarged it and attached their names to its protection. New walls rose over old walls. Victories required inscriptions. Defeats required memory.

People travelled great distances to stand where righteousness was believed to have entered the world.

Some disagreed about who owned the ground.

They argued in courts, streets and royal halls. Buildings rose, fell and rose again. Each generation inherited the quarrel and insisted it had begun with the one before. Some men carried weapons to defend the birthplace of restraint. Others destroyed in order to restore. Every side called possession devotion.

Rama’s painted smile survived them all.

Ravana’s prediction required no surviving tablet.

It was built in stone.

---

The first effigy was smaller than expected.

Its maker had difficulty supporting ten heads upon one bamboo neck. The three on the left leaned inwards. The uppermost face turned whenever the wind changed, causing the demon to appear interested in the sweet seller behind him.

Children found this hilarious.

The next year, the craftsmen improved the frame. They added internal ropes, stronger crosspieces and a central spine. Firecrackers were packed behind each painted face. Kumbhakarna stood beside Ravana with an enormous stomach. A panel near the stage showed Surpanakha reaching towards Rama with tusks, green skin and breasts hanging to her waist.

Families arrived before sunset.

Vendors sold sweets, toys and small bows. Fathers lifted children upon their shoulders. Mothers arranged shawls against the evening wind. Actors painted themselves blue and gold. Someone responsible for Rama’s crown had misplaced it and accused Lakshmana. Lakshmana found it beneath Hanuman’s artificial tail.

The audience laughed.

The play began.

Surpanakha scratched, danced and rolled her eyes. Children shrieked with delight when Lakshmana raised his wooden blade. Kumbhakarna snored so loudly that a baby began crying. Servants pushed carts of painted food towards his mouth while the actor complained that one wheel had trapped his foot.

Ravana entered last.

Ten faces surrounded the actor’s real one. He laughed, abducted Sita and boasted of powers no man could possess. Rama lifted his bow. Hanuman flew. Stones floated. The audience knew every turn and loved the story partly because nothing unexpected remained inside it.

At the edge of the crowd, a child held a sweet in one hand and his father’s finger in the other.

He stared at the ten-headed effigy rising above the field.

“Was he really like that?” the child asked.

The father smiled.

“Worse.”

The actor playing Rama received the flaming arrow.

His first attempt went out.

The crowd laughed warmly. A stagehand relit it and wiped excess oil from the shaft. Rama drew again, holding the pose long enough for families to admire him.

The arrow crossed the field.

It entered Ravana’s painted chest.

Fire moved up the dry cloth. The central body brightened, then opened. One head exploded. Then another. Children counted as the faces disappeared.

One.

Two.

Three.

The craftsmen had finally solved the problem of making ten fit.

Above the crowd, Ravana’s heads opened one after another in flowers of fire. Kumbhakarna burned beside him, still holding a bowl. Surpanakha remained painted upon the stage, ugly enough to make her wound amusing.

The children cheered.

Rama was unmistakably good. Ravana was safely monstrous. Kumbhakarna was hungry. Surpanakha was ugly. Sita, smiling beside her husband, had nothing left to say.

The singers began the final refrain.

The metre was perfect.
