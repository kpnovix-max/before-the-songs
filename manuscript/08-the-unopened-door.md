# Chapter Eight

## The Unopened Door

*Some doors are opened for us. Others we must decide to walk through.*

The harbour launch came alongside before the naval ship had finished turning towards its berth.

Māran had been watching for it. He was holding the sealed report when the pilot climbed over the rail, bringing with him the smell of wet rope and the sour harbour water. The captain gave him the ordinary docking instructions first: thirty-one children aboard, one in need of a physician, no fever, a difficult passage through the southern reef, and a list that the receiving officer must sign before anyone was transferred from ship to quay.

Then he held out a second tablet.

“This goes to the king. Not through the morning registers. Take a runner now.”

The pilot glanced at the seal. “Is there trouble?”

“His daughter is aboard.”

The pilot looked towards the passengers, where a girl sat with a blue cloth folded on her lap. She was helping a younger boy untie a knot in his sandal. He had no idea that he was being observed.

“Which daughter?”

“The one they have been searching for.”

The pilot did not ask again.

He descended into the launch and ordered the oarsmen towards the inner landing. Māran watched the narrow boat pass between the harbour markers. Only when it was clear of the ship did he turn to the registrar.

“The other report?”

“Already copied for the harbour master. One hundred and seventy-five remaining on the island. The provisions order is attached.”

“Tell them it is not a request to be considered next week.”

The registrar, who had watched children disappear behind the island's houses whenever a strange sail appeared, nodded.

On the afterdeck, Analā had freed the sandal knot.

“You pull the short end,” she told Dev. “Otherwise you will spend the rest of your life married to that shoe.”

He considered the warning and put the shoe back on.

From the shore came the ordinary noises of a working port: a mallet striking a wedge, animals protesting at being unloaded, a woman demanding that two men stop arguing and move her baskets before the fish spoiled.

Lanka sounded alive.

It took Analā a moment to recognise that this was what troubled her.

***

Vibhishana was trying to persuade the treasury that a repaired well did not become a successful well merely because somebody had painted the stone around it.

His surviving son sat at the end of the table, arranging the inspection reports into three piles. Taranisena's right hand still carried pale marks where his own teeth had broken the skin during the hours he spent hiding in water. He no longer concealed the marks beneath his sleeve. On some mornings he could work without stopping to listen for noises nobody else had heard. On others, he moved his chair so that the door remained within sight.

The king did not ask which kind of morning this was. He had learned to notice without naming it.

A clerk explained that the northern district had received seven repaired wells.

“Seven where water runs?” Vibhishana asked.

“Seven repaired, Majesty.”

“I asked a different question.”

Taranisena moved one tablet from the first pile to the second.

“Four,” he said quietly.

The king looked up. “Four?”

“Three still draw saltwater. The wells have been repaired. The water has not.”

Vibhishana regarded the clerk.

“Then we have repaired three expensive holes.”

A small laugh escaped Taranisena. The king heard it and nearly smiled himself. He had learned not to turn the boy's rare laughter into a public event.

The messenger came in before the clerk could defend the holes.

He had crossed the outer courtyard at a run. One sandal was wet to the ankle. He bowed too quickly, then caught his breath with a hand against the doorframe.

“From Captain Māran, Majesty. The southern island.”

Vibhishana rose halfway from his chair.

“How many?”

“Two hundred and six living. Thirty-one aboard his vessel. He has ordered further ships for the rest.”

The king closed his eyes briefly. Two hundred and six names that had been carried in an official record as loss. He reached for the tablet.

“Has the harbour master been told? The stores?”

“Yes, Majesty. The requisition—”

“Then why are you here?”

The messenger placed the second tablet in his hand.

Vibhishana saw the name before he understood the line beneath it.

Analā.

Daughter of Vibhishana.

Alive. Identified by personal testimony and island register. Travelling aboard Māran's vessel.

His mind did what it had done for fourteen months whenever a witness offered hope. It searched for the mistake. There were other girls with the name. Names could be misheard. Registers could be copied badly. A frightened child might have been told to say anything that would get her aboard a safe ship.

“Who verified this?” he asked.

“Māran himself.”

“How?”

“The island leader's register. He spoke with her. She knew the family.”

Vibhishana read the line again.

His hand began to shake. He put the tablet down because he could not bear the possibility of dropping it.

“Where?”

“In the harbour, Majesty.”

“Where in the harbour?”

“The outer landing, I think. They will be coming in now.”

He looked at Taranisena.

His son was already standing. The boy had gone so pale that Vibhishana thought, absurdly, of calling for the physician.

“Your sister,” he said.

Taranisena opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

“Your sister,” the king said again, and the second time his voice did not sound like his own.

The messenger was still speaking. There were supplies to issue, two vessels to prepare, an inquiry to make into the previous report. Vibhishana heard the words, understood them, and could no longer remain in the room where they were being said.

He took two steps towards the door, turned back, and found himself looking for something he had lost.

“What is it, Majesty?” asked the clerk.

“My cloak.”

“It is on your shoulders.”

He looked down. The cloak was indeed there.

For the first time in many months, he laughed without first deciding whether it was appropriate.

Then he was crying.

He had been about to say something useful. The first relief transport would need a medical officer; the 175 children would require names entered against households, not merely columns marked *surviving*. He knew which warehouses held spare grain. He knew the harbour master's limits. For fourteen months, knowing what to do next had been the means by which he had kept moving.

Then a small sound came out of him, so broken and unfamiliar that the clerk nearest the door looked towards the messenger, as though somebody must have been hurt in the passage.

Vibhishana pressed his palm to his mouth.

He had walked out of his brother's palace convinced that a man could endure the loss of a family if he was right about the reason. He had crossed to a camp where every gesture of welcome reminded him that he had come from the other side. He had stood beside men drawing maps of the city where his wife and children slept. He had told himself that duty could not become duty only when it was painless.

He had believed it, even when the northern gate fell.

He had believed it while men called his brother a monster and looked to him for confirmation. He had believed it while learning that his wife had been identified among the dead. He had sat beside Taranisena when the boy woke screaming without knowing which wall surrounded him, and when the boy was quiet he had listened for the screaming anyway. The city had needed clean water. The bodies had needed names. The soldiers had needed wages. He had been given a crown and discovered that even grief must wait until the day’s work was done.

And at night, alone, he had tried to remember his daughter's face without imagining what fire or sea might have done to it. The effort had exhausted him more completely than governing.

Now he had been told that she had a face. That she had spoken to Māran. That she had been alive through every night he had feared otherwise.

Another sound escaped him. It rose sharply and broke into two short, helpless cries. He was breathing as a child breathes after running too far: a breath, a catch, a breath that could not quite arrive. He gripped the edge of the table with both hands, ashamed to discover that his knees would not hold him steadily.

No one in the room knew the etiquette for a weeping king.

The clerk with the water accounts lowered his head. The messenger half raised a hand, then dropped it. An older official went to close the door and stopped, because closing it seemed to admit that something disgraceful was happening. The physician, summoned for Taranisena often enough, could not have prescribed anything for this.

Vibhishana tried to tell them he was all right. The words came out as a small, thick grunt. He laughed at himself and began to cry harder.

For a moment there was no kingdom, no victorious prince, no explanation for the choices he had made. There was a tired boy inside the man who had made them, one who had gone on being brave long after he wanted somebody else to be brave for him.

He had tried. He had tried so terribly hard.

Taranisena came around the table.

Vibhishana caught his son against him. The boy was almost as tall as he was.

“She's here,” he said into his hair.

“I heard.”

“She's here.”

The boy held him, uncertain what to do with a father who had suddenly become lighter and less able to stand.

A clerk made a movement towards the tablets.

“Leave them,” Vibhishana said. “No, take the order for the children. Send the transports. Give the harbour master whatever he needs.”

He began to walk, then stopped again.

“Someone must tell the kitchens.”

“Tell them what, Majesty?”

He stared at the clerk. He had meant to say that Analā liked warm rice with toasted sesame, and the little tamarind cakes his wife used to forbid her to eat before supper. He remembered how she would hide one in her sleeve and how the grease betrayed her. He remembered a small voice asking whether the cut above his eyebrow still hurt, every time he returned from a journey, even after it had become a scar.

“Tell them,” he said, “that my daughter is coming home.”

He turned towards the harbour and did not wait for his escort to catch up.

***

When Analā stepped onto the quay, the crowd was watching the other children.

A woman had found a nephew she thought had died. She was holding him so tightly that the child had begun to protest. A registrar was trying to keep two groups separate while their names were checked. Dev stood by a bollard, peering into the faces of adults as though one might suddenly turn into his mother.

Analā bent to straighten the strap of his bundle.

Then the people on the quay began moving aside.

Her father came towards her without a guard in front of him.

The first thing she noticed was his hair. There was more grey in it than she remembered. The second was how his clothes hung at the shoulders. He was not old, but something had made him smaller inside them.

He stopped a few paces away.

“Analā?”

She smiled.

“Yes, Father.”

He crossed the remaining distance as if he had been holding himself back all his life. His arms closed around her. The force of it lifted her feet briefly from the wet stone.

She embraced him. She was glad he was alive. She was glad the voyage had ended. She was glad to find that his voice could still say her name in precisely the way she remembered.

But he was trembling.

At first she thought the trembling came from the hurry of reaching her. Then his arms tightened, and a noise escaped into her hair. He was trying to say her name. It came out in three small pieces, each separated by a gasp. The next sound was worse: a child's sharp, desperate wail from a grown man who seemed startled by his own voice.

Analā steadied him.

She had known a father who could turn an angry household into laughter, who let her inspect the little scar above his eye with the seriousness of a physician and who always pretended it hurt when she touched it. She had never known him to lose command of his breathing. Now his face was pressed against her shoulder, damp and hot, and he was shaking so violently that she placed one foot behind the other to take his weight.

A sailor looked away. A receiving clerk took off his cap, put it on, and took it off again. Two women standing beside a returned child stared openly; a guard caught their eyes and then found that he, too, was staring. Taranisena stood with one hand lifted, uncertain whether to help his father or leave him undisturbed. The entire quay seemed to be waiting for a person who knew what one did when a king cried like this.

No one knew.

Vibhishana pulled back, attempted a smile, saw his daughter looking at him, and began again. A small, breathless grunt, then another. His face crumpled. He covered his eyes with his hand and said he was sorry, although there was nothing for which he needed to apologise in that moment.

“It's all right,” Analā said.

She did not know if he heard her. She held him while he cried. She had crossed the sea expecting a man whom she could question. For a few minutes, instead, she was holding someone she had never met: her father without the careful strength he had worn in all her memories.

He seemed so terribly glad that she was alive.

Over his shoulder she saw the city behind him.

A banner hung above the harbour office. Its saffron cloth snapped against the pole. Somewhere behind it men were shouting Rama's name to the accompaniment of a drum.

Her father's hand moved to the back of her head as though he were still protecting it from the crowd.

“I thought—” he began.

He could not finish.

“I know,” she said.

He pulled back, searching her face with wet eyes. His old scar showed above the left eyebrow. She saw it. She didn't reach towards it.

He waited a heartbeat longer than he meant to. Then he smiled through his tears, embarrassed by an expectation she could not possibly have known he carried.

“You're taller.”

“So are you,” she said.

He laughed. “I was already tall.”

“Then perhaps it is the crown.”

He touched his head. He wasn't wearing the crown. For an instant he looked confused; then they both smiled.

He wanted to tell her that he had looked for her every day. He wanted to take back every morning when the work of the kingdom had distracted him from looking. He wanted to ask about the island, about her food, her sleeping place, the people who had held her when she cried. He wanted to tell her that the worst hours were finished.

Instead he said, “Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Were you ill?”

“Sometimes, when the rains came. Nothing serious.”

“Did they feed you?”

She looked at him.

“Very well.”

“Who looked after you?”

“A woman named Mēni. Her daughter Ilā.”

He nodded rapidly, committing the names to memory.

“We shall thank them. Properly. Whatever they require—”

“They didn't ask for anything.”

“Then all the more reason.”

He reached for her hand. She allowed him to take it. Beside them Taranisena stood so quietly she had almost missed him.

Analā moved towards her brother before anybody could introduce them.

For a moment they only stared. He had been a boy she could outrun. Now he had long wrists and a serious face. When she hugged him, he held his breath, then let it out very slowly against her shoulder.

“You're alive,” she said.

“So are you.”

As they walked towards the receiving carriage she looked from one brother to the other.

“And Arun?” she asked. “And Mother?”

Taranisena's face changed. Vibhishana reached for the carriage door as though it had suddenly grown heavy.

“Your mother died during the fighting,” he said. “We found her afterwards.”

Analā nodded once. She had feared this, though fear had left room for a small, obstinate hope.

“And Arun?”

“We found something,” Vibhishana said. “We will tell you. I promise we won't leave you without the account.”

She looked at their faces and understood enough not to ask on the quay, with children being called by name all around them.

A little later Taranisena said something about how she had always been better at untying knots than he was. She laughed, and this time the laugh was unguarded.

Vibhishana heard it. His face filled with hope.

***

There were three roads from the harbour to the palace. Analā remembered two.

The carriage took the broadest. It passed a new drainage channel, repaired shops and a public well where women were filling copper vessels without queuing. A school had reopened in the old grain exchange. The children emerging from it wore ink on their fingers. Their arguments about multiplication sounded very much like the children on the island.

Her father pointed these things out with quiet pride. He had helped make them possible. She asked about the water and listened when he explained the new cistern arrangements. It was easier to talk about pipes than about families.

Then they passed the market where her mother had once bought flowers from a woman with three missing teeth.

The stall was gone. In its place stood a painted board showing Ravana with ten heads, each mouth open in a different expression of rage. Below it, a procession of soldiers with red and saffron cloths was marching towards a new shrine. Two of them were laughing so hard at their own chanting that they struck a fruit seller's baskets as they passed.

The seller bent to gather oranges rolling across the road.

Analā watched until a corner took him away.

“Has the market moved?” she asked.

“Some of it. That quarter was badly damaged.”

“Not the flower stall.”

“Which flower stall?”

She told him. He did not remember it.

He had remembered the wells, the schools, the warehouses. He had forgotten the woman with three missing teeth.

Analā looked out again.

The city had been cleaned. It had not been put back.

***

Her bedroom smelled of cedar and newly washed linen. Someone had placed a brass bowl of jasmine on the window ledge. The old painted panels remained along the inner wall, but the largest had been scraped. The red figures of musicians had become pale shadows. A different painting stood where the family portrait had hung: a radiant Rama above the city, with the victorious host arranged beneath him.

An attendant explained that the room had been prepared as soon as news came from the harbour.

“You must be exhausted, Princess. Let us take your clothes.”

Analā had already set her bundle down and begun loosening the knot.

“I can do it.”

“Of course you can.” The attendant smiled and continued to reach for it.

Analā yielded because the woman seemed genuinely distressed at being unable to help.

The bath was warm. The water smelled faintly of vetiver. Analā closed her eyes and let herself enjoy it. She had spent fourteen months bathing when water was available, heating it when wood was spare, taking turns when the youngest children had been washed. Here somebody poured the water before she asked.

Afterwards an attendant brought a comb and stood behind her chair.

Analā remembered Ilā tugging at the same knots with a wooden tooth that had broken off the comb years ago. The girls would quarrel about it until Mēni took the comb away and told them both to stop making their own lives difficult.

“Does that hurt?” the attendant asked.

“No,” Analā said. “I was thinking.”

She slept deeply at first. The bed was soft. The room was quiet. That surprised her more than anything else.

In the night something rattled softly against the shutter. She turned, still half asleep, towards the place where Ilā would have lain on her mat.

“That's only the wind,” she began, in the impatient whisper she used when Ilā mistook ordinary noises for visitors.

There was nobody beside the bed. The space held a low table, a beautifully carved chest, and a folded blanket that had never been needed.

Analā kept her face turned that way for a little while. On the island Ilā would have argued that it was not the wind and then fallen asleep before Analā could prove it. The thought almost made her smile. Instead she lay awake, listening to a room so quiet that every small movement belonged to her alone.

In the morning she woke before the servants, reached for the broom she expected to find beside the door, and discovered that the floor had already been swept.

***

The king had imagined a homecoming as a series of returns.

Analā would return to her room, her favourite foods, her father's council courtyard. She would find the scar above his eye, demand to know whether it still hurt, ask if he had been careful. She would complain about his absence and then forgive it because she was in his arms again. He had rehearsed these small possibilities on nights when he did not permit himself to imagine her alive.

Instead she thanked people.

She thanked the cooks for things they had made all her childhood. She thanked servants for bringing water. She thanked him when he offered to show her the restored gardens. She rarely complained about anything.

He told himself that this was what suffering had done: it had made her cautious, older, too polite to demand the affection she deserved.

He was careful not to question her about the evacuation until she was ready.

One afternoon he invited her to sit beside him while the household carpenter repaired a door hinge. He had hoped she might remember the joke about a particularly determined old door, the one that left the scar. He found himself touching his eyebrow as the carpenter worked.

Analā watched the hinge being fitted.

“On the island we'd turn the pin upside down,” she said. “The weight would sit better.”

The carpenter considered it. “It might.”

“Then try it,” Vibhishana said.

The pin fitted. The door stopped scraping the floor.

His daughter looked pleased. He was pleased to have pleased her.

He almost made the old joke about doors being violent creatures. But the carpenter was bowing and two officials had entered, each carrying tablets about harbour dues, and the moment passed.

***

Taranisena told her about Arun on the sixth morning.

It happened in the courtyard beside the southern kitchen. Analā had asked twice, once on the quay and once over supper. Each time somebody had answered that they would speak when there was time. She understood why people postponed difficult things. She also understood that postponement could become a way of never saying them.

Her brother had brought the wooden tray on which she used to sort shells by colour. Only two shells remained.

“Father thinks you should hear it from us,” he said.

“Then tell me.”

He set the tray down. His right hand was trembling, and she noticed the faded marks upon it for the first time.

“People said Mother went back into the city after the boats left,” he said. “There was a boatman who saw her taking a girl to the harbour. Father thought it might have been you. Nobody could prove it.”

“It was me.”

“I know that now.”

Analā waited. Taranisena looked at the doorway, then away from it.

“I wasn't with Mother. I didn't see where she went. I only knew the fighting was coming closer. I got into the drainage place because there was nowhere else I thought would hold.”

“I know where they found you.”

He nodded.

“Later there were people who said Mother was looking for the boys. Other people remembered seeing her near the western houses. I can't tell you what she knew or where she went. Nobody can.”

He placed one of the remaining shells beside the other.

“They searched the western quarter for Arun. There had been a fire near the holding rooms. They found children there.”

He stopped.

Analā waited.

“One of them was holding a horse.”

The air in the courtyard seemed to grow thin.

“A wooden one?”

“Yes.”

“With a broken ear?”

“The left.”

She remembered standing in an evacuation boat, nearly calling her brother's name to a boy whose horse had both ears. She remembered sitting down because the boy was not Arun. For fourteen months she had carried that small disappointment as evidence that the real Arun could still be somewhere else.

“Was it his?”

“Father knew it. So did the woman who carved it. They could not recognise the child.”

She looked at the shells.

The one with a brown stripe had always been Arun's favourite. He said it looked like a horse seen from above, which was an absurd claim, and would become furious when anyone disagreed.

“Did Father bury him?”

“Yes.”

“And Mother?”

“Yes.”

Her brother reached for her hand. She turned hers over and held his. She noticed the small tooth marks on his skin and did not ask how they came to be there. Sometimes a question could wait because the person beside you was already answering one that hurt.

“She told me she would come after me,” Analā said. “She said she'd find you both first.”

Taranisena looked at her with an expression she could not quite name. Until this moment, he had not known what their mother had promised at the harbour.

“She was trying,” he said.

Analā nodded. There had been no certainty in the promise. Only a woman insisting that she would try.

She did not cry immediately. That night, when the lamps had been extinguished and the large soft bed held her entirely alone, she remembered Arun carrying the horse through the house by its neck. She remembered scolding him because the remaining ear was splintering his fingers. She remembered him saying he would never need another horse.

She turned her face into the pillow so that the attendant sleeping beyond the screen would not hear her.

In the morning she asked where the grave was. Taranisena took her there.

She left the brown-striped shell beside the marker.

There was no further search to order. That was the cruel kindness of the broken ear: it had given her brother a place to mourn.

***

The relief vessels sailed for the southern island before the end of the week.

Analā watched the provision sacks being checked at the harbour: grain, pulses, oil, salt, cloth and medicine, enough for three weeks with a fourth held in reserve. The registrar counted the names of the one hundred and seventy-five children still under Sēnan's care. The captain who would take the supplies asked whether she wished to send a message.

She dictated one for Mēni, and another for Ilā.

She told them that the ship had reached safely. She told them about Taranisena. She did not write about Arun yet. Ilā would recognise the missing name and wait until Analā was ready to explain.

She almost asked after the little wooden boat, then crossed the question out. Ilā would look after it.

A naval official told her an inquiry had been opened into the earlier recovery voyage. The older crew's report would be examined alongside the island register. The allegations about the officer's conduct were recorded, but witnesses had offered contradictory accounts, and the evidence did not yet establish grounds to detain anyone.

Analā asked whether Sēnan's account was included.

“Yes.”

“Did anyone ask the children?”

“We're preparing to.”

She considered this.

“Ask the ones who remember the dance,” she said.

The official looked at her, uncertain whether she was describing entertainment or an offence.

She did not explain.

***

The palace had become solemn in ways no renovation could repair.

Analā remembered her uncle sitting at a crowded table while the physician corrected his medical assumptions, the chamberlain corrected his understanding of doors, and sometimes a cook corrected his estimate of how quickly people could eat. Ravana would complain that the kingdom had become a conspiracy against his intelligence. People laughed. They argued. He listened. And when he decided, the room moved.

Vibhishana's court was quieter.

It functioned. Orders were signed. Petitions were heard. People received answers. But the room held itself as though laughter were something that required permission.

At one meeting a junior clerk placed a tax tablet in the wrong pile. Vibhishana corrected him sharply. The clerk went pale. Nobody joked that the kingdom would collapse under the burden of misplaced pottery. Analā could almost hear her uncle saying it.

Then an allied commander entered without waiting to be announced.

He wore the saffron sash of the northern coalition and spoke as if he were addressing a colleague in a marketplace.

“Your patrols have stopped our men at the western gate again,” he said.

Vibhishana set down his stylus.

“They stopped men without permits carrying grain purchased for the relief stores.”

“Our men helped give you those stores.”

“And they will receive the permits when the purchases are entered properly.”

The commander laughed. It was not a friendly sound.

“Will you make me ask the northern prince to explain this to you?”

Nobody moved. Vibhishana's jaw tightened almost invisibly.

“The permits will be prepared this afternoon,” he said. “The grain will remain accounted for.”

“Good.”

The commander left without bowing.

Vibhishana turned to the clerk with the misplaced tablet.

“Begin again,” he said, in a voice that was too firm for the small mistake.

Analā looked at the men around the table. They sat straighter. They were showing respect, or perhaps performing it. She could no longer tell.

That evening her father came to her room with an old volume of riddles. He remembered that she used to enjoy finding errors in the answers.

They sat together. She found one and laughed. For a moment his face became the face she remembered.

He read another.

She answered it without difficulty.

He was so delighted that she felt sorry for him.

***

The first time she saw the officer, she knew him by the movement before she saw his face.

Two men stood opposite each other in a square near the eastern market. They extended their arms as if about to embrace, but held their hands an inch apart. The drummer began a dull, slow beat. Their hips rolled with almost painful deliberation, shoulders rigid, eyes fixed on each other. Then the beat changed. The men snapped their heads away, closed their eyes in an expression of bliss, and opened them again in fierce, theatrical anger. The movement repeated, too solemn to be harmless, too ridiculous to be solemn. Their companions shouted in approval.

Analā remembered the same movement on Sēnan's shore. She remembered children breathing through their mouths behind the houses, trying not to make a sound.

Then she heard the laugh.

He wore a saffron tunic dark with sweat beneath the arms, a long vermilion mark drawn down his forehead and nose, and a belt from which a sword hung with its hilt polished by constant touching. Betel had stained the corners of his mouth red. He drank from a small cup, wiped his lips with his forearm, and spat against the base of a wall on which an older painting of Lankan musicians could still be made out beneath a new layer of whitewash.

A fruit seller was attempting to move his cart past the dancers. The officer seized his wrist and pulled him into the square.

“Dance,” he said.

The seller tried. His arms trembled in the required position. The officer placed one hand against the man's hip and made him move it. When the man stumbled, the officer stopped the drums with a gesture. Everybody fell silent.

“No,” he said softly. “You don't understand what you've been given.”

He shouted Rama's name into the seller's face, then laughed at the frightened attempt to repeat it. He waved the drums back to life. The seller danced badly while oranges rolled from his cart and were crushed under the feet of spectators trying to get out of the way.

A boy reached to gather the fruit. The officer caught him by the shoulder and pushed him back against the wheel.

“Let your father show gratitude first.”

A woman beside Analā touched her sleeve.

“Don't stare.”

“His fruit—”

“Don't,” the woman said again, without looking at her.

When the officer tired of the game, he let the seller go. He spat red betel juice onto the broken rind of an orange, then set off with his men, the drums still beating.

Analā found the seller kneeling beside his cart. She helped gather fruit that could be saved. He thanked her without meeting her eyes. His son continued watching the street, as though the man might return.

Later she asked a guard who the officer was.

“A northern officer,” he said. “Still attached to the coalition stores.”

“Does he always behave that way?”

The guard looked towards the street.

“Often enough.”

She thought of telling her father. She was sure people already had.

***

By the second week Analā knew which parts of the palace welcomed her and which expected something from her.

The kitchens welcomed her. They gave her onions to cut when she asked and pretended not to notice how badly she did the first one. The younger attendants welcomed her, though some were horrified when she carried her own washing. Taranisena welcomed her when the day had been easy and sat quietly with her when it had not.

The audience rooms expected a princess.

She was given clothing suitable for ceremonies. She was told which doorway to use when foreign officers were present. A well-meaning chamberlain advised her to avoid speaking with the harbour labourers without an escort; the king's enemies might twist such conduct into a criticism of the royal household.

She had been told on the island not to wander alone at night because the tide could trap her against the rocks. That warning had a practical reason she understood.

Here a woman was troubled by the sight of Analā sweeping the terrace at dawn.

“Someone might see you, Princess.”

Analā leaned upon the broom.

“Then they'll know the terrace is clean.”

The woman laughed despite herself, then glanced towards the corridor to make certain nobody important had heard.

Analā resumed sweeping. She was beginning to understand that the broom was not the issue.

Near the harbour she met a woman who traded rice and oil from a stall she had inherited from her husband. She was sixteen, had an infant tied against her back and negotiated with two merchants twice her age without consulting anybody. Analā admired the calm way she refused a bad offer.

The trader asked whether Analā had ever kept accounts.

“Only for rice and children.”

“Then you have kept the difficult kind.”

They laughed together. Analā bought a length of blue cord and carried it herself.

When she entered the palace with it, an attendant hurried to relieve her of the parcel. She surrendered it and watched the woman carry a handful of cord as though it were a ceremonial object.

On the island, Analā had learned to repair nets because the fishing families had no patience with a pair of hands that could only count bowls. Mēni showed her the first knot. Ilā laughed at the first net she mended, whose holes had become smaller than the fish they were supposed to catch. By the second rainy season, Analā could replace a torn edge, splice a line and reinforce the corner of a small sail. The work was never noble or ignoble; it was either sound enough to put to sea or it wasn't.

In Lanka she found a net-maker whose apprentices were behind on an order for the coastal boats. He gave her a short length of damaged net to repair while he went to fetch a shuttle.

She was nearly finished when someone addressed her as Princess.

The net-maker became a different man. He took the net gently from her, declared it perfect without checking a single knot, and told his apprentice to bring payment from the best coin jar.

“You haven't looked at it,” Analā said.

“I don't need to, Princess.”

“Then how do you know it's good?”

“Your Highness would not have done poor work.”

She wondered what the man would have said if her knots had opened under a fishing boat in rough water.

She left the payment untouched.

At the back of the palace kitchen, a girl of thirteen called Valli kept the morning purchases. Her father had taught her the weights, and the head cook entrusted her with the buying of vegetables, pulses and fresh herbs. She had three suppliers and a fourth whom she refused to use because he mixed damp grain into dry sacks. When a merchant complained about her age, Valli made him weigh the sack again, watched the scale fall short and sent him away without raising her voice.

Analā asked how she had learned to argue with grown men.

“I don't argue with them,” Valli said. “I tell them what a sack weighs.”

She was betrothed, though the household arrangements were not yet settled, and she spoke of marriage in the same practical tone she used for storeroom repairs. Analā had met other girls of thirteen and fourteen already running family stalls or helping support younger siblings. Nobody considered their competence remarkable. Only the princess, apparently, must be kept from carrying a parcel.

The next day Analā returned to the net-maker through his side entrance. She asked him to put a basket of damaged netting before her, leave her alone to work and pay her only for repairs he would trust aboard his own son's boat. If he chose not to employ her, she would find someone else.

He stood looking at her for some time.

“Your father could give you the whole workshop,” he said.

“I haven't asked him.”

He touched the last repair she had made, worked the knot against his thumbnail, then reached for another net.

“Try this corner,” he said. “And don't pull it so tight.”

Her first payment was small. The net-maker deducted the cost of a shuttle she had worn down. She was delighted by the deduction, because it meant he was finally treating her like an apprentice who could be corrected.

Later an official offered to pay her twice the going rate for canvas repairs at the harbour stores. She refused when she discovered he meant to charge the difference to a royal hospitality account.

“You're making it difficult to pay you,” the official complained.

“I'm making it possible,” she said.

She kept the few ordinary coins in a cloth pouch with the small savings Mēni had once insisted she accept for helping with the island's fishing nets. Now and then she asked sailors the price of a deck passage to nearby ports. They assumed she was arranging transport for one of the returning families. She did not correct them.

***

The second time she saw the officer, he was sitting at the entrance of a tavern beside a boy selling boiled groundnuts.

The boy could not have been more than twelve. His mother stood a little way off, watching her cooking pot while customers gathered. The officer demanded a bowl without payment. The boy hesitated. The officer dipped two fingers into the hot nuts and dropped them on the ground.

“Did you hear me bless your stall?” he asked.

The boy looked confused.

“Then say it.”

“Say what?”

The officer leaned close and shouted the chant into his face.

The boy repeated it. His voice was thin.

The boy said the chant, trying to get the words right. It did not satisfy the officer.

“Again. With gratitude.”

The boy's mother came forward. “He's only trying to sell his food.”

The officer gave her a long look. He asked whether the boy belonged to one of the Rakshasa households, and when she didn't answer he smiled.

“Then he's fortunate we're teaching him manners.”

He tipped the bowl into the drainage runnel. The groundnuts scattered among greasy water and the waste from the tavern. He caught the boy by the back of his tunic and pushed him down to his knees.

“Pick up your earnings.”

The mother moved towards them. One of the officer's companions took a step into her path. She stopped. The boy reached into the runnel with both hands, his face turned away from the smell.

The officer laughed, drank, and released him. His companions followed him up the street. Only after they had gone did the mother pull the boy away from the drain and begin wiping his hands on the edge of her sari. He kept apologising for having lost the food.

Analā paid the mother for the entire spoiled pot. The woman resisted at first, then accepted when Analā said the money was payment for food already prepared, not charity.

The boy looked at Analā's clean hands.

“Why didn't anybody tell him to stop?” he asked.

She had no answer that would have helped him. She stayed while his mother scrubbed the red marks from his neck and waited until his breathing settled.

The incident was small enough to be reduced to two lines in an official complaint. It remained with her all afternoon.

***

Vibhishana tried to arrange a supper at which Analā might meet the children of court families she had known before the war.

Most were older, some married, some living with relatives far from the city. A girl Analā remembered chasing through the palace passages now came with her husband, who asked polite questions about the island and then explained what a hardship it must have been to live without proper attendants.

Analā said she had been happy there.

He smiled, interpreting this as courage.

At another table Vibhishana tried to repeat an old family joke about the amount of honey his daughter used to steal. She remembered it. She laughed. He looked relieved.

Then somebody mentioned the city's new victory festival.

The conversation changed. People praised the repaired shrines and the procession that would pass through Ravana's old cultural quarter. A court musician spoke carefully about which songs were now appropriate for public performance.

Analā listened.

Her father leaned towards her.

“Are you enjoying yourself?”

“Yes,” she said. “The food is lovely.”

He smiled at the answer. She had meant it sincerely. It was not the answer to the question he was asking.

After the guests departed he offered to walk with her in the old garden.

“Perhaps tomorrow,” she said.

He stood alone after she had gone, looking at the cup she had used.

He could remember the shape of her small fingers around cups like it. He could not remember when she had last reached for his hand without being asked.

***

The pink flowers survived.

Not all the trees had survived the war, and some walls were stained black where fire had found the eaves. But the garden that had once held Sita remained green. Ashoka flowers fell onto the paths, pink against the earth. A small pool reflected the open sky. Somewhere water ran through a channel Ravana's gardeners had designed long before the fighting.

Trijata met Analā beside the old writing table.

She was older than Analā remembered. Or perhaps Analā had never noticed her age before. Her hands were busy tying a damaged branch to a support. She asked Analā to hold the string.

“You're doing that wrong,” Analā said.

“Then tell me how to do it correctly.”

Analā showed her. They worked without ceremony.

Trijata remembered her as the daughter who had once brought a message to the woman living in the garden. Analā remembered Sita's stillness, her way of asking questions without revealing which answers she wanted, and the fact that the women around her had not behaved like prisoners.

“Was she unhappy?” Analā asked.

“Sometimes. Sometimes she laughed. She read. She argued. She missed people. Captivity isn't a single expression worn all day.”

They walked beneath a canopy of pink flowers. The gate was visible beyond the pool, open enough for a gardener to push his basket through without touching the latch.

Analā had heard different stories about how her uncle had kept the northern princess. On the island, the smaller children sometimes called Sita a guest. The word was easier to say than captive. Analā knew that a person could be treated with kindness and still not be free to go where she wished.

“Uncle admitted he had been wrong?” she asked.

“Yes,” said Trijata. “He said it without asking anything in return.”

“And he opened the gate before the fighting ended?”

“He did.”

“Did she go?”

“Not then.”

They sat beneath an ashoka tree. Trijata told her how Sita had calculated the meaning of every road: leaving alone, returning to Rama, remaining for another day. Men had attached honour, shame and allegiance to journeys she might make. The northern prince's army would read one meaning into her arrival; people in Lanka would read another. Her private wishes would be discussed as evidence in arguments she had not started.

“She had been taken,” Trijata said. “After that, even the manner of her release ceased to belong entirely to her.”

Analā listened, her fingers resting on the edge of the garden bench.

“But the gate was open?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“And she knew?”

“She stood beside it. I was there.”

Analā looked down the path towards the patch of sunlight outside the entrance. She had never seen the northern woman as entirely helpless; she remembered Sita asking difficult questions and accepting no answer simply because it was spoken by a king.

“I don't understand it,” she said at last.

“You may, one day.”

Trijata wasn't being dismissive. She had lived long enough to know that explanations could be convincing without becoming comfortable. Analā helped her gather the cut branches and left with a flower caught in the fold of her sleeve.

***

During the third week Vibhishana found his daughter in the kitchen courtyard, laughing with a young worker who had dropped an entire pan of sweets onto the stones.

Analā had flour on her cheek. She was helping rescue the sweets that had not touched the ground. The worker said something about how kings must surely have better food than this. Analā answered that kings sometimes ate worse, because nobody dared tell them when the cook had burned it.

They both laughed.

Vibhishana stood where they could not see him.

It was her laugh. The one he had remembered through the fourteen months. He had imagined hearing it again at their family table, hearing it because he had made some foolish joke or she had remembered a game. Instead it filled a kitchen where she did not need him at all.

He felt ashamed of his disappointment.

He went in and greeted them. The worker straightened at once, eyes lowered. Analā's laughter faded. She stood and wiped her cheek.

“What are you making?” he asked.

“Sweets.”

“I gathered that from the evidence.”

The worker looked at the flour on the floor and did not laugh.

Vibhishana tried to remember how Ravana had once made such rooms feel easy. His elder brother would have blamed the floor for getting in the way of the pan. Or demanded that the cook promote the surviving sweets because they had shown courage under attack. People would have laughed even if the king was having a terrible day.

Vibhishana smiled at his daughter.

“Save me one,” he said.

“I will.”

He went away. Behind him the kitchen returned to its ordinary noises, but not to the laughter he had interrupted.

***

The officer appeared a third time during the preparations for the victory procession.

This time Analā watched him from a shaded passage beside the old hall of instruments. His men had brought drums and were rehearsing the same ugly movement of outstretched arms and rolling hips. A man in work clothes was passing with a wooden case of flutes. The officer called him over and asked him to join the chant.

The man said he needed to deliver the instruments.

The officer blocked his way.

“Then deliver them after you have thanked the prince.”

“I have work.”

“So did Ravana's men. We finished their work for them.”

The officer laughed and kicked the flute case. It struck the paving and opened. Two flutes rolled out; one cracked beneath a boot.

Behind the man came an elderly musician with a veena wrapped in cloth. He halted when he saw the flutes.

“Those belong to the old school,” he said. “They are being taken for repair.”

The officer drew back a corner of the cloth. A polished resonator caught the afternoon light. He looked at the old man's face, then at the instrument.

“Does this play the king with ten heads?”

“It plays what the hands know,” the musician said.

The officer set his boot on the cloth-wrapped neck. The old man made a small movement forward and stopped when two soldiers closed in beside him. Under the weight of the boot there was a dry crack, slight but unmistakable. The old man's mouth opened; no sound came from it.

“Then teach your hands a better song,” the officer said.

He went away laughing. His drummer struck three loud beats as they rounded the corner.

Analā helped collect the flutes after the men had gone. The musician knelt beside the veena, lifting the cloth as carefully as if he expected the wood to feel pain. He didn't weep. He asked for string and a small piece of clean linen, and he asked it twice before Analā realised he was speaking to her.

She brought both.

“Can it be repaired?” she asked.

“The wood, perhaps.” The musician ran a finger along the crack. “It belonged to my teacher.”

A woman from the doorway told Analā that complaints had been lodged against the officer for months. Once he had been held after laying hands on a Rakshasa woman in a crowded street; his unit had obtained his release while statements were still being collected. There were other complaints of damage, threats and stolen goods. The woman did not know whether the list was twenty reports long or merely felt that way to those who lived nearby.

The musician wrapped the veena again. He thanked Analā, then carried it away against his chest.

Analā had seen enough to recognise the pattern: the humiliation, the laugh, the demand that someone join an act of devotion, the sudden ruin of an ordinary person's work. She had seen it on the island and here, again and again.

She asked a palace officer whether the complaint about the first recovery ship had progressed.

“Statements are being taken,” he said. “There are difficulties with the commanding formation.”

“Is that man named?”

“Yes.”

“Then you know where he is.”

“Knowing where a man is and having proof sufficient to discipline him are not the same thing.”

She looked at the broken flute on the table beside him. Someone had brought it in as evidence and then apparently decided it was too small a matter to interrupt more important work.

“Perhaps ask the people who watched him break that,” she said.

He told her he would.

She believed that he meant it. She was less certain he would do it before another flute was broken.

***

At the end of the fourth week the king asked his daughter whether she wanted new dresses made for the procession.

She said the clothes she owned were sufficient.

“Of course,” he answered. “I thought you might enjoy choosing the cloth.”

“I would rather see the harbour stores.”

He tried not to show that this disappointed him.

“They'll let you in,” he said. “Take an escort.”

“Why?”

“The streets are not always safe.”

“Then perhaps the streets need an escort more than I do.”

He smiled because he recognised the sharpness of the remark. For a moment it reminded him of the girl who used to argue that if rain were allowed to fall on the palace roof, it should also be allowed into the kitchen because the kitchen was hotter.

But he was tired.

“I am asking you to be careful.”

“I am careful.”

He wanted to say that careful girls could still be hurt. He knew she understood that better than most people in the palace.

Instead he said, “Take someone who knows the roads.”

She agreed.

He watched her leave, relieved by the agreement and troubled by how little pleasure it gave him.

***

The days that followed were not unhappy.

Taranisena showed her the rebuilt western terrace. A gardener gave her a cutting of the jasmine that had grown beside her mother's room. She learned that the harbour master had personally checked the second relief voyage. More children had returned from the island. Some had found surviving relatives. Others were being placed with households that agreed to receive them without separating siblings.

Ilā's letters arrived with the supply ship. The first was mostly complaints about the weather and Mēni's cooking. Then came a list of children who had left, and the words of a new verse Ilā was teaching the youngest ones. The verse said that Ravana had stood on the cliff and promised not to leave until every boat had escaped.

Analā read it twice.

She had waved to her uncle. She did not know whether he had seen her. She knew he had remained to defend the city. She did not know what he had promised anyone on the cliff.

She thought of correcting the verse. Then she imagined Ilā standing among children who still woke at night asking for their mothers. She folded the letter instead.

When her father found her smiling over the page, he asked what it contained.

“News from the island.”

“Good news?”

“Yes.”

He offered to send gifts.

“They need proper supplies more than presents,” she said.

“Then we'll send supplies.”

He meant it. He was capable of generous acts and, increasingly, of making them happen. It made the distance between them harder to name.

***

Analā returned to the pink garden near the end of the fifth week.

Trijata was replacing a broken latch on the gate. The new wood was pale against the old, and a bowl of iron pins stood at her feet. She had been told that a carpenter would come tomorrow. She preferred to do the work before the next rain.

“Hold this,” she said when Analā arrived, handing her the latch without ceremony.

Analā held it. They worked for a little while, setting the pin and testing the fit.

“I've been thinking about Sita,” Analā said.

“I hoped you might.”

“She was free to go.”

“She was free of your uncle's order to stay. Those are not always the same thing.”

“I know what you told me.”

Trijata set down the hammer. Analā looked through the gate towards the road beyond the garden. There was nothing remarkable there: two women walking with baskets, a boy leading a goat, sunlight moving across the paving stones.

“I would have left,” Analā said.

Trijata turned towards her.

“Left where?”

“If I had been Sita. When the gate opened.”

“That is easily said by someone who was not in her place.”

“Yes,” Analā said. “But I'm not saying it because I think there would have been no cost. I know there would have been. She might have lost a husband's trust or a kingdom's welcome. People might have called her names. She might have had to work for food in a strange place.”

“And you think she should simply have accepted all that?”

“I think she could have chosen to accept it. She could also have chosen to stay. But if she stayed only because men had decided what leaving would mean, then they were still deciding for her.”

Trijata watched the road. “Words spoken about a woman can make a road dangerous. You have seen how little it takes.”

“I have. I couldn't leave the island when the wrong ships came. If we went outside, someone might take us. I know there are times when a road really is closed.”

Analā touched the newly fitted latch.

“But an open road can be frightening without being closed. I don't want to learn that fear is the same thing as a lock.”

Trijata considered this for a long time. She remembered Sita standing by the same gate, understanding clearly what every choice would cost. She was not prepared to call that understanding cowardice. The younger woman beside her was not asking her to.

“People will still judge you,” Trijata said.

“Yes.”

“And sometimes their judgement will have consequences.”

“Yes.”

“Then what have you gained?”

Analā eased the gate open. The repaired latch rose cleanly from its catch.

“The chance to be answerable for a choice I made,” she said.

She went a few steps beyond the gate to check whether it would swing against the outer wall. It didn't. She turned back, smiling at how little work had been needed to make the hinge run true.

Trijata watched her return, carrying the hammer. She wondered what the girl might choose when the choices grew costly, and found that the question troubled her less than she expected.

***

The king remembered the evening of his daughter's birth more vividly than the day he was crowned.

It had rained. His wife had become furious with the midwife for insisting that a prince must remain outside the room, then furious with him for entering without washing his hands. He remembered washing them twice in a bowl already clouded with soap, and the astonishing weight of a very small child whose eyes would not close.

She had seized his finger. He had thought, with the foolishness of new fathers, that this meant she recognised him.

Years later she would inspect the scar above his eyebrow and lecture him on being careful with doors. She had climbed onto his knees while he read court reports, asking why a tax collector needed so many numbers when everybody knew how much rice there was in the storeroom. She had loved him without knowing anything about his disagreements with his brother.

Now she sat across from him at supper, listening politely while he described the reopening of the southern market.

He had made real progress. He had restored roads, water, food, wages, schools. He was proud of those things, and he wanted her to be proud of him.

She asked whether the market's old musicians had returned.

“Some have. Others left.”

“Are they allowed to play the old songs?”

He took a moment before answering.

“Most music is not a matter for the government.”

“That's not what I asked.”

He looked at her. She did not sound angry.

“There are sensitivities,” he said. “People lost family on both sides. Some celebrations would be misunderstood.”

She nodded and ate a little rice.

For the rest of the meal he spoke about ordinary things: the weather, the gardens, the repairs to her old writing room.

When she left, she thanked him for supper.

He sat with the untouched dish he had ordered especially for her. It had been her favourite. She had eaten two mouthfuls.

He told himself she was still learning to be home.

***

On a morning in the sixth week, the net-maker returned three lengths of repaired net to Analā. He had inspected them in front of her, tugging hard at the joints until her fingers hurt from watching.

“Sound,” he said.

He paid her the agreed amount, withholding one small coin because she had used too much new cord on the first repair.

She argued about the cord. He showed her where it had been wasted. She conceded the point and asked whether he had any other work.

“There's always work,” he said. “Whether people pay for it is another matter.”

She walked back to the palace with the coins warm in her hand. At a stall near the harbour she asked the fare for an ordinary passenger travelling without a cabin or attendants. The boatman named three possible destinations and two prices, depending on the wind. Analā listened, thanked him and went away. He assumed she was making inquiries for someone else.

That afternoon Taranisena found her putting her shell collection back into its old wooden tray. He asked whether she was giving it away.

“I'm trying to remember which one was yours.”

“None of them. You said I wasn't fit to own seashells.”

“I was right.”

He laughed. She gave him the brown-striped shell's companion and kept the other one, which had a narrow white seam through its middle.

Later an attendant saw a small bundle of plain clothes on her chest and offered to put them away. Analā said she would manage. The attendant shrugged; the princess had developed several odd habits since coming home.

Vibhishana was reading a report from the western fields when Analā came to his workroom. He put it aside at once. He had learned to do this without appearing to be waiting for her, although waiting was what he did each day.

“Come,” he said. “Sit. They've finally learned that fields don't produce grain merely because we list them as cultivated.”

She smiled.

He smiled back, hoping she might tease him about expensive holes or the number of tablets required to grow one grain of rice.

“May I ask you something about Uncle?” she said.

“Of course.”

“When did you last speak to him?”

Vibhishana lowered his eyes to the table. He could remember the precise arrangement of the chairs in his brother's room. He could remember a cup left untouched. There were aspects of that day he had rehearsed so often that he no longer knew whether he remembered the words or the account he had made of them since.

“Before I crossed,” he said.

“Crossed where?”

“To Rama's camp.”

“Then it's true.”

“Yes.”

He waited for an accusation. She was looking at him attentively, her hands quiet in her lap.

“I had hoped,” he began, “that you might ask me why.”

“I would like to understand why. But first I need to know what happened.”

She had spoken as gently as she might have asked a fisherman which rope was holding a boat. Her father found the calmness harder to meet than any charge.

“Did you give Rama information that helped his men defeat our army?”

“Yes.”

“What did you tell them?”

He spoke about the salt approaches, the city's vulnerabilities, the limits of its stores, the habits of its commanders. He told her that some things could be known by any observant opponent and others could only have come from a member of the family. He did not disguise the distinction.

“And you thought that would save people?” she asked.

“I believed continuing the war as your uncle intended would kill many more. I believed Sita should never have been taken. I thought that by ending his rule I might save what could still be saved.”

“You helped them defeat him.”

“Yes.”

The answer settled between them. He wished she would tell him he was wrong, so he might explain further. Instead she nodded.

“When did they speak of making you king?”

“Before the city fell.”

“While he was alive?”

“Yes. Rama promised that I would govern Lanka if he prevailed. I accepted that promise. The coronation came afterwards.”

She looked at the open window. Beyond the courtyard a worker was calling for a second ladder. Somewhere someone laughed at the suggestion that one ladder should be made to serve three roofs.

“Did he know?” she asked.

“Your uncle knew I had left him. I cannot say what he knew about every discussion in Rama's camp.”

She folded her hands once more.

“Did you know Uncle saved my life?”

“I knew he ordered the children evacuated. I did not know you had reached the boats.”

“Mother put me aboard after the lists were closed. She said she would find the boys. I saw Uncle on the cliff before our boat turned. I waved to him.”

Vibhishana's fingers closed around the edge of the table.

“He stayed,” she said. “I know he did terrible things. I know what happened to Sita. But he stayed to give us time.”

“Yes,” her father said. “He did.”

She looked at him for a long while. The grey in his hair was no longer strange to her. The scar above his eye was exactly where it had always been. She knew the shape of his hands and how he bent his head to hear someone who was speaking quietly.

“I thought you'd find me,” she said. “For a long time.”

“I searched for you.”

“I know. I've seen the reports. I don't think you chose to leave me there.”

He breathed out. Some tension that had accompanied him since the quay seemed to loosen.

She smiled at him, the open, gentle smile that made it possible for a moment to imagine she was about to give him something beautiful.

“But I'm glad I found you.”

He reached across the table. “My darling girl—”

“Because I needed to hear you tell me. Not a merchant, not soldiers, not someone guessing what happened. You.”

He withdrew his hand slowly.

“I believed,” he said, “that you were coming to see who your father had become. I hoped you would see what we have repaired.”

“I have seen it. The clean wells. The schools. Your captains. Taranisena growing stronger. You have done good work.”

“Then perhaps—”

“I don't want to live here, Father.”

He stared at her.

For several heartbeats he seemed not to understand which part of the sentence had been addressed to him. Then he glanced at the door, expecting a servant or an official to have entered with a confusion that could be corrected.

“This is your home.”

“It was.”

“You've been back barely six weeks. You're still grieving. Of course a place feels strange after everything you've endured. Give it time.”

“I have given it time.”

“Six weeks is not time. Not after fourteen months.”

She did not argue about the number. She had spent those fourteen months building a home elsewhere. He spoke of them as time stolen from her life.

“There are things you could do here,” he said, leaning towards her. “You've learned practical work. Good. There are workshops and schools that need people who understand such work. I could put you in charge of part of the coastal relief programme. Not as a figurehead. You could oversee the repair stores or the children's homes. You would be very good at it.”

“Some people would do the work and I would be praised for being your daughter.”

“Not if I instructed them otherwise.”

She smiled sadly. “That is rather the difficulty.”

He began again, quicker now. Her position gave her opportunities few women possessed. She could influence trade and schools. She could preserve the old music if that mattered to her. In a few years, if she wished, a good marriage could be arranged with a family that respected her independence. There would be no pressure, he said, and then heard how much he had begun to sound as though he was trying to arrange her future before she could answer.

“Father, I don't want a marriage arranged to solve this conversation.”

“I'm trying to show you there is a life here.”

“I know there is. You have made it. It's yours.”

He stood abruptly. The chair scraped the stone.

“Mine? Do you imagine I have spent fourteen months making a kingdom for myself? Do you imagine this crown has given me pleasure? I buried your mother. I buried Arun. I carried your brother home when he was afraid to be touched. I sat with reports that told me you were dead. Now you are alive, and you sit in front of me explaining that you cannot bear to share a roof with me.”

Analā did not move.

“Do you know what you are asking of me?” he demanded.

“I know it hurts you.”

“Hurts me?”

His voice rose. A clerk outside the room stopped walking. Vibhishana heard the silence through the door and lowered his voice, furious that he must still think of the dignity of a king at such a moment.

“I gave you everything I knew how to give,” he said. “I love you. I never stopped. What sort of daughter comes home to a father who has suffered as I have and tells him that his love is not enough?”

She looked down. The words had hurt, but she did not withdraw from them.

“The sort who loves him too,” she said.

The answer seemed to make him angrier. He turned away, then returned, searching for something that would make her defend herself. He told her she was making the wrong judgement about Rama's men, about the war, about the crown. He insisted that she had seen the ugliness of a few officers and mistaken it for the nature of a kingdom. He reminded her that Ravana had made choices that brought death upon them all. His brother had not been perfect; why must Vibhishana alone be required to be?

“I don't require you to be perfect,” Analā said.

“Then forgive me.”

“I'm not punishing you.”

“What else can you call leaving?”

“A choice.”

He looked at her as if the simplicity of the word were intolerable.

After a while the anger went out of him. He leaned against the table, looking suddenly older than he had on the quay. She had seen his helpless joy that day. This helplessness was quieter and seemed to hurt him more.

“Analā,” he said, “what will I do?”

She did not answer. He moved closer.

“Your mother is gone. Your uncle is gone. Arun is gone. Your brother is getting stronger, but some nights I still sit outside his room because I hear him breathing badly and remember the water he hid in. I know I chose this path. I know you believe I chose wrongly. Must I lose you again because I made that choice?”

He covered his face. The sound was not the enormous, unguarded crying of the harbour. It was a smaller, defeated sound, drawn through his teeth. He tried to stop it and failed.

“I thought I could learn how to be a father again,” he whispered. “I've been trying so hard to make you comfortable. Tell me what to do. Tell me one thing that I can do.”

Analā came around the table. She put a hand upon his arm. He caught it and held it against himself, as if this were the answer.

“I haven't forgotten you,” she said. “I won't. You taught me the old songs and how to fold a cloth properly, and how to see whether a story is true by asking who told it first. I know you loved us. I know you still do.”

“Then stay.”

“I can't make myself believe that what you did was right. And I can't live here pretending that I do.”

He clung to her hand a little longer, then let it go. His mind found another arrangement. It had solved impossible problems for a year by discovering arrangements that seemed absurd before they were tried.

“Then go to the island,” he said. “For a little while. I will send a ship. I'll send medicines and whatever Sēnan needs. You can stay with that woman—Mēni, isn't it? And Ilā. You love them. Of course you do. Spend a season there. Two, if you like. Come back when you wish. We needn't decide anything now.”

She looked at him with such tenderness that for a moment he thought he had reached her.

“Don't make it a visit from which I must return,” she said.

“Then we won't call it that. I'll arrange whatever you need.”

“I have what I need.”

“You have a little purse of wages. You can't expect—”

“I can repair nets. I can splice rope. I can mend a small sail. I've been paid for it here and on the island. I've asked the fares of the coastal boats.”

He stared at her. For the first time he understood the coins he had seen her counting, the parcels she carried and the hours she spent in the harbour stores. He had thought she was learning to be useful in his kingdom. She had been learning how to leave it.

“Where will you go?”

“I haven't decided.”

“Not even back to the island?”

“I might. I might go somewhere else. I want to decide when the time comes.”

“You're fifteen.”

“Yes.”

“People will take advantage of you.”

“Some may try.”

“You'll be alone.”

“Sometimes.”

He searched her face for the fear that might make him necessary. She was not promising herself an easy life. That made it harder to argue with her.

“Please,” he said. “Let me do something.”

“You have done things. Some were good. Some I can't accept. I came to understand them from you.”

His shoulders shook again. He sat, rose, sat once more. He spoke of roads that were unsafe, of money she might need, of a father who deserved at least to know whether his daughter was alive. She told him she would try to send word when she could. She did not promise what she could not know.

Then he said something about the scar above his eyebrow.

“I waited for you to ask,” he said. “On the quay. I knew you'd always asked whether it still hurt.”

“I saw it.”

“Why didn't you ask?”

She looked at the small pale line beneath his hair. She could remember standing on a stool to reach it, accusing him of carelessness with doors. Her father's face had always been the safest place in the world to be ridiculous.

“I didn't know if you were still the man I used to ask,” she said.

He put his hand to the scar. She reached up, almost without thinking, and rested two fingers lightly against it. For a moment his eyes closed. She could have been a child again. He could have been the man who pretended to cry out when she touched the healing cut.

He did not pretend this time.

She lowered her hand.

“I am glad I found you,” she said again, so softly he almost didn't hear. “I wanted you to be able to tell me yourself.”

He understood now what the words had meant the first time. They had not promised him that she had come home. They had told him that she had come for the truth.

He wanted to ask how a man could answer honestly and be punished for it. He knew the answer. He had once stood in his brother's room, asked questions he considered necessary and walked away because he could not accept the replies. For a moment the past seemed to turn around him. Then he realised how different the two departures were, and how little that difference could help him now.

Analā stepped back.

“I have to go,” she said.

He shook his head. He was not consenting. He simply had no new words to try. She waited while he searched for one. None came.

At the door she turned.

He looked at her with the terrible concentration of someone trying to make a memory he might have to live on for years. She was taller than the child who had run to meet him before every journey. Her hair was tied back roughly. She carried no jewels. Her face was open and kind, and belonged wholly to her.

He could not remember when he had last felt so proud of her, or when pride had hurt so much.

She raised her hand.

He raised his.

Then she left the room.

Vibhishana stood in the silence that followed. The sunlight remained where it had been. The irrigation report still waited for his seal. Outside, somebody was asking which gate would be opened for a delivery of grain.

He went towards the door as if he might catch up with her. Instead he called for the chamberlain.

“Find her,” he said when the man arrived.

The chamberlain bowed, waiting for the rest of the order.

“Find out which road she's taking.”

“Shall I send men to bring her back, Majesty?”

Vibhishana opened his mouth. He thought of Analā's quiet face, of the things she had asked, and of his own brother's voice in the room he had left long ago. He could order men to close a road. He could order ships to remain in harbour. He could make his daughter safe by taking away every freedom she had spent a year learning to use.

“I don't know,” he said.

The chamberlain waited. It was the first time he could remember the king offering him an answer he could not turn into an instruction.

Outside, somewhere beyond the palace walls, a drum began to beat.

***

Analā crossed the eastern market on foot, carrying her own bundle.

The late sun had reached the upper walls. A woman selling flowers was putting away the last of her garlands. Two workers laughed over a cart wheel that had come free at the wrong moment. The ordinary smells of roasting grain and wet clay travelled between the stalls.

Analā stopped to ask whether a small coastal boat would be leaving with the evening tide. The boatman's apprentice pointed towards the lower quay. She thanked him, tightened the knot on her bundle, and walked on.

Then she heard the drum.

Two low strokes. A pause. A shuddering run of beats that sounded as though someone were teaching a wooden table to laugh.

She knew the rhythm. She knew the little moment of silence the dancers always inserted before they turned their faces fierce. She had heard it while crouched behind a wall with frightened children; she had heard it in this city beside broken fruit, a drainage gutter and a musician holding a wounded instrument.

The square outside the old instrument hall was crowded.

The officer stood at its centre, taller than almost everyone around him. Sweat had darkened his saffron tunic. The vermilion stripe on his forehead had begun to run down one side of his nose. His mouth was crowded with red betel. Two men faced each other in the familiar outstretched pose, arms trembling because they had been made to hold it too long. The officer moved between them, demonstrating the rolling hips, the closed eyes, the sudden sharp glare. Each time he turned towards his companions they laughed a little louder.

One of the men was a lamp-maker carrying a shallow tray of finished clay lamps. A Rakshasa family knot was tied at his wrist. He had been trying to pass through the square. The officer had stopped him to ask whether he was grateful to be allowed to work under Rama's peace.

“I have to deliver these,” the man said.

“Then deliver them with a grateful heart.”

The lamp-maker bowed his head. He wasn't young. His hands were marked with the pale scratches left by dried clay. He had evidently chosen the smallest possible concession, hoping it would be enough.

“Say it,” the officer ordered.

The man spoke Rama's name quietly.

The officer leaned close. “Is that how you thank a prince?”

He seized the edge of the tray and tilted it. Lamps slipped down the wood. The first struck the paving and broke. Then another. The small brittle sounds continued, one after another, while the lamp-maker tried to catch them without letting go of the tray. One of the officer's men laughed so hard he had to stop drumming.

The lamp-maker crouched to pick up the pieces.

“Not yet,” said the officer.

He caught the man by the collar and pulled him upright. A narrow line of red betel juice slid from his lip.

“First you bow properly.”

A woman moved forwards from the crowd, then stepped back when one of the companions turned towards her. The officer seemed not even to see her; her fear was one of the familiar conveniences of his day.

Analā lowered her bundle to the ground.

It was a small bundle. The knot came loose as it touched the paving. A corner of blue cloth fell against the stones. She had been thinking of a harbour fare and a road she had never taken, and of a father sitting in a room she had left. Now she thought only that the lamp-maker was being made to stand above the work he had spent his day making, unable even to rescue what was left of it.

“Let him pick them up,” she said.

The officer turned. He had not noticed her approach.

“What?”

“His lamps,” she said. “Let him pick them up.”

He took a step towards her, amused by what appeared to him an unexpected addition to the entertainment.

“Another one,” he said. “Does this city breed disobedience?”

The drummer made a small flourish. The companions laughed. The sound was practiced enough that they hardly needed to hear the joke.

“He hasn't done anything to you.”

The officer glanced down at the broken lamps, then at the knot of cloth beside her foot. He noticed her height, the plainness of her clothes, her empty hands.

“Perhaps he needs somebody to teach him gratitude,” he said. “Perhaps you do too.”

He shouted the chant into her face. His breath smelled of wine and betel. He waited for her to repeat it. She did not.

The little gap in his performance unsettled him. He was accustomed to silence that yielded. This silence stood in front of him and looked back.

“Say it,” he ordered again.

“Move away from him.”

The officer smiled. He leaned towards the crowd, inviting them to appreciate the insolence. Then he turned back and reached for her face, two fingers extended to tilt her chin upward as he had tilted the fruit seller's wrist weeks before.

Analā moved before his fingers touched her.

Her right arm came up from beside her body. There was no preparation, no warning for the drummers to turn into another beat, no grand sentence to let the officer arrange his response. Her open palm crossed the little space between them and struck his cheek with a flat, hard sound.

His head snapped sideways.

A spray of red betel juice escaped his mouth and struck the pale paving. One sandal slipped. His arm remained raised for an instant, fingers still curved around a face that was no longer there.

The drums stopped.

Not gradually. A drummer had lifted his stick and simply forgotten to bring it down. The other struck the rim once by accident, an absurd little knock that travelled farther through the silence than the chanting had.

The officer was looking at Analā with his mouth slightly open.

She was smaller than him by a full head. She stood where she had stood before. Her hand had returned to her side. Nothing about her face suggested that she was preparing an explanation.

The lamp-maker stared at her. The woman who had stepped back put one hand over her mouth. The crowd looked first at the officer, then at one another, as though checking whether what they had seen was permitted to have happened.

His companions waited for him to laugh, because he always laughed. He tried. His swollen cheek made a lopsided movement and nothing followed it.

Someone behind the potter's stall let out a surprised snort.

Then the potter said, in a voice that carried across the square, “Go on, then. Ask her to say it again.”

A few people laughed. Someone else, too far away to be seen, began to clap. The first clap was isolated, almost apologetic. The next was not. The lamp-maker straightened. The woman beside him stepped forward and knelt to collect the surviving lamps without waiting for permission.

The officer's eyes grew bloodshot. He put a hand on his sword, not drawing it, touching the hilt as he always did when he expected people to become afraid.

No one moved away.

His companions, who had been so certain of the scene while the drums were playing, discovered that there were more people in the square than they had counted. One lowered his sticks. Another took a step back into the crowd and found that nobody made room for him.

“You struck an officer,” the man said at last, his voice thick with betel.

“Yes,” Analā said.

He looked around, searching for a face that would give him back his importance. The lamp-maker was collecting his work. The potter had gone back to the rim of his wheel, though he was plainly still listening. A girl at the fruit stall was smiling in open disbelief. A pair of Lankan watchmen stood at the far arch, watching the officer's hand near the sword. One of them moved nearer. He did not raise a weapon. He simply waited.

The officer had threatened a hundred people in this square and had never needed to finish the threat. His advantage had always been that they finished it for him, inside their own minds.

Now he had no idea what would happen if he tried.

He stepped aside.

Not far. Just enough to leave a path between himself and the lamp-maker. It was the smallest movement he had made that afternoon, and it changed the square more than all the noise before it.

The lamp-maker lifted his tray. The surviving lamps made a faint, uneven rattle. A woman handed him one more from the ground. The potter called out that he had clay for replacements. Somebody laughed again—not at a joke made by the officer, but at the officer himself.

Analā bent and gathered the cloth that had fallen from her bundle. She tied it carefully. Her hand did not shake.

The lamp-maker looked at her, then at the man beside whom nobody now wished to stand.

“Thank you,” he said.

She nodded.

The officer tried once more to summon his companions with a movement of his head. They followed him, not dancing, not chanting, walking away as quickly as men could walk while pretending they had somewhere important to be.

Analā lifted her bundle and continued towards the harbour.

Behind her the drummer lowered his instrument without playing another note.
