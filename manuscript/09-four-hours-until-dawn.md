# Chapter Nine

## Four Hours Until Dawn

The fire began close enough for the king to feel its heat before anyone told him where it was.

A dull orange light appeared beneath the western door. It strengthened slowly, filling the narrow gap between wood and stone. Then came the smell: first heated oil, then resin, then the dry sweetness of palm leaves catching flame.

Shuka turned towards it.

The king did not.

He stood beside the table with one hand resting upon the maps. The other hung near his sword. Shuka saw the fingers tremble once.

The king closed them around the hilt.

The trembling stopped.

Running feet crossed the passage. Someone shouted for water. A bronze vessel struck the floor and rolled in a widening circle until it fell against the wall.

The king asked, “How long remains until dawn?”

There was nothing casual in the question.

He did not sit. He did not look towards the chair that had held him through arguments, confessions and the last remains of food. His face had altered without changing expression. Usually, some private light remained behind the king’s eyes, even when the news was bad. Tonight Shuka could not find it.

Shuka understood that the night had changed.

“I said four hours last night, when I arrived.”

“You did.”

“That was before yesterday’s dawn. Before the bridge, the garden and the negotiations.”

“How long now?”

“I do not know.”

The king looked at him.

Shuka had seen that look turned upon generals, engineers and men who arrived with numbers they had rounded to protect themselves. It did not contain anger. Anger would have been easier.

“You measured the sky,” the king said.

“There is too much smoke.”

“Then measure the lamps. Measure the watches. Measure how long we have been speaking.”

Shuka glanced towards the eastern lattice. Beyond it, the sky remained black. No birds called. No leaf had acquired the grey edge that came before morning.

“Perhaps four hours,” he said. “Perhaps a little less.”

The king released his sword.

“Four hours,” he said. “Two hundred and forty minutes.”

He moved the nearest oil cup to the upper edge of the map.

“We will count from there.”

The lamp between them had burned low enough for the bronze beneath its oil to show through. Its wick leaned towards the rim beneath a small black crown of ash.

The northern wall struck the earth.

The flame flattened. The crown broke away, fell into the oil and floated there without a sound.

The king watched it darken.

“Two hundred and forty minutes,” he repeated.

Another impact crossed the floor. Dust descended from the roof beam and formed a pale line over the map.

Shuka looked towards the orange light beneath the western door.

“What is burning?”

The king opened it.

Smoke moved through the passage in a low sheet. Servants ran towards the western court carrying water jars, wet blankets and baskets of sand. Above them, fire pulsed against the ceiling.

The chamberlain appeared through the smoke.

“The roof above the map store has caught,” he said. “The inner door is holding. The records house is not yet affected.”

“Not yet,” the king repeated.

A young servant arrived with a bucket. He saw the king and stopped so abruptly that water crossed the rim and soaked his feet.

“Do not stop for me,” the king said.

The servant ran on.

The chamberlain said, “At least the enemy will find the western wing properly illuminated.”

The king raised one hand.

That was all.

The chamberlain became silent.

Shuka noticed it. So did the servants nearest them.

During the previous hours, a cook had disputed salt with him while an officer waited to report casualties. The physician had corrected his judgement while stitching his body, as naturally as her mother had once corrected his mother’s. No one in the palace had ever needed to rehearse fear before speaking to him.

Tonight the same freedom remained. Only his attention had changed. Their words reached him, but nothing unnecessary was allowed to remain in the room.

“Seal the passage between the map store and the archive,” he ordered. “Move the water teams away from the decorative halls. If a carving burns, let it burn.”

“The old royal panels are in the western gallery,” the chamberlain said.

“Then future kings may commission less flammable ancestors.”

The chamberlain’s mouth moved almost towards its accustomed response. By then the king was already giving the next instruction.

“Children first from the western rooms. Then the wounded. Then records that teach someone how to keep another person alive. Nothing else is worth a body.”

The chamberlain bowed.

The king stopped him before he turned.

“Mark the time.”

The chamberlain looked towards the water clock beside the council-room door. Its lower bowl had almost filled.

“Two hundred and thirty-seven minutes, Majesty.”

“Send someone every thirty minutes. Whatever I am doing, interrupt me.”

The chamberlain hesitated.

“Even in council?”

“Especially in council.”

“And when there are fewer than thirty?”

“Then every ten.”

The chamberlain bowed again.

This time the king had already turned away.

---

The northern captain arrived with mud across one side of her breastplate and blood drying at her temple.

Behind her came the harbour engineer and a boy from the signal tower. His drumsticks remained tucked into his belt. The physician entered last.

She carried no tray.

Shuka had never seen her without something in her hands.

The king saw it too.

“Who?” he asked.

“Not yet,” the physician answered.

It was the most frightening answer in the room.

The captain placed three wooden markers upon the map. One marked the road the city had broken. The second marked a drainage channel east of it. The third she set beneath the old dyers’ quarter.

“The bear companies began withdrawing,” she reported. “Two reached the shore. The bird standard moved its grain carts towards the boats.”

Hope rose in Shuka before she had finished.

The captain removed it.

“They have turned back.”

“Why?” Shuka asked.

“The northern prince addressed them himself. He said the woman’s release did not end the crime. It proved the island king understood that he had committed one.”

The king’s face remained still.

“He told them they had not crossed the sea merely to recover one person,” the captain continued. “They had crossed to end the power that had allowed a king to take her.”

The physician breathed out through her nose.

“A war that survives its own purpose.”

Her tone invited the king to answer.

He did not.

He looked at the captain. “Did it work?”

“Yes.”

“How completely?”

“Every standard returned.”

“Then do not dismiss it because it is convenient. A convenient argument that moves an army is still an effective argument.”

The captain nodded.

The signal boy drew one drumstick from his belt.

“They changed every command,” he said. “No rhythm is repeated from one unit to another. Officers carry small boards. They show the order before the drums begin.”

“Can you imitate the boards?” the king asked.

“Not from behind the wall.”

“Then remove the wall from the problem.”

The northern captain understood.

“Observers outside?”

“In the abandoned kilns. Give each one a shuttered lamp. One opening for advance. Two for stone teams. Three for retreat. Our drummer answers only after seeing the light.”

The boy frowned.

“What if they use more than three commands?”

The king’s eyes moved to him.

“Then you will count beyond three.”

The boy remained where he was for an instant, as though another sentence normally occupied that space.

The king had already turned to the captain.

“Yes, Majesty.”

The chamberlain began assigning runners.

The northern captain remained beside the map.

“That is not the breach,” she said.

The king looked down.

She touched the marker beneath the dyers’ quarter.

“They found the culvert.”

The room changed around the small piece of wood.

The culvert was too narrow for armoured men. It had been built to carry rainwater and used dye towards the northern channel. Iron bars divided its outer mouth. A child could crawl through when the water was low. A determined man without armour could enter and enlarge the opening from within.

“How?” Shuka asked.

No one answered.

The king moved the marker half a finger’s width.

“The outer mouth is concealed behind the lime bank.”

“It was,” the captain said.

“No scout could have seen it from the road.”

“No scout did.”

The harbour engineer looked at the wall rather than at him.

“Your brother knew.”

The words entered without needing the name.

Vibhishana had played beside the culvert as a child. He had hidden there during summer lessons, emerging with blue feet and the conviction that water absolved truancy. Years later, when the city rebuilt the dyers’ quarter, he had argued that the bars should open inward so trapped workers could escape a flood.

The king had agreed.

“They are cutting the bars at the inner hinge,” the captain said. “They knew which direction they opened.”

The salt road had been knowledge of the land.

This was knowledge of the man.

Shuka said, “Seal it.”

“There are forty-seven civilians in the shelters above it,” the captain replied. “Smoke has closed the western stair. The culvert is their second escape.”

“Move them,” Shuka said.

“We are trying.”

The king asked, “How long?”

“Half an hour if no one panics.”

“Then you require an hour.”

“We may not possess one.”

The captain placed her finger upon the marker.

“We can collapse the culvert now.”

“With forty-seven people above it.”

“We can hold the inner mouth while they move.”

“How many soldiers?”

“Thirty.”

“And when the enemy enters the narrow passage?”

“Oil.”

“No.”

“Majesty—”

“Fire will draw through the shelter vents.”

“Then flood it.”

“And drown the people whose second escape you are flooding.”

The captain struck the map with one finger.

“This is what your brother gave them. Not merely the culvert. He told them which answers you would refuse.”

The king met her eyes.

His hand returned to the sword hilt. Shuka saw the smallest movement beneath the skin of his wrist.

The king’s voice remained level.

“Then let him remain correct.”

“Correctness may cost us the wall.”

“The wall exists to protect those forty-seven people.”

“And the city behind them.”

“Move the civilians. Hold without fire. When the last person clears the blue stair, drop the lime bank into the outer mouth and release the north cistern.”

The captain waited.

The king’s expression changed.

Not much. Only enough.

“Do it.”

The captain bowed immediately.

“Yes, Majesty.”

As she turned, the king added, “If you must choose between the culvert and your soldiers, abandon the culvert.”

“That is not how one holds a city.”

“It is how this city will be held.”

The captain left.

The signal boy followed her without waiting to be dismissed.

For several moments no one spoke.

The king looked at the marker his brother had placed without touching it.

Vibhishana had not merely shown the enemy where the city was weak.

He had shown them where its king would refuse to become cruel.

“Two hundred and twenty-eight minutes,” the chamberlain said from the doorway.

The king moved the oil cup down the map.

The first portion of the night was gone.

---

The harbour engineer placed a torn bronze fitting upon the table.

The king recognised it.

“The western frame.”

“The lower wing,” she said. “The workshop roof was struck. We moved the craft, but the fire is spreading along the cliff passage.”

Shuka looked at the piece of metal.

“What craft?”

The engineer spoke only to the king.

“The coastal wind has turned inland. It will climb the western cliff for perhaps another hour. After that the air becomes uncertain.”

“How much weight?”

“With a new pin? Two adults and ballast. Three if the third is small and no one values the landing.”

“Range?”

“To the southern island with certainty.”

“The evacuation boats?”

“Still inside the reef. They cannot see the channel markers through the smoke.”

The king looked at the map.

“How many children are waiting at the western quay?”

“Three hundred and twelve,” the chamberlain answered. “Another seventy may reach it from the infirmaries.”

The harbour engineer understood his thought.

“The craft cannot carry them.”

“No,” the king said. “It can carry the channel.”

She looked at him.

He pulled the harbour map towards them.

“Put the two sickest children aboard with a pilot. Add the channel chart, signal cloth and two lamps. The pilot crosses above the smoke, lands on the southern island and lights the eastern marker.”

“The boats still have to find the reef opening.”

“The craft circles it first. One lamp opening for left. Two for right. When the first boat clears the reef, it follows the craft’s line towards the island.”

“The frame was not built to circle beneath full load.”

“Then reduce ballast.”

“That makes the landing less stable.”

“Move the rear seat forward.”

“The control bar—”

“Can be shortened.”

The harbour engineer stared at him.

Then she turned the bronze fitting over in her fingers.

“It may work.”

“It will work long enough.”

The physician spoke from beside the door.

“And the king?”

The engineer did not pretend to misunderstand.

“The craft was kept for continuity.”

“Do not hide him inside an abstract noun,” the physician said.

The engineer faced her.

“If he dies at the wall, who organises what remains?”

The physician’s answer was quiet.

“If he leaves while the city burns, what remains of the king?”

Both women looked towards him.

He picked up the torn fitting.

“Show me the frame.”

---

They moved through palace passages that had forgotten their intended uses.

The audience hall contained bandages drying upon ropes between carved pillars. The treasury corridor had become a grain queue. Two old men slept beneath a mural of a royal hunt, their heads resting upon sacks intended for the elephant stables.

At the western stair, a servant scrubbed blood from the third step.

She moved aside when the king approached.

“Leave it,” he said.

“It will set.”

He looked at the stain, then towards the orange light beyond the passage.

“Use cold water. Quickly.”

The servant nodded.

The chamberlain glanced at him. The king had remembered his instruction from another night, or perhaps from another life when there had been time to discuss cleaning.

By then the king was already climbing the next stair.

The western workshop lay beneath the cliff road, where sea wind entered through tall shutters. Tonight the shutters had been sealed with damp cloth. Lamps stood inside bronze boxes pierced by narrow slots. Their light fell in straight lines over tools, treated silk and the pale ribs of the flying frame.

At first Shuka saw a great flower lying upon its side.

Two curved wings opened from a central body. Silk, sealed with oil and tree resin, stretched between narrow lengths of seasoned wood. Adjustable slats ran along the trailing edges. Braided cords passed through bronze rings towards two hand levers and a bar shaped for the pilot’s feet.

Nothing about it floated.

Nothing about it looked divine.

It was more astonishing for both reasons.

Shuka stepped around it.

“This flies?”

One of the younger engineers answered before considering the king’s mood.

“Not while lying on the floor.”

The answer usually belonged easily in that workshop.

The king raised his palm before anyone could add to it.

“Test the left control,” he said.

The engineer moved immediately.

The king crouched beside the damaged lower frame. The physician reached towards him.

He stopped her with the same raised hand.

“Not now.”

“You have opened two stitches.”

“I know.”

“You cannot command blood by refusing to discuss it.”

He looked at her.

The physician had spoken to him beside wounds, meals and military reports with the same directness her mother had once used in the women’s chambers. Nothing in the king’s face asked her to surrender that right.

It asked only that she understand what now stood ahead of his pain.

She stepped back.

The king moved the control lever. One portion of the wing tilted. The other answered late.

“The cord is stretching.”

“Humidity,” the harbour engineer said.

“No. The third guide is cutting it.”

The younger engineer held a lamp beneath the frame. Fine fibres had collected upon one bronze ring.

The harbour engineer swore.

No one commented upon the suitability of the language.

“Replace it,” the king ordered.

Three engineers moved together.

Shuka watched the king’s hands. They knew where the silk would tear first if the frame twisted. They knew which pin carried the greatest load and which rope could be shortened without making the entire wing rigid. He shifted a ballast tray by two finger-widths and asked for the repaired section’s weight before anyone offered it.

The harbour engineer needed a small weight for the balance. The king took Kumbhakarna’s clasp from inside his robe and placed it in her palm.

“You built this,” Shuka said.

“Many people built it.”

“You understand all of it.”

“No one understands all of a machine that has not yet finished failing.”

The harbour engineer held up the replacement ring.

“His half-brother kept the first frame as a ceremonial wonder. It could descend from a hill provided the hill was high and the audience forgiving.”

The harbour engineer paused, expecting him to defend the insult with one of his own.

“How many completed trials?” the king asked.

“Thirty-one.”

“How many pilots capable of landing on the southern island?”

“Three. One is injured. One is already at the northern wall.”

“The third?”

A slim woman near the rear bench lifted her hand.

The king studied her.

“Children?”

“Two.”

“Where?”

“Waiting at the western quay.”

“Then you understand what you are carrying.”

The pilot swallowed.

“Yes, Majesty.”

The king turned to the engineers.

“Shorten the control bar. Move the rear seat forward. Attach two shuttered lamps beneath the left wing. Bind the channel chart to the pilot’s arm in case the frame breaks.”

The harbour engineer said, “If it breaks, the chart will not be her first concern.”

“It will be the concern of every boat behind her.”

He looked towards the damp cloth over the shutters. Firelight moved across it in red bands.

“How many boats?”

“Eight ready. Five more can be loaded.”

“Capacity?”

“Between twenty and thirty children each, depending upon water and medicines.”

“Load all thirteen. No household chests. No ceremonial objects. One physician’s assistant in every second boat. Tie the boats in pairs until they clear the reef.”

The chamberlain began writing.

The king continued.

“The craft launches first. It marks the channel from above. The first boat follows. Once the southern island lights its eastern tower, the remaining boats cross in sequence.”

The harbour engineer looked at the frame.

“If the craft lands badly, the pilot may not light anything.”

“Then the first boat carries a second signal team.”

“And if the first boat strikes the reef?”

“The second cuts the rope and continues.”

The engineer opened her mouth.

The king looked at her.

“Tell me the risk. Do not tell me to wait for a safer night.”

She closed her mouth.

“The wind may hold for forty minutes.”

“Then we use thirty.”

A messenger arrived at the workshop entrance.

“Two hundred and four minutes, Majesty.”

The number moved through the room.

The king pointed at the frame.

“Launch in twenty.”

The workshop erupted into motion.

The king was already moving towards the archive.

---

The records house caught fire twelve minutes later.

The first messenger called it the eastern library. The second called it the physicians’ archive. By the time the king entered the courtyard, three roofs were burning and it no longer mattered which name had been correct.

Flame travelled beneath the tiles where dry palm leaves had been stored above clay tablets. Scribes formed a line between the doors and the rain cistern. They passed baskets out and water in.

Neither movement was fast enough.

The night smelled of wet ash, hot clay and burning binding oil.

A woman emerged with tablets held against her chest. Heat had reddened both forearms. She set them down and turned towards the building again.

The king caught her shoulder.

“What remains?”

“The western shelves.”

“Contents?”

“Court judgements, sky calculations, surgical notes, southern crop records, the royal lines—”

“Which surgical notes?” the physician demanded.

“The red fever and the children’s lung sickness.”

The physician turned towards the door.

The king blocked her.

“No.”

“Those records took thirty years.”

“And will take one life to retrieve badly.”

“There is an unburned passage,” the archivist said. “We can reach one shelf.”

The king looked at the central beam. Resin boiled along its underside.

“Medicine first. Water and seed records next.”

“The royal genealogies?” the archivist asked.

“Leave them.”

“The flight calculations?” asked the harbour engineer.

The king looked towards the workshop where the craft waited.

“Leave them.”

She stared at him.

“We will not rebuild the frame without those tables.”

“The children in the boats will not reach an island worth inhabiting without medicine or water.”

The choice hurt him.

Shuka saw it. The king’s right hand opened and closed beside his robe as if attempting to release something it had spent years holding.

“Medicine,” he repeated.

The archivist sent four people through the side passage.

They returned with two chests wrapped in wool. The physician opened the first. Inside were diagrams of lungs, dosage tables and the record of a child whose fever had fallen after seven days. No one knew her name. Someone had carefully recorded the weight of her urine.

The roof groaned.

The second team emerged with water tables and maps of underground springs.

“There is time for one more,” the harbour engineer said.

The king listened to the beam.

“No.”

“We can recover the balancing tables.”

“No.”

“I know the shelves.”

“No one enters.”

The central roof fell.

Flame struck through the doorway. A wave of heat drove everyone backwards. Loose hair lifted. Tears came from eyes that had not intended to cry.

Inside, the city’s memory became light.

Painted diagrams curled: lenses ground to carry sunlight into windowless rooms; counterweighted platforms used to lift quarry stone; speaking tubes fitted through palace walls; valves that opened when cisterns reached a measured height; powders arranged according to the colours in which they burned; mirrors by which towers sent messages across water faster than a horse could ride.

One shelf contained failures.

Wings that had folded. Medicines that had poisoned. Bridges that had sung before collapsing. The names of people whose mistakes had purchased knowledge for strangers.

The fire distinguished neither achievement nor warning.

The king stepped towards the doorway.

The physician caught his arm.

For one moment Shuka thought he would pull free.

Instead, the king stopped.

His face had lost its control. Fire moved inside his eyes. His jaw tightened until the muscles stood beneath the skin.

“This was four hundred years,” he said.

No one answered.

“Four hundred years.”

His voice was not loud.

The people nearest him heard it and moved faster.

A young engineer stood beside the rescued chests. Soot made two clean paths beneath her eyes.

“They will say the craft flew because a god allowed it.”

The king looked into the building.

“They may.”

“They will forget the measurements.”

“Then measure again.”

“With what? The tables are burning.”

The king turned towards her.

“Begin with what failed.”

“That is burning too.”

He had no answer that could survive the fire.

The wall behind the western shelves collapsed.

The king stepped back at last.

Not because the fire had defeated his desire to enter, but because others were watching to discover whether their lives mattered more to him than the things he had made.

He raised his voice.

“Move the medicine to the boats. Water records with the first signal team. Seeds divided across three vessels. No single boat carries everything.”

The archivist looked at him.

“And the rest?”

The king watched the archive burn.

“We remember what we can.”

A messenger reached the courtyard, bent over and struggled for breath.

“One hundred and eighty-seven minutes.”

The king moved immediately.

“Then we have spent fifty-three.”

The words were for himself.

Shuka heard them.

---

The flying frame launched above the western cliff.

It ran along its wooden rail with the pilot strapped beneath the wings and two fevered children secured behind her. The younger child made no sound. The older one held the channel map against his chest until an engineer took it from him and tied it properly to the pilot’s arm.

The frame reached the end of the rail.

For one terrible instant, it simply fell.

The harbour engineer whispered the angle.

The cliff wind found the curved silk.

The wings flexed, caught and rose through smoke lit from below by the archive fire. Two lamps beneath the left wing opened and closed.

One.

One.

Two.

The first evacuation boat answered from the reef.

The frame banked south.

It crossed the moon like a black flower upon silver light.

Shuka forgot to breathe.

The king did not watch for long.

“First boat,” he ordered.

The ropes were released.

Thirty children moved into the dark water.

The next boat followed. Then the next.

The craft circled above the reef and marked the opening through the smoke. Each time its lamps flashed, boatmen corrected their oars. Behind them, the archive continued burning.

The king stood upon the cliff platform while the first six boats crossed.

The harbour engineer watched the wings.

“The left control is late.”

“It will hold,” the king said.

“You cannot know that.”

“No.”

The answer frightened Shuka more than certainty would have.

The seventh boat carried children from the workers’ quarter. The eighth carried the injured who could sit upright. The ninth contained infants laid in padded grain baskets. The physician moved between vessels checking cloth tags tied around wrists.

The king looked at the dark water below.

“How many?”

“Two hundred and eleven aboard the boats,” the chamberlain said. “Another ninety-six waiting.”

“Send the remaining boats.”

“The tide is turning.”

“So is the wind.”

“The last vessels may have to return along the coast.”

“They only have to leave the burning quarter now. Route them to the southern caves if the crossing closes.”

The chamberlain nodded.

A signal appeared across the water.

Three bright flashes from the southern island.

The flying frame had landed.

The eastern tower lit a moment later.

A sound passed through the people on the cliff—not a cheer, not yet, but the release of breath from hundreds of bodies that had expected the sky to fail.

The king closed his eyes.

Only briefly.

When he opened them, the trembling had returned to his hand.

He pressed the palm against the stone wall until it steadied.

Shuka stood close enough to see.

The king knew he had seen.

“Fear is information,” he said.

Shuka did not pretend the sentence was instruction rather than confession.

“What is it telling you?”

“That I want to live.”

The king looked at the boats.

“And that wanting is about to become expensive.”

---

The shrine lay beneath the oldest portion of the palace.

It was smaller than Shuka expected.

There were no golden doors. No jewelled image waited beneath a canopy. Water darkened the threshold. A black river stone stood upon a low base, smooth from generations of hands and the slow passage of poured water.

Beside it lay three wilting white flowers, a copper bowl and a line of ash.

An old veena rested against the wall.

Its gourd had been repaired with lacquer. One tuning peg did not match the others. The bridge carried a pale groove where a string had pressed for years.

The king removed his sandals.

Shuka remained at the entrance.

“Come in,” the king said.

“I thought kings prayed alone.”

On another night, Shuka’s remark might have opened a smaller conversation.

The king stepped across the threshold.

“I have very little time in which to explain what prayer is not.”

Shuka entered.

Beneath the stone, the sounds of war changed. Impacts arrived as pressure rather than noise. The enemy drums became a pulse beneath the feet. Water fell somewhere behind the shrine, one measured drop at a time.

The king poured water over the black stone.

He did not ask for victory.

He did not ask for his sister, his brothers, his city or himself to be saved.

He wiped the stone with his bare hand and renewed the line of ash.

Shuka watched him.

“You are not asking for anything.”

“I am asking to see clearly.”

“Is that enough?”

“No.”

The king sat before the veena but did not play.

Shuka looked at the stone.

“Your physician heard men say the northern prince is Vishnu. They say he carries a halo. His arrows find openings no one else can see. He crossed the sea. His commander flies.”

“A parasol becomes a halo when the sun stands behind it,” the king said. “Arrows find openings because craftsmen balance them and archers practise. His commander crossed water by boat and cliff. His road required rope, stone, trees and men who died testing it.”

“Then you do not believe he is a god.”

“I believe calling him one prevents us from understanding him.”

“Perhaps understanding is no longer enough.”

“It is the only thing that has ever been enough.”

Shuka looked towards the old veena.

“And your machine?”

“Silk, timber, air and thirty-one failures.”

“They will call it magic.”

“Then the engineers will be killed twice. Once by the war. Once by the story.”

The king drew ash across his forehead.

Shuka asked, “Will Shiva save you?”

The king’s hand stopped.

There was no smile.

“From death?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

The answer arrived without ceremony.

“Then why worship him?”

The king looked at the black stone.

“Because Shiva is not a guard appointed to preserve the shapes I prefer.”

Outside, something heavy struck the northern wall.

Dust fell from the shrine ceiling.

The king continued.

“He is not the promise that my body will remain, that my city will keep its name or that my children will inherit what I built. Forms arise. Forms break. A forest becomes a city. A city becomes ash. Ash enters soil. The same matter takes another name.”

“That sounds like resignation.”

“No.”

The king’s voice sharpened.

“Do not confuse acceptance of death with consent to defeat.”

Shuka became still.

“To worship destruction is not to assist it. To know that a form can end is to understand what must be done while it remains.”

He pressed his ash-covered hand against his chest.

“Shiva is not elsewhere arranging my rescue. He is in the part of me that sees the end clearly and acts without demanding exemption.”

Shuka looked at the king’s hand.

It trembled against his breastbone.

“You are afraid.”

“Yes.”

The admission filled the shrine.

“Of dying?”

“Yes.”

“Of losing?”

“More.”

“Of being remembered as the cloth painted you?”

“Yes.”

The king lowered his hand.

“I am afraid that fear will make cruelty appear practical. I am afraid I will burn the culvert because forty-seven lives seem small beside a city. I am afraid I will place myself in the flying frame because a king can always invent a future that requires his survival. I am afraid I will call my desire duty.”

He looked directly at Shuka.

“That is why I came here.”

“What did you ask Shiva?”

“That when fear offers me another man’s face, I recognise it before I wear it.”

The enemy drums accelerated.

Shuka listened to them.

“You still believe you can win?”

The king stood.

“I have two hundred and forty minutes in which to save a civilisation.”

“Fewer now.”

The king looked towards the shrine entrance.

“Then you understand why I am standing.”

A messenger waited at the top of the stairs.

“One hundred and forty-six minutes, Majesty.”

The king put on his sandals.

Ninety-four minutes were gone.

---

The dyers’ culvert opened before they reached the council room.

The northern captain met them upon the shrine stair. Blood ran from her hairline into one eyebrow. She carried a boy of perhaps six years beneath one arm and her sword in the other.

“Forty-five are clear,” she said.

The king looked at the child.

“Forty-six.”

“His mother is still below.”

The boy held a wooden horse so tightly that one wheel had marked his palm.

The captain placed him in the chamberlain’s arms.

“The enemy entered before we could drop the bank. We hold the blue stair. Ten minutes, perhaps less.”

“The mother?”

“Two assistants are searching.”

The physician turned.

The king caught her sleeve.

“My people are there,” she said.

“Which is why you remain where they can bring patients.”

“I can search faster than—”

“No.”

The physician stared at him.

In earlier hours she might have continued until his answer contained a reason she could accept.

She saw that the reason was already moving towards the gate.

The king released her sleeve.

The captain leaned against the wall for half a breath.

“Enemy teams have reached the old records lane. If they take the blue stair, they enter behind the northern gate.”

“How many reserves?”

“Eighty-two fit to stand. Perhaps twice that many who will stand whether fit or not.”

“The western guard?”

“Twenty-seven. The rest are moving the final children.”

“Call them once the boats clear.”

“If we strip the west, the cliff road opens.”

“The evacuation will be complete.”

“And after?”

“After, the cliff is stone.”

The captain studied him.

“We need you in the inner command room. From there you can see every signal.”

“The blue stair cannot see me.”

“That is why I am asking.”

“No.”

The single word ended the discussion.

The captain bowed.

The king looked at the boy in the chamberlain’s arms.

“Find his mother.”

“We are trying.”

“Find her.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

Authority was not noise.

It was the point after which argument became movement.

---

They returned to the room where the night had begun.

The lamp still lived.

Its wick had shortened. A trembling blue edge surrounded the flame. The fallen piece of ash floated in the oil beside it.

The map now held four oil cups.

The king moved the fourth to the centre.

“One hundred and twenty minutes,” he said.

The chamberlain checked the water clock.

“One hundred and nineteen.”

“Then do not give me the extra one.”

The armour chest stood open.

Inside lay a breastplate repaired across three different periods. One shoulder had been replaced after a northern campaign. The inner leather was newer. The dark metal reflected the lamp without becoming bright.

The physician cut away the king’s bloodied bandage.

Two stitches had opened.

“You cannot wear armour over this.”

“I will.”

“I can bind it tightly enough to slow the bleeding.”

“Do it.”

“It will hurt.”

“I know.”

“That is a surprising development,” the physician said.

The king looked at her—not angrily, but from a distance already occupied by the gate.

She tightened the cloth without waiting for an answer.

His breath stopped.

Shuka held one end.

“Tighter,” the physician ordered.

They pulled again.

The king gripped the table. The muscles in his forearm hardened. Sweat appeared beside his temple, but he made no sound.

The chamberlain lifted the breastplate over him.

The side strap would not meet.

“It is too short,” Shuka said.

The physician fastened an extension buckle. Her fingers paused upon it, perhaps remembering what she would normally have said.

“How long will it hold?” the king asked.

“Longer than you should require it.”

“That is not a measurement.”

“It is the only one your body has given me.”

The chamberlain attached a plain field officer’s shoulder covering and removed the gold border.

The king noticed.

“Why remove it?”

“Archers aim at gold.”

“Good.”

The harbour engineer entered carrying Kumbhakarna’s clasp.

“The frame balanced without it,” she said.

The king took the black bronze from her palm.

“Did it land?”

“Badly.”

“The children?”

“Alive. The pilot broke one arm.”

“The boats?”

“Ten reached the island. Three entered the southern caves. Three hundred and nine children, including the two in the craft, are clear of the western quarter. The final vessel carries the fever chests.”

The king closed his hand around the clasp.

For the first time that night, relief changed his face.

It did not become a smile.

“Three hundred and nine,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Record every name.”

“We do not have every name.”

“Then record every description. No child becomes a number merely because the city is busy dying.”

The chamberlain added the order to his tablet.

The engineer looked at the king’s armour.

“You could still take a boat along the cliff.”

“No.”

“The southern island will answer to you.”

“No.”

“You can command from there.”

The king fastened his brother’s clasp at his throat.

“Three hundred and nine children are safe because adults held this city long enough for them to leave. I will not use their route to become the last evacuee.”

“This is pride.”

“Yes.”

The engineer faltered.

The king continued.

“It is also command. Both can occupy the same body. Tonight I have no time to pretend otherwise.”

A new impact shook the room.

The northern wall answered with the sound of stone falling inside the palace quarter.

“Eighty-nine minutes,” called the messenger from the passage.

The king lifted his sword belt.

“Then we finish the things that require words.”

---

The painted cloth had survived the fire.

Someone had hung it upon the damaged council wall. The white-haloed northern prince stood above animal standards. Beneath him, the island king possessed ten heads. Kumbhakarna lay beside mountains of food. Their sister reached towards the prince with tusks, green skin and a swollen belly.

Shuka stood before it.

“If we are the better kingdom,” he asked, “why are they winning?”

The king did not answer immediately.

Outside, the enemy ram struck the gate.

The sound entered the room through the floor.

“Because war is not a court appointed to identify goodness,” the king said.

“Then what is it?”

“A method by which one side acquires enough silence to describe the other.”

Shuka looked at the ten heads.

“And if they win?”

The king’s jaw tightened.

He did not speak like a visionary observing a future already settled. He spoke like a commander naming the consequence he intended to prevent.

“If they win, their strengths become virtues. Ours become tricks. Their dead become sacrifices. Ours become evidence that justice was done.”

“The woman knows what happened here.”

“She knows what was done to her. She is not responsible for preserving the reputation of the man who did it.”

“Your sister knows.”

“If she lives.”

“The captain, the physician, the engineers—”

“Witnesses die. Records burn. Songs travel.”

The king pulled the cloth from the wall.

His movement tore one damaged corner.

“This is already travelling.”

He held the ten-headed figure towards Shuka.

“A builder with ten disciplines becomes a creature with ten skulls. A man who writes music and orders executions, builds drains and steals a woman, feeds a city and sends his brother to die—such a man requires attention.”

He struck the painted monster with the back of his hand.

“This requires only an arrow.”

The blow startled Shuka.

The king looked at Kumbhakarna.

“My brother’s exhaustion will become laziness. The food he carried for his soldiers will become appetite. They will make him enormous because then his courage requires no choice.”

His hand moved to the painted woman.

“They will give my sister teeth. They will make her belly large enough for children to laugh at. No one will ask what sort of righteous men answer attraction by cutting a woman’s face.”

“And the northern prince?”

The king looked at the halo.

“His doubt will become patience. His ambition will become duty. His refusal of peace will become the necessary completion of justice.”

His fingers tightened around the cloth.

“No song will preserve the workers beneath his road or the soldiers who begged him to take the woman and go home.”

“You speak as though this has happened.”

The king turned upon Shuka.

“It has not.”

The force in his voice filled the room.

“Do you understand me? It has not happened.”

Shuka said nothing.

The king stepped closer.

“The archive burns. The gate is breaking. My brother has shown them the ways beneath our walls. None of that is the same as defeat.”

He pointed towards the passage.

“There are still people carrying water. There are still soldiers at the blue stair. There are children crossing the sea with our medicine. There are still hands that obey when I give an order.”

The northern ram struck again.

Dust fell between them.

“I am telling you what defeat will cost,” the king said, “because you must understand what we are preventing.”

Shuka looked at the painted prince.

“And if we fail?”

The king’s anger receded.

When he answered, his voice was lower.

“They will give children his name. They will speak it over cradles as a wish for goodness.”

He looked at the ten-headed figure.

“My name will become a warning. A mother may admire my music. A father may drink from my reservoirs. Neither will place my name upon a child without preparing him to defend it.”

“You cannot know that.”

“I know how names behave after victory.”

The king folded the cloth once.

“They will build me tall enough to be seen across a field. Ten painted heads. Straw inside. Every year, a man dressed as righteousness will release a burning arrow. Families will bring children to watch me catch fire.”

“Every year?”

“Hatred requires rehearsal once the hated man is no longer present.”

The words did not carry resignation.

They carried fury disciplined into prediction.

“And the prince?” Shuka asked.

“His birthplace will become more valuable than anything he said there. Generations will argue over the precise ground. They will build, break and rebuild upon it. Some will kill to prove that the earth belongs to peace.”

“How can you know?”

“Because men who cannot possess a god will possess the place where they say he stood.”

Outside, enemy soldiers began chanting.

The words remained too distant to distinguish.

The name inside them was not.

The northern prince’s name crossed the wall before his army did.

The king listened.

“That,” he said, “is how a man becomes an answer.”

The ram struck again.

The king looked at the water clock.

“Seventy-three minutes.”

Shuka held the folded cloth.

“Then tell me what to save.”

The king opened the old veena case and removed a small metal plate. Fine lines had been scratched across it to mark the positions at which a string produced its cleanest notes.

He placed it in Shuka’s hand.

“Not my innocence.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Shuka thought of the open garden gate. He thought of the woman taken from a forest, the sister returning without her nose, the peace tablets and the brother sent towards death.

“Yes.”

“The truth is not that I was good.”

“What is it?”

The king looked at him.

“That I was more than the worst thing I did.”

Shuka closed his fingers around the plate.

“What should I say of you?”

“Remember me inaccurately if you must. But do not remember me simply.”

---

The northern captain returned.

Blood had dried along her temple. Five soldiers waited behind her. One wore a kitchen apron beneath borrowed armour. Another held the palace doorkeeper’s spear. The youngest had been the apprentice drummer’s elder sister. Flour still marked the skin beneath her nails.

“The mother?” the king asked.

“Found. All forty-seven are beyond the blue stair.”

Relief entered the room and was immediately forced to stand.

“The culvert?”

“Lost.”

“The gate?”

“Opening.”

“How many minutes?”

The chamberlain checked the bowl.

“Fifty-eight.”

The king lifted his sword.

The captain looked at Shuka.

“You keep behind the line.”

“I am his intelligence officer.”

“Then use your intelligence.”

“Captain—”

The king stopped them.

“Shuka stays behind the line.”

The words hurt more than Shuka expected.

“To do what?”

“Carry what remains.”

“You cannot ask me to sit beside a lamp while you—”

“I am not asking you to sit.”

The king pointed towards the tuning plate.

“Find the woman in the garden when the fighting passes. Find my sister if she lives. Find anyone who remembers my brother as a man. Decide what you can carry without turning it into another weapon.”

“And if you return?”

Shuka waited for the familiar punishment of his pessimism.

The king looked instead towards the northern passage.

“If I return, you may give it back.”

Shuka swallowed.

“What if they sing it wrongly?”

The king stopped at the door.

For one moment, Shuka expected him to provide the name the story had withheld. A signature. Something that would transform the tuning plate from debris into testimony.

The king looked back.

“Then sing longer.”

---

The palace doors opened into night.

Smoke moved through the outer court in low bands. The archive fire painted the underside of the clouds, but the sky above them remained black.

Dawn had not begun.

The king descended the steps.

The city did not cheer.

At first, people merely looked.

A king in armour meant that every reassuring report had ended. Water carriers understood it. Cooks understood it. Wounded soldiers lifting themselves upon their elbows understood it. Mothers guiding children towards the southern tunnels understood before anyone spoke.

The king reached the centre of the court.

An old mason raised his soot-blackened hand.

The king raised his own.

A wounded soldier stood. Another tried and failed, so the woman beside him lifted his spear. The apprentice drummer climbed upon an overturned grain cart and struck the new enemy rhythm once upon the rim.

His sister answered with the butt of her spear.

Others joined.

Not a ceremonial beat.

Not a hymn.

A pulse for frightened feet.

The king walked through the civilisation the coming song would call demonic.

He passed the eastern infirmary, where women read dosage marks by firelight. He passed kitchens where palace vessels boiled bandages and lentils beside one another. Engineers drew the enemy culvert upon a plaster wall because no clean tablets remained. Servants carried children through a corridor once reserved for crowned heads. A woman from the treasury divided the final arrows according to the skill of the hands that would use them.

No one bowed.

There was no room.

Pink petals had blown from the garden into the palace road. Ash darkened their edges. They crushed beneath the king’s sandals and clung to the wet leather.

From behind the open garden gate came the single note of a woman singing to herself.

The king slowed.

Only once.

Then he continued.

At the next turning, the physician caught his arm.

“You are bleeding again.”

He looked down at her hand.

She released him.

“If you fall,” she said, “they will gather around you.”

“Then keep them fighting.”

She pressed folded cloth into the captain’s hand.

“When the first is red, replace it.”

“And when the next is red?” the captain asked.

The physician looked at the king.

“Bring him back before then.”

The king touched two fingers to the shrine ash upon his own forehead, then placed them gently above hers.

“Keep the city alive.”

The physician’s mouth moved towards a familiar answer.

The king raised his palm.

Not tonight.

She nodded.

“Yes, Majesty.”

He left before farewell could become another form of delay.

At the armoury court, the chamberlain stopped.

“I should come.”

The king looked at the man’s soft shoes, writing belt and ring of household keys.

“No.”

“I can carry a spear.”

“You can carry names. Record the evacuated, the wounded and the dead. If the throne changes hands, make theft difficult.”

The chamberlain bowed.

It was the last bow the king received.

A messenger ran towards them.

“Thirty-one minutes.”

The king continued walking.

---

The northern gate was visible from the final street.

Its left door had split around the central brace. Every enemy strike opened the fracture far enough for firelight to enter, then closed it again. Defenders packed timber behind the right door. Above them, archers fired through smoke without seeing where their arrows landed.

The blue stair had become a narrow battle.

Men entered through the culvert without armour, climbed over their own wounded and tried to seize the mechanism that raised the locking bar. Island soldiers met them two at a time because the stair permitted no more.

Water ran beneath everyone’s feet, carrying blue dye and blood into the street.

The king took command before reaching the gate.

“Six archers to the roofs,” he ordered. “Shorten the timber behind the right door. It must release without trapping our people.”

The captain repeated the commands.

“Move the reserve into the bath lane,” the king continued. “No torches there.”

“They will not see the signal.”

“The drummer will.”

The apprentice raised his remaining stick.

The king pointed towards the fractured door.

“When it opens, give them twenty paces.”

The captain looked at him.

“Twenty?”

“They will crowd behind success. Close the side lane after the first ranks pass. Strike from both ends.”

“And the gate?”

“We stop pretending it is a wall.”

He looked at the frightened defenders.

“We make it a funnel.”

The captain began issuing orders.

Fear changed shape around him.

Soldiers who had stared at the breaking door looked towards the side lane. Men who had expected to die holding timber received positions from which death was no longer the only instruction. Shields moved. Archers changed roofs. The reserve disappeared into darkness.

A young soldier approached with a report, saw the king and forgot the words.

The king gripped his shoulder.

“Breathe.”

The soldier did.

“Now tell me.”

“The locking bar is splitting.”

“How long?”

“Three more strikes. Perhaps four.”

The king released him.

“Then we possess three.”

The boy returned to his place steadier than he had arrived.

Shuka watched.

This was not the absence of fear.

It was fear being given work.

---

The king climbed the inner barricade.

The street narrowed between a grain store and the wall of a public bath. Smoke reduced the rooftop lamps to dull red stains. Lime dust floated in the air. Blue water ran around the king’s sandals, warm from the bodies through which it had passed.

A pink ashoka petal adhered above his left ankle.

It moved each time he breathed.

The enemy ram struck.

The gate did not make one sound.

Outer planks cracked. Iron straps groaned against their bolts. Timber shifted upon stone. Dust fell in a pale sheet from the arch and settled upon the king’s hair and shoulders.

The left door opened the width of a hand.

Through it Shuka saw torchlight, animal standards and hundreds of bodies waiting for one object to fail.

The door closed.

The men around the king flinched after the sound rather than during it, as though their bodies required permission to be afraid.

The king did not flinch.

His fear had gone elsewhere.

It sat beneath his tongue and dried his mouth. It tightened the skin between his shoulders. It entered the wound beneath his armour and beat there with his pulse.

His eyes were red from smoke, exhaustion and the refusal to close. A small vessel had broken across the white of the left one. In the firelight, it appeared that blood had risen inside his gaze but could find no way out.

He drew his sword.

There was nothing divine about the sound.

Metal left leather.

A man adjusted his grip.

His hand slipped.

Only once.

Sweat had collected beneath his fingers. He wiped the palm against his armour and took the hilt again.

The captain saw.

She looked away, granting him the privacy of a weakness upon which they might all depend.

The ram withdrew.

For several seconds there was no impact.

The waiting was worse.

The king heard the enemy prepare the next blow: feet finding mud, officers calling the count, rope tightening as men lifted the weight.

Someone laughed beyond the gate.

It was a young laugh, surprised by something another soldier had said. The sound crossed the timber more easily than an army.

The king wondered whether the young man had a sister.

He wondered whether she had ever returned home bleeding while men she trusted described the blood as a joke.

His own sister came to him—not the green creature painted upon the cloth, but a girl of nine standing upon a sea wall with her hair across her face. She had insisted that the wind struck her first and was therefore entitled to strike it back.

He had laughed.

She had thrown his writing tablet into the water.

Behind her came Vibhishana, small and serious, carrying the wet tablet after climbing where he had been forbidden to go. He held it in both hands as though returning a sacred object.

The king remembered praising the tablet before praising the boy.

There were injuries that waited years to discover their names.

“Twenty minutes,” a messenger from the palace called from behind the barricade.

The king did not turn.

The ram struck.

The door leapt inward.

A support cracked and kicked backwards. Two defenders fell beneath it. The fracture opened wide enough for a spear to enter. Its blade searched blindly, found only smoke and withdrew.

Through the opening, the king saw an eye.

For one instant, the enemy soldier saw him too.

Neither looked like the creature the other had been promised.

The gate closed between them.

Someone beside Shuka whispered a prayer.

Someone else whispered the king’s title.

The king did not look back.

In the glow upon the gate, he saw the flying frame crossing the moon. He saw children carried beyond the smoke with medicines, seed records and the measurements of water hidden beneath the earth.

He saw Kumbhakarna at twelve, asleep beneath the veena table after insisting he had remained awake through the entire composition.

Then he saw his brother older, filling a doorway and asking him to make the decision no king should have been permitted to make.

The clasp at his throat felt heavier than the armour.

He remembered the woman in the garden holding out her hand for the key he could not turn without pulling at his stitches. He remembered her opening the lock herself.

Taking you was wrong.

The sentence returned without its council or witnesses. It remained true. It had begun the road that now ended at this door.

He could tell himself that the northern prince had always wanted the island.

Perhaps that was true.

He could repeat his sister’s blood, the duty of a king, the pride of a husband and the necessity of answering violence. Each explanation contained enough truth to remain useful.

None could lift responsibility from his hand.

The hand still held the sword.

“Fifteen minutes.”

The ram withdrew again.

“Ready,” the northern captain called.

Her voice moved through the defenders. Shields lowered. Archers drew. The reserve waited inside the side lane. The apprentice drummer lifted his unbroken stick.

The king looked at the people beside him.

The kitchen soldier’s apron showed beneath borrowed armour. The young woman with flour beneath her nails held her spear too tightly. An old guard moved his lips through the names of his children. A boy who had lied about his age kept swallowing because terror had filled his throat with water.

These were not the demons who would inhabit the song.

They were tired people standing in a dirty street because everything they loved was behind them.

So was he.

The sound outside began as a murmur.

“One,” an enemy officer called.

Hundreds of feet entered the rhythm.

The king tightened his grip.

“Two.”

The ropes creaked.

The king’s heart struck hard enough for the broken place in his side to answer.

“Three.”

For one shameful, precious instant, he wanted the door never to open.

He wanted another hour beside the lamp. Another disagreement with the physician. Another impossible request from the kitchen. He wanted Kumbhakarna to complain that court portions were designed for decorative men. He wanted to repair the third string of the old veena.

He wanted his sister to enter the palace alive and insult him with enough accuracy to make forgiveness unnecessary.

He wanted to live.

The desire did not diminish him.

It was the full measure of what he was preparing to spend.

“Four.”

The king inhaled until the binding stopped him.

In the shrine, he had asked for no rescue.

He had asked to recognise the other face when fear offered it.

Fear offered it now.

It told him that fire in the culvert would have saved the gate. That killing the trapped workers would have saved the road. That the woman in the garden could still have been useful. That the children could have been left behind so the king might escape through the air.

It offered survival one reasonable cruelty at a time.

The king recognised the face.

He refused to wear it.

“Five.”

The captain looked at him.

He nodded.

The apprentice struck the drum.

The ram hit.

The central brace broke.

For the smallest portion of a second, nothing moved. The two halves remained aligned through habit alone.

Then the upper timber fell, struck stone and split again.

The left door swung inward through dust and torchlight.

The night beyond it was filled with men.

They stood shoulder to shoulder beneath animal standards, faces marked with mud and ash. Some shouted. Some looked terrified. Others had arranged their expressions into the righteousness expected of them.

Behind the first ranks stretched spears, torches, ropes and the dark road upon which the prince had brought them across the sea.

The red standard entered first.

The defenders gave way.

The enemy mistook discipline for collapse and poured into the street.

The king waited.

Ten paces.

The kitchen soldier retreated beside him.

Fifteen.

The young woman’s spear shook against her shield.

Twenty.

The side lane closed.

The king raised his sword.

Firelight caught the shrine ash upon his forehead, the blood inside his eye and his brother’s black clasp at his throat.

Fear remained in every part of him.

It did not leave.

Courage did not replace it.

Courage placed one foot before the fear and gave it somewhere to go.

For the last time, Shuka saw the whole man clearly: musician, engineer, brother, captor, worshipper, liar, protector and king.

Neither the god one army required nor the monster it had already painted.

His face did not show peace.

It showed decision.

The king opened his mouth.

What came from him was not a command.

It was a roar.

It struck the narrow street with such force that the nearest soldiers turned towards him. Men who had served beside him for twenty years had never heard that sound. The captain’s eyes widened. Even the first enemy ranks faltered, not because they understood what he shouted, but because there were no words inside it to understand.

His face had broken open.

Tears streamed from his bloodshot eyes—part smoke, part pain and part everything for which there was no longer time. The veins stood along his throat. His teeth were bare. Anger, fear and grief had entered the same expression until none could be separated from the others.

Later, painters would give him ten heads in order to make him terrible.

None of them would paint this one.

This face was worse than a monster’s because it belonged to a man who knew exactly what could be lost.

He lifted his sword and rushed forward.

The defenders answered—not with his title, nor with a prayer, but with the same raw sound. It travelled backwards through the street, caught among the shields and roofs, and returned larger.

For several heartbeats, the roar belonged to the city.

Then steel met steel. Wood broke. Men struck men. The sound entered the battle and became impossible to distinguish from it.

For several breaths, Shuka followed the king by the effect he had upon other men. A line beginning to bend became straight. A dropped shield rose. The red standard disappeared, returned at an angle and fell.

Defenders upon the roof shouted as the reserve entered from the side lane.

Then smoke moved between them.

Shuka climbed the grain cart.

He saw the captain’s sword above the crowd. He saw the apprentice drummer striking until his final stick broke. He saw Kumbhakarna’s clasp once, black bronze catching the fire.

Then he could not see it.

The crowd surged towards the gate.

It drew back.

Somewhere beyond the smoke, a voice issued an order that hundreds obeyed.

Shuka could not tell whose.

He held the metal tuning plate so tightly that its scratched lines entered his palm.

Behind him, in the room where the conversation had begun, the water clock released its final measured thread.

The exhausted wick bent beneath another crown of ash.

It fell into the oil.

The flame went out.

Two hundred and forty minutes had passed.

The city remained dark.

The king had not returned.

The sun had not risen.

That was the only mercy left to them.
