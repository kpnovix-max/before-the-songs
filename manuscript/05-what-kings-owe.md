# Chapter Five

## What Kings Owe

The order required only the king’s thumb.

The boy whose death it authorised was seventeen years old.

He stood beneath the western window with soot across his face, one sleeve burned away and both hands tied in front of him with bowstring. His left hand would not stop shaking. He kept closing the fingers around the trembling as though disobedience could be concealed inside a fist.

Outside, the northern alarm began.

One strike. A pause. Two strikes. A pause. Then the iron plate was beaten without rhythm by someone who had abandoned the message and chosen fear.

The king remained beside the window where Shuka had helped him stand. Blood had passed through the cloth above his hip. Salt dried in pale lines upon the ceremonial armour. The painted parrot across his chest had lost half its yellow beak at the sluice and now looked more sensible than it had during the coronation for which it had been made.

The messenger who had announced the enemy movement waited near the door. Beside him stood a captain from the northern wall, a grey-haired woman in a night robe and a little girl carrying one red sandal.

No one appeared certain which of them belonged to the matter of the execution.

“Begin again,” the king said.

The northern captain bowed.

“The accused was assigned to the oil cistern above the potters’ quarter. At the second alarm he left his post without permission. The cistern was opened nine minutes late. Fire reached the lower stair. Two watchmen died preventing the flames from reaching the main store.”

“I know what the tablet says.”

The captain looked towards the bound boy.

“Desertion during assault carries death.”

“I also know what the law says. Why did he leave?”

The captain’s mouth tightened.

“His family lived below the cistern.”

The grey-haired woman stepped forward. “Lives.”

The captain glanced at her.

“His family lives below the cistern.”

The little girl held up the red sandal as though it constituted evidence.

The king looked at the boy.

“You left a military post to enter the burning quarter?”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“Why?” the king asked.

The boy looked at the child.

“My sister was alone.”

“She was not alone,” the grey-haired woman said. “I was going back for her.”

“You cannot climb the stairs,” the boy replied.

“I can climb stairs when people stop telling me I cannot,” the old woman said.

“You fell yesterday,” the boy said.

“Yesterday the stairs were not on fire.”

The king closed his eyes for one brief moment.

“Madam, are you his mother?”

“Grandmother.”

“Where is his mother?” the king asked.

“Dead,” the old woman said.

“His father?” the king asked.

“Also inconveniently dead.”

The king opened his eyes.

“War?”

“Fever. War has no exclusive claim upon bad timing.”

The child leaned against the old woman’s leg. The red sandal was too small for her hand and too damaged for her foot. Its leather strap had burned through.

The king turned to the captain.

“Did he return to the cistern?”

“After carrying the girl to the temple court.”

“Was the fire contained?” the king asked.

“Eventually,” the captain said.

“That word is doing a great deal of work.”

“Two men died.”

The boy’s fist closed harder around its own shaking.

“I know,” he said.

The captain faced him. “You knew the law when you left.”

“Yes.”

“And you left,” the captain said.

“Yes,” the boy said.

“Had the lower cistern caught, half the quarter would have burned.”

“Yes.”

“Stop agreeing with me as though that repairs it.”

The boy lowered his head.

The king crossed to the black table. His injured leg dragged on the third step. Shuka moved to help him, but the king reached the chair before assistance could become visible.

On the table lay the execution order, Vibhishana’s map of the salt road and the length of wet blue cord returned from the sluice. The three objects appeared to belong to separate governments.

The old chamberlain entered carrying a dry robe over one arm.

He took in the bound soldier, the family, the waiting captain and the blood collecting beneath the king’s armour.

“I see changing clothes has become a matter of state,” he said.

“Everything becomes a matter of state if ignored for long enough.”

“Your physician is outside.”

“Tell her I am deciding whether to kill someone.”

The chamberlain looked at the boy.

“That will improve her mood.”

“Nothing improves her mood.”

“She says the same of you,” the chamberlain replied.

He placed the robe over the broken breakfast chair and withdrew.

The northern alarm continued. Men ran across the lower court carrying bundles of arrows towards a wall already short of archers. Somewhere close, a mule refused to move. Three soldiers pushed it from behind while a fourth stood in front and explained military necessity. The mule appeared unconvinced.

The king read the order.

The script was careful. The law was cited correctly. The names of the dead watchmen had been entered beneath the charge. A blank circle waited at the bottom for the royal mark.

“If I spare him,” the king said, “what happens at the next fire?”

The captain answered immediately. “Every man with family below the wall decides his own post is optional.”

“And if I kill him?”

The captain hesitated.

“The law remains clear.”

“That was not my question.”

The captain looked towards the little girl.

The king waited.

“A child grows up knowing the city killed her brother for carrying her out of a fire.”

“Yes.”

The old woman placed one hand on the girl’s hair.

“And two other families,” the captain continued, “know their men died because he was not where he had sworn to stand.”

“Yes.”

The king looked at Shuka.

“You wished to learn what a king does with the truth once he has been given all of it.”

Shuka glanced at the salt-road map.

“I did not expect the lesson so quickly.”

“Government rarely permits preparation.”

The king pushed the execution order away.

“Untie him.”

The captain did not move.

“Majesty—”

“Untie him.”

Shuka cut the bowstring with the small knife upon the table. The boy rubbed his wrists but did not look relieved.

“You are not pardoned,” the king told him.

The boy looked up.

“You will report to the temple court. There are children being moved from the northern quarter. You will carry them until no child remains there or your arms fail. Afterward you will present yourself to the families of the two men who died and tell them exactly why you left. You will not tell them the king ordered you to do it. You will not offer them the protection of my decision. If they spit upon you, you will remain. If they strike you, you will remain unless the second strike is likely to kill you. If they ask you to work for them, you will work.”

The boy’s lips parted.

“For how long?”

“Until they decide the debt has changed shape.”

The captain stepped closer to the table.

“The law says death.”

“The law was written to prevent a man saving himself while others burned.”

“It does not say that,” the captain said.

“Then the law has met a life more precise than its language,” the king replied.

“The wall will hear that desertion was forgiven.”

“Then teach the wall the entire sentence.”

The king leaned forward. Pain sharpened his voice.

“Tell them he left his post. Tell them two men died. Tell them a child lived. Tell them he returned. Tell them mercy did not make him innocent and guilt did not make him useless. If they require a simpler story, they may wait for the poets.”

The little girl crossed the space before anyone could stop her. She placed the burned sandal upon the black table.

“He found only one,” she told the king.

The king examined it gravely.

“Then his failures continue to accumulate.”

The child considered this and nodded.

Her brother made a sound dangerously close to a sob.

“Go,” the king said.

The grandmother bowed. The child did not. She took the boy’s newly freed hand and pulled him towards the door. He looked back once at the names of the dead men upon the unsigned order.

Then they were gone.

The captain remained.

“You disagree,” the king said.

“Yes.”

“Good. A captain who agrees with a king before breakfast is either dishonest or underfed.”

“It is nearly noon.”

The king looked towards the paling sky.

“Then this has been an unusually long night.”

A runner arrived from the northern embankment. Mud covered him to the waist. He carried no weapon and one of his ears was bleeding.

“The outer trench is lost,” he said. “Their forward companies are assembling beyond the brickworks. We have begun moving the northern quarter, but the cart road is blocked by the hospital wagons.”

“How long until they reach the inner embankment?”

“Less than an hour.”

“Who commands there?”

The runner’s eyes moved towards the captain.

“No one now.”

The captain went still.

“Commander Vara?” the captain asked.

“Dead,” the runner said.

“His deputy?”

“Missing.”

“How many companies remain?” the king asked.

“Three that can stand. Parts of two others.”

“That is not enough to hold the brickworks,” the king said.

“No,” the runner replied.

“How long must they hold?”

“The quartermaster says two hours to clear the northern road. Three if the hospital carts keep their wheels.”

The king turned towards the window.

Beyond the palace, the northern roofs descended towards the embankment. Smoke moved between them in low grey layers. The brickworks chimney had collapsed during the night. Its upper half lay across the evacuation road, and people climbed over it carrying bedding, grain, water pots and the elderly.

The enemy standards were visible beyond the wall.

Thousands of small figures moved beneath them.

The king’s fingers rested on the unsigned death order.

“There is one commander who can hold it,” the northern captain said.

No one answered.

The captain looked at the old chamberlain, who had returned with the physician and was now pretending not to listen.

“He will not come for me,” she continued. “He will not come for the council. He may come for you.”

The king’s hand remained upon the tablet.

Shuka understood before the name was spoken.

There was another brother.

One who had not crossed.

One whom no one had mentioned while speaking of loyalty because his loyalty had taken the inconvenient form of remaining nearby and refusing to be useful.

“Where is he?” Shuka asked.

The chamberlain answered.

“In the old western barracks.”

“Sleeping?”

The chamberlain’s mouth moved at one corner.

“That is the official account.”

The king lifted his hand from the execution order.

“Bring my walking staff.”

The physician stepped between him and the door.

“Remove the armour.”

“Later.”

“The cut above your hip is deep,” the physician said.

“Then it will remain deep while I walk,” the king replied.

“You may have broken a rib.”

“I have several.”

“Not several to spare.”

The king looked towards the northern smoke.

“Bind the hip. Leave the armour.”

The physician began objecting.

“Quickly,” he said.

She cut away the blood-soaked cloth with more anger than tenderness. The wound had opened along the edge of the breastplate. Shuka looked away too late.

The king noticed.

“You have spent six nights among an army and remain offended by the interior of a man?”

“The interior is generally meant to remain private.”

“A comforting administrative principle,” the king replied.

The physician packed the wound with powdered bark and clean linen. The king’s hands closed around the arms of the chair. He made no sound until she tightened the final knot.

Then he said something about her ancestry which caused the chamberlain to inspect the ceiling.

“Your mother knew mine,” the physician said.

“Then she will understand.”

She fastened the dry robe over the damaged armour because removing it would take too long. The result made the king look as though he had dressed for court during an earthquake.

The chamberlain brought the walking staff.

The king took it and faced Shuka.

“Remain here.”

Shuka stared at him.

“You used that instruction at the sluice.”

“And yet here you are.”

“I am improving,” Shuka said.

“Slowly,” the king replied.

“If this concerns the brother who remained, then it concerns the story you asked me to hear.”

The king’s expression changed slightly.

“Not every room belongs to a witness.”

“No. But you are about to enter one carrying a crown, a military necessity and an old wound. You may later remember only the version that allowed you to leave it.”

The old chamberlain looked at Shuka with sudden respect.

The king did not.

For several breaths he said nothing.

Then he held out the staff.

“If you insist upon becoming wise, make yourself useful and carry this on the stairs.”

***

The old western barracks stood beyond the palace kitchens where the ground fell towards the sea.

It had once housed the royal guard. Newer quarters had been built beside the northern training field, and the old compound became a place for cracked shields, retired officers and furniture awaiting decisions. Its inner courtyard was shaded by a rain tree large enough to have survived three generations of architects.

The king’s brother occupied the commander’s rooms at the far end.

The doors were closed.

Twenty-seven soldiers waited beneath the covered walk. Some wore armour. Others carried cooking vessels, folded blankets and coils of rope. No one stood close to the door.

Shuka counted the soldiers twice.

“Are they guarding him?” he asked.

“They are waiting for him,” said the chamberlain.

“How long?”

“Some of them, three months.”

The king crossed the courtyard slowly. Each step pressed the staff into damp earth. The soldiers bowed. He acknowledged them without stopping.

Beside the door stood six covered food trays.

The largest was wide enough to bathe a child.

Shuka looked at the chamberlain.

“Is that also official?”

“That is lunch.”

“For whom?” Shuka asked.

“The question has caused disagreement in the kitchen.”

From behind the closed doors came a deep voice.

“If you intend to discuss my eating, at least bring the food inside.”

The chamberlain lifted the largest tray with both hands.

The king stopped him.

“Leave it.”

“He will complain.”

“He has been practising,” the king replied.

The king opened the door.

The room beyond was dark and unexpectedly bare.

No royal carpets covered the stone. No weapons hung upon the walls. A sleeping mat lay beneath the western window, rolled and tied. Beside it were four books, a cracked cup and a wooden box containing pieces of a disassembled model bridge. Dust covered the council robe hanging from a peg.

The man seated on the floor was repairing a sandal.

He was very large.

Not large enough to require legend. Large enough to explain why legend had found him convenient.

Even seated, his shoulders rose above the low table. His hair had grown past his neck and had been cut unevenly with a knife. A dark beard concealed most of his face. One knee showed through a tear in his plain cotton garment. His hands, bent around the leather strap, made the sandal appear to belong to a child.

He looked at the king’s bloodied armour.

Then at the walking staff.

Then at Shuka.

“You brought an audience,” he said.

“He refuses to remain where he is placed.”

“Then he will suit the family.”

The brother returned to the sandal.

“You are bleeding on the floor.”

“It was an unattractive floor.”

“I had grown used to it,” his brother replied.

The king closed the door behind them. The chamberlain remained outside with the soldiers and the food.

The brother pulled the strap through the sole and tested it.

“How many came through the eastern channel?”

“Enough.”

“That is the number men use when the number embarrasses them,” his brother said.

“Sixty-two. Perhaps more who did not return before the sluice opened,” the king replied.

“And how did they find the stones?”

The king did not answer.

His brother looked up.

The resemblance between them was not immediate. The larger man’s features were broader, his eyes deeper beneath the brow. But when he waited through another person’s silence, his face became the king’s face without the years of ceremony laid over it.

“Our brother?” he asked.

“Yes.”

The sandal strap broke.

The large man stared at it.

“That seems excessive,” he said.

It was unclear whether he meant the strap or the betrayal.

He placed the sandal beside him.

“Is he alive?”

“Yes.”

“Safe?” his brother asked.

“Safer than the men who followed his map,” the king said.

“Do you intend to kill him?”

“I intend to survive the next hour.”

“You always were ambitious.”

The king lowered himself onto the only stool. It complained beneath neither his weight nor his injury, having evidently spent years preparing for the other brother.

Shuka remained near the door.

The large man looked at him.

“Sit. You make the room appear guilty.”

There was no second stool. Shuka sat upon the floor.

The brother reached for the cracked cup and smelled it.

“Empty,” he observed.

“A condition common to cups.”

“Not in a civilised kingdom.”

He struck the door twice with his palm.

The chamberlain entered carrying the largest tray.

“You see?” the brother said. “Government.”

The tray contained rice, lentils, fried plantain, curd, green mango, flatbread and enough tamarind vegetables for the twenty-seven men waiting outside.

The chamberlain set it down.

“The kitchen requests clarification on whether the remaining trays are also yours.”

“Tell them I have been insulted,” the brother said.

“They assumed so,” the chamberlain replied.

“Bring all of it.”

“Your brother wishes to speak.”

“My brother has wished to speak for three months. Hunger has been patient longer.”

The king picked up a piece of flatbread.

“The curd is thin.”

His brother tasted it.

“Treason.”

“We are finding it everywhere.”

The chamberlain left before either could ask him to smile.

For several minutes the brothers ate.

It was not the meal of a giant. It was the meal of two men who had not shared food in months and did not know how to begin with the reason one of them had come.

The larger brother divided the rice with the serving spoon. He placed the smaller portion before himself and the larger before the king.

The king exchanged the bowls while he was reaching for the mango.

The brother exchanged them back.

Neither commented.

Shuka watched this occur three times.

“You are both aware,” he said, “that there is more rice outside.”

The brothers looked at him.

“He is a spy,” the large man said. “Nothing escapes him.”

The king almost smiled.

The room relaxed around the failure.

Outside, soldiers began distributing the other trays. Bowls knocked against stone. Someone laughed when the chamberlain announced that the curd had betrayed the island.

The brother filled his cracked cup with water.

“They say I eat thirty pots at a meal,” he said to Shuka.

“Who says?”

“The kitchen, whenever requesting funds,” the brother said.

“Do you?”

“Of course. Then I swallow the pots. Washing them is tedious.”

He passed most of the tray through the doorway to the waiting soldiers.

“By nightfall,” he continued, “someone will have counted the vessels outside and sworn they saw me empty each one.”

“People prefer an appetite they can measure,” the king said.

“Particularly when they cannot measure a man.”

The brother wiped his hand and looked at the damaged armour again.

“Why are you here?”

The king set down the flatbread.

“The northern outer trench is lost. The brickworks will fall within the hour. The quarter requires three hours to clear.”

“And you have how many companies?” his brother asked.

“Three. Parts of two others,” the king said.

“Commander?”

“Dead.”

“Deputy?” his brother asked.

“Missing,” the king replied.

The brother drank.

“Then withdraw to the inner embankment.”

“The evacuation road runs behind the brickworks.”

“Use the sea lane,” his brother said.

“The tide is turning. Half the boats are at the eastern sluice,” the king replied.

“Open the cattle road.”

“Blocked by the hospital wagons.”

The brother looked at him.

“You have already solved every answer except the one for which you came.”

The king’s face became still.

“I came to ask you to command the evacuation.”

The brother laughed once.

There was no humour in it.

“No. You came to ask me to hold the brickworks while the evacuation passes behind me.”

“The command includes both.”

“Do not hide death inside administration. It is too large. The papers tear,” his brother said.

Shuka lowered his eyes.

The king looked towards the western window. Sea light moved upon the wall.

“I need two hours.”

“Three.”

“The quartermaster may be wrong,” the king said.

“Quartermasters are always wrong in the direction that preserves their hope.”

The king said nothing.

His brother leaned back against the wall.

“How many men will I have?”

“Fewer than you require.”

“How many?” his brother asked.

“Eight hundred fit to stand. Perhaps five hundred who can still move with a shield,” the king said.

“Against?”

“Four thousand in the forward companies. More behind.”

“Archers?” his brother asked.

“Two northern towers remain,” the king replied.

“Engines?”

“One stone thrower. It turns only to the west.”

The brother looked at the ceiling.

“Excellent. We shall request that the enemy approach from the wrong direction.”

The king’s mouth moved despite him.

His brother saw it.

For an instant the months between them thinned.

Then the larger man’s face hardened again.

“Send the woman back.”

The words entered the room without preparation.

The king did not pretend to misunderstand.

“Now?”

“Yes. Now is generally when a man performs the thing he should have done earlier.”

“And they will withdraw?” the king asked.

“Perhaps,” his brother replied.

“Do you believe that?”

The brother looked at the black smoke beyond the courtyard.

“No.”

“Neither do I.”

“Return her anyway,” his brother said.

“Why?” the king asked.

“Because the uselessness of a right act does not make the wrong act right.”

The king looked at Shuka, then back at his brother.

“She is not harmed.”

“That has always been your weakest defence.”

“It matters,” the king said.

“It matters to the size of the wrong. It does not alter its direction.”

The king’s hand tightened around the edge of the stool.

“They mutilated our sister.”

“Yes.”

“They destroyed the southern garrison,” the king said.

“After the garrison attacked them,” his brother replied.

“They settled armed inside our frontier.”

“And you answered an argument about a frontier by taking a woman who had not cut anyone.”

The king rose too quickly. Pain caught him above the hip. He steadied himself against the table.

His brother did not move to help.

That refusal was more intimate than assistance would have been. He knew exactly how much pain the king could bear before help became necessary.

“You think I do not know?” the king asked.

“I think knowing has become another room in which you hide from doing.”

“And you have hidden here.”

“Yes.”

The answer was immediate.

The king had prepared for defence. Agreement left the anger nowhere to land.

His brother continued.

“I hid because every time I entered your council I became one more man whose presence made your decision look shared. I would not cross the water. I would not stand beside them. I also would not stand behind you while you converted my silence into consent.”

“So you slept.”

“Apparently,” his brother replied.

“For three months,” the king said.

“An impressive rest,” his brother replied.

“Men came to this door.”

“I spoke to some.”

“They said you were sleeping,” the king said.

“It embarrassed them less than saying I had refused the king,” his brother replied.

“And the others?”

“I was sleeping.”

The king stared at him.

His brother shrugged.

“Refusal is tiring.”

Shuka looked towards the closed door. Outside, twenty-seven soldiers ate from the trays intended to prove one man’s monstrous hunger. In the courtyard, three months of political withdrawal had already become an impossible sleep.

He could almost hear the story arranging itself.

The king lowered himself onto the stool again.

“Our brother believes he crossed to save the city.”

“Partly.”

“He accepted my throne before I was dead,” the king said.

The larger man’s face changed.

“Did he?”

“Seawater upon the forehead. A cooking cup. Sand included.”

His brother looked down at his cracked cup.

“He always did prefer modest ceremonies.”

The same joke.

This time it sounded older than either man.

“Will you condemn him?” the king asked.

“For leaving?”

“For showing them the salt road,” the king said.

“Yes,” his brother replied.

“For accepting the crown?”

“Yes.”

“For saying I was wrong?” the king asked.

“No,” his brother said.

The king looked away.

“You want the privilege of agreement without the inconvenience of standing beside him.”

“And you want the comfort of my loyalty without hearing what it costs.”

“You think staying makes you morally superior,” the king said.

“No. It makes me present.”

The words remained between them.

Outside, a soldier scraped the bottom of a pot with his spoon. The sound was small and persistent.

The brother’s voice softened.

“He left because he believed the kingdom could be separated from you. I stayed because I do not know how to separate the people from the consequences of what you have done.”

“That is not loyalty to me.”

“No,” his brother replied.

The king absorbed it.

“Then why will you go?”

His brother looked almost surprised.

“Because children are climbing over a broken chimney while an army advances upon them.”

“You have not yet agreed.”

“You have not yet asked honestly.”

The king stood again.

This time his brother rose with him.

The room changed around the full height of the man. His head did not touch the ceiling. It came close enough that generations would improve the detail.

The king looked up at him.

For the first time since entering the room, he appeared not as a ruler confronting a commander but as an elder brother who had once been taller.

“Hold the brickworks,” he said. “Keep the evacuation road open for three hours. If the road clears sooner, withdraw to the inner embankment.”

“It will not clear sooner.”

“No,” the king replied.

“The inner embankment cannot be reached once their forward companies close the western lane,” his brother said.

“No,” the king replied.

“Then do not place a return inside the order merely because you cannot bear its absence.”

The king’s voice dropped.

“Hold the brickworks until the last civilian has passed.”

His brother waited.

“And after?”

The king could have lied. A king possessed many words for death: remain, delay, resist, preserve, buy time. Each allowed the speaker to leave the body outside the sentence.

“After,” he said, “there may be no road left for you.”

The larger man nodded.

“Better.”

The king looked at him.

“Will you go?”

“You knew before entering.”

“I need to hear it,” the king said.

“Why?” his brother asked.

“Because I will spend the rest of my life wondering whether I ordered you or asked.”

His brother’s eyes filled, though his face did not change.

“You asked,” he said. “That will not make it kinder.”

The king looked down.

“No,” the king replied.

“And I choose to go. That will not make you innocent,” his brother said.

“No,” the king replied.

The brother placed one enormous hand against the side of the king’s neck.

It was not an embrace. It was an older gesture, from a childhood in which fever had passed between them and their mother had taught each boy to feel for heat beneath the other’s ear.

“Good,” he said. “For once we have understood the same thing at the same time.”

The king closed his eyes beneath the hand.

Shuka looked away.

There were griefs at which observation became theft.

***

The armour had been stored beneath the old barracks for twelve years.

Two soldiers carried the breastplate into the courtyard and placed it upon a wooden frame. Dust had settled inside the engraved lines. One shoulder strap was cracked. The lower plates had been made for a waist the commander no longer possessed.

His brother examined it.

“Has the kingdom shrunk this as well?”

The armourer bowed nervously.

“You have become broader, Highness.”

“That sounds expensive.”

They found an older set of side plates and joined them with leather. The result was inelegant but strong. The large man lifted both arms, rolled his shoulders and caused three straps to complain.

“If I breathe deeply,” he said, “the enemy may be killed by the metal.”

The armourer adjusted the knots.

The king sat beneath the rain tree while the physician stitched the wound above his hip. He watched every buckle fastened upon his brother.

Men began arriving from across the compound. Retired guards. Storehouse officers. Two kitchen workers still wearing flour upon their arms. A carpenter carrying an axe intended for beams rather than bodies. They came because word had crossed the palace faster than orders.

The sleeping commander had awakened.

No one had needed drums.

Nevertheless, drums sounded at the northern gate to assemble the remaining companies.

The first beat rolled across the barracks.

The large man looked at Shuka.

“There. You hear it?”

“The muster.”

“By supper it will be said they beat drums beside my bed.”

The second beat came.

“By next year,” he continued, “they will say elephants were required to turn me over.”

The king spoke without looking up from the physician’s needle.

“Only because the elephants complained first.”

The soldiers laughed.

The laughter moved through the courtyard more carefully than wind. Men repeated the joke to those who had not heard. For half a minute the army assembling to die became an audience for two brothers who had been irritating one another since childhood.

The large man smiled.

“He has been saving that for three months,” he told Shuka.

“It required sleep.”

“Most of his wit does.”

The physician tied off the stitch.

“Do not ride,” she told the king.

“I am not going.”

The words silenced the men nearest him.

The large brother looked over.

“No,” he said. “You are not.”

The king rose.

“I can command the inner embankment.”

“You can barely command your left leg.”

“It obeys when threatened,” the king said.

“It takes after the family.”

The brother crossed the courtyard. The crowd divided before him.

“You will remain here,” he said quietly. “You will move the hospital carts. You will open the grain stores. You will send every unnecessary palace guard to the northern road.”

“You are giving orders to your king.”

“I am preventing my brother from making my death useless by arranging his beside it.”

The king’s jaw tightened.

“I did not ask you to die so that I could remain comfortable.”

“There will be nothing comfortable about remaining.”

The brother’s gaze moved towards the palace, the smoke and the thousands of people inching south beneath bundles of their lives.

“That is what you owe.”

The title passed unspoken between them.

Not the crown.

Not glory.

Remaining.

The king had asked another man to walk towards the place from which he would not return. His own punishment was to stand still and know it.

The armourer brought a helmet.

The large man tried it. The rim stopped above his ears.

“Whose head was this made for?”

“Yours, Highness.”

“Then either the metal has tightened or thought has enlarged me,” the commander said.

“It may be your hair,” the armourer replied.

“Cut it.”

The armourer looked alarmed.

“I repair metal.”

“Today you have been promoted.”

They seated him on an overturned grain measure. The armourer approached with shears while the entire courtyard offered instructions.

“Not too close,” someone called.

“Leave enough to frighten them.”

“Take the beard as well.”

The large man turned towards the last voice.

“Come closer and discuss it.”

The voice discovered other duties.

Hair fell around the grain measure in dark coils. The king watched the younger face emerge beneath it. Not young. Merely familiar.

There was the boy who had followed him into the sea before learning to swim. The boy who had broken an instructor’s nose because the man mocked their brother’s careful speech. The boy who could lift a grain sack at twelve and wept when a stable dog died. The boy who believed music lived inside clay.

The king reached down and picked a cut strand from the armour.

His brother saw him.

Neither spoke.

The helmet fit.

***

Before leaving, the commander went to the storage arcade behind the kitchen.

The king followed despite the physician. Shuka followed despite the king. The chamberlain followed because resistance had become inefficient.

Rows of clay jars stood beneath the vaulted roof: rice, millet, salt, dried tamarind, sesame and lentils. Some were taller than a child. Each had been sealed with cloth and wax against damp.

The commander stopped beside an empty jar awaiting the new harvest.

Its rim had been repaired with copper staples.

He placed his palm upon it.

“This one?” he asked.

The king looked at the jar.

“No. The old one was red.”

“They were all red.”

“Then why ask?” the king asked.

“To see whether age had made you honest.”

The commander bent and placed one ear against the clay.

The helmet made it impossible. He removed it and tried again.

The gesture looked ridiculous in armour.

It also transformed the storage arcade around them.

The king was thirteen again, seated in the round music room with the veena across his knee. His younger brother was six and forbidden to touch the instrument. Forbidden things had always improved his concentration.

He had discovered that the great grain jars gathered the instrument’s lowest notes. If he pressed his ear to the clay while the veena was played, the sound entered through the vessel and seemed to rise from beneath the floor.

For weeks he believed music slept inside empty pots.

The elder brother knew the truth.

He continued playing after lessons so the younger boy could search the jars for songs.

“Do you remember?” the commander asked.

“You believed my music lived inside them.”

“You told me it did,” the commander said.

“You were six,” the king replied.

“You let me believe it until I was nine.”

“You were happier at nine.”

The commander straightened.

“I was happier at six.”

“At six you ate lamp oil.”

“Once,” the commander said.

“You said it smelled like sesame,” the king replied.

“It did.”

“You were ill for two days.”

“An excessive response from the body.”

The chamberlain turned away, but his shoulders moved.

The king touched the copper staples along the jar’s rim.

“You broke the red one.”

“You pushed me.”

“You had put your head inside it,” the king said.

“To hear better,” the commander replied.

“Your ears caught beneath the rim.”

“The pot was badly designed.”

“Mother found us striking it with a wooden sword,” the king said.

“You struck it,” the commander replied.

“I was rescuing you.”

“You laughed for most of the rescue.”

The king smiled.

This time the smile arrived fully. It changed his face so completely that Shuka understood why men who remembered the younger king continued serving the older one.

The commander saw it too.

For one dangerous instant, leaving became difficult.

He placed the helmet beneath his arm.

“Do you still play the ending our brother corrected?”

The smile went.

“Sometimes.”

“Do you tell people he corrected it?” the commander asked.

“Now I do,” the king replied.

“Too late.”

“Yes.”

The commander looked at him.

“You loved him badly.”

The king’s fingers remained upon the jar.

“And you?” the king asked.

“I loved him while disagreeing loudly. It is a method with its own failures.”

“He crossed,” the king said.

“Yes,” the commander replied.

“You stayed,” the king said.

“Yes,” the commander replied.

“Why?”

The commander looked down the long arcade towards the open court. Servants carried grain from the stores. Children waited with cloth bags. A woman measured rice into a soldier’s helmet because every proper vessel had already been taken.

“Because leaving you and betraying them would have worn the same footprints.”

The king breathed in.

“Do you think he betrayed us?”

“He betrayed you. He may yet save them. That is the cruelty of it.”

“And you?” the king asked.

“I am about to die for people endangered by a decision I opposed. Do not ask me to make that morally tidy.”

The king looked at the floor.

The commander stepped closer.

“Listen to me. I am not going because you were right. I am not going because a younger brother owes his life to an elder. I am going because eight thousand people are trying to fit their homes into what they can carry. If I can give them three hours, those hours belong to them.”

“I know.”

“No. You know the sentence. Knowing it will begin after I leave,” his brother said.

The king looked up.

His brother’s voice became gentle.

“Do not turn me into proof of your cause. Not tonight. Not afterward.”

“I will not.”

“Do not say I died defending your honour,” his brother said.

“You have spent years preventing anyone from believing I possess it,” the king replied.

“Then my work is complete.”

The joke almost saved them.

Almost.

The commander held out his hand.

The king ignored it and embraced him.

The armour was cold between them. The larger man folded both arms around his brother carefully, mindful of the wound above the hip. The king’s face rested for one moment against the repaired shoulder plates.

They had embraced at weddings, funerals, victories and the births of children. Each occasion had supplied a reason and therefore prevented the embrace from saying everything.

This one had no adequate name.

The commander spoke beside the king’s ear.

“You are still wrong.”

The king’s hand closed against his back.

“You are still tiresome.”

“Good.”

They separated.

There were millions of things left unsaid, though neither man could have listed more than five.

That was the nature of a life shared from childhood. Its secrets were not all concealed facts. Some were glances whose meanings had never required language; names of dead dogs; a tune hummed badly; the reason one brother disliked papaya; the exact voice of their mother when she caught them lying; which stair creaked in a house demolished thirty years before. No historian would know any of it. No inscription would have room.

The commander put on his helmet.

“Look after my household.”

“I will.”

“Do not move them into the palace. My wife dislikes the eastern rooms,” the commander said.

“She dislikes me,” the king replied.

“That is why I married her.”

“Your children will lack nothing.”

The commander’s expression changed.

“They will lack me.”

The king stopped.

“Do not promise absence away,” his brother said. “It insults those who must live inside it.”

“Then what may I promise?”

“Tell them I was frightened,” his brother said.

“Were you?” the king asked.

“Yes.”

The admission entered the storage arcade more quietly than courage would have done.

“Tell them I went anyway. And let them ask whether that made me brave or merely foolish.”

“They will never have to ask whether you were brave.”

“Then make certain they are allowed to ask whether I was foolish,” his brother said.

The king nodded.

“I promise.”

The commander turned towards the courtyard.

After three steps, the king called him.

He turned immediately.

The response was so ordinary that Shuka would remember it longer than the departure.

“The western lane,” the king said. “If it remains open—”

“It will not.”

“If it does.”

The commander looked at his brother and allowed him the lie.

“If it does, I will come home.”

***

He led nine hundred and twelve people towards the northern embankment.

The official count said eight hundred because the clerks had stopped recording names when the archive roof caught fire. The later songs said ten thousand because songs disliked clerks.

They passed through the palace service gate beneath a sky the colour of ash. At the head walked the king’s brother in repaired armour. Behind him came trained soldiers, storehouse guards, cooks, stable hands, retired officers and men who had discovered weapons while looking for somewhere safe to hide.

Women leaned from windows to throw flowers.

There were few flowers left. Some threw leaves. One woman threw a lime, which struck a soldier’s helmet and improved the solemnity of the procession.

The commander looked up.

“Save those,” he called. “The palace curd requires help.”

People laughed.

The sound followed the company down the hill.

The king stood above the gate.

He did not raise his hand. His brother did not look back again.

Shuka stood beside the king. The blue cord from the salt road remained tied around his wrist.

“You could still recall him,” Shuka said.

“And replace him with whom?”

Shuka had no answer.

“That is not what I asked,” he said.

The king watched the repaired helmet move among the men below.

“No,” he replied. “It is what I must.”

The company turned north and disappeared between the grain offices.

The king remained above the empty gate.

Then he began the work for which his brother had purchased time.

He ordered the hospital wagons unloaded. Men carried the wounded upon doors, market tables and woven sleeping frames while the empty wagons were dragged from the road. He opened the western grain stores without tablets or seals. He sent palace carpets to wrap infants and tore ceremonial awnings into bandages. He ordered the southern temples to remove their bronze doors and lay them across a drainage ditch so carts could pass.

When a priest objected that the doors had been consecrated, the king told him that carrying frightened people was a respectable religious use.

The priest agreed after looking towards the smoke.

The king moved through the evacuation with the walking staff in one hand and a list in the other. Every few minutes he stopped to ask the same questions.

How many streets remained?

How many carts?

How many unable to walk?

How long?

No one told him what occurred at the brickworks unless he asked directly.

At first the reports were clean.

The commander had reached the outer yard.

The stone thrower had been turned by breaking its western brace.

The first enemy advance had withdrawn.

The evacuation road remained open.

Then the reports became smaller.

The eastern tower had fallen.

The commander had abandoned the yard and held the kiln line.

The remaining archers possessed nineteen bundles of arrows.

The western lane was closed.

Each message removed one possible future.

The king received them beside ordinary lives.

An old man complained that a soldier had left his goat behind. A woman refused to evacuate without three jars of pickled mango. Two brothers argued over which of them must carry their mother. A palace clerk attempted to preserve six years of tax records until the king took the bundle from him and replaced it with a crying child.

“The records are irreplaceable,” the clerk protested.

“So is she.”

“We do not know whose she is,” the clerk replied.

“Then she is presently the government’s.”

The clerk carried her south.

War did not pause its small absurdities in respect for grief. That was perhaps its greatest cruelty. The king wanted the city to become silent enough for him to hear whether his brother still lived. Instead everyone required directions.

A cook asked where to place six sacks of onions.

A wheel came off a medicine cart.

A child had lost a green bird in a cage.

A woman went into labour beneath the covered market.

The king answered each question.

This was what his brother had meant by remaining.

***

The first hour passed.

From the palace roof, the brickworks appeared as a low red scar beyond the northern houses. Smoke hid the ground. At intervals the wind opened a space through which figures could be seen moving around the kilns.

The king stood at the parapet with Shuka and the northern captain.

“Which is ours?” Shuka asked.

“The line nearest the chimney,” said the captain.

“They are falling back,” Shuka said.

“They are bending,” the captain replied.

“What is the difference?”

“Whether they straighten.”

A roar rose from beyond the houses.

It did not sound human from that distance. Thousands of voices compressed by stone and air became the noise of one animal.

An enemy company emerged through the smoke and then stopped.

Something moved against it.

One man could not be seen clearly. His effect could.

The advancing line folded around a point near the kiln entrance. Shields lifted. Spears tilted inward. Men behind pushed those in front. The point continued moving.

The northern captain exhaled.

“He has counterattacked.”

“With how many?” the king asked.

“From here? Perhaps a hundred,” the captain said.

“He was ordered to hold,” the king replied.

“Sometimes the safest wall is several steps in front of the place one must defend.”

The king recognised his brother’s reasoning in her words.

The enemy line broke.

From the palace roof it looked small. A dark ripple moved backwards across red earth. The defenders regained the outer yard.

People watching from the streets began to cheer.

The sound spread south along the evacuation road. Those who could not see asked what had happened. By the time the answer reached the temple quarter, the commander had destroyed an entire army alone.

“Correct them,” the king said.

The captain looked at him.

“Now?”

“Tell them the line held. Tell them nine hundred people are holding it.”

“They need courage,” the captain said.

“Then give them courage without stealing eight hundred and eleven men from the truth.”

The captain sent a runner.

Shuka watched the cheering move through the city faster than correction could follow.

“They will remember him alone,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Because he is large enough to see.”

“And because many men prefer to disappear inside a hero. It relieves them of explaining what ordinary people did together.”

The king leaned against the parapet.

Below, the last hospital wagon cleared the broken chimney.

“How many streets?” he asked.

“Seven,” said the captain.

“Time?”

“At least another hour.”

The roar began again.

***

During the second hour, the stories reached the palace before the wounded.

The commander had lifted a cart and thrown it.

He had torn a gate from its hinges.

He had eaten before battle until six servants collapsed from carrying food.

He had slept through the eastern assault and awakened only when war drums shook the walls.

He stood twice the height of an ordinary soldier.

He could not be pierced by iron.

Each report contained something visible and something required.

The cart had been tipped into a lane to block cavalry.

The gate had already burned through one hinge.

The food had fed his company.

The drums assembled the army.

He was taller than most men.

His armour was very good.

Truth stood at the centre of every lie like a small person inside ceremonial clothing.

Shuka wrote the reports as they arrived. Beside each, he added what could be confirmed.

“Why?” the king asked.

“Because by tomorrow the lies will agree with one another.”

“And then?”

“Then agreement will be mistaken for evidence.”

The king looked towards the smoke.

“Write quickly.”

The next messenger was twelve years old.

He had served water at the kiln line. Blood covered his tunic, but none appeared to be his.

“The western kiln is lost,” he said. “The prince holds the drying sheds. He asks how many streets remain.”

The king looked at the evacuation board.

“Four.”

The boy nodded.

“He said you would say four.”

“How many does he believe?”

“Six.”

The king turned to the quartermaster.

“How many?”

The quartermaster stared at the board.

“Four listed. The dyers’ lane and the eastern workshops were marked clear.”

“Were they inspected?”

No one answered.

The king closed his eyes.

“Six,” he told the messenger. “Tell him six.”

The boy looked towards the northern smoke.

“He also said to tell you the curd was watered deliberately.”

The king’s face broke.

Not dramatically. One breath entered and failed to leave correctly.

He turned away from the room.

The messenger waited.

When the king faced him again, the face belonged to government.

“Tell him the cook has been arrested.”

The boy grinned.

“Truly?”

“No. Do not let the truth damage morale.”

The boy ran.

Shuka wrote neither message upon the military tablet.

Some communications belonged to kingdoms of two.

***

The third hour began before anyone announced it.

The last families from the dyers’ lane crossed the inner bridge. Their feet were stained blue and red from pigments spilled into the road. They carried no furniture. One man carried a door upon his back because his wife had written their children’s heights along its edge and refused to leave the years behind.

The eastern workshops were not empty.

Thirty-seven people had hidden beneath a tile store after hearing that the northern road was lost. Palace guards found them and brought them out. Among them were two infants, a blind potter and a woman who insisted upon carrying a basket of unfired lamps.

“They will break,” a guard told her.

“Then they will break somewhere we are alive,” she replied.

The final group entered the inner quarter.

The king counted them himself.

“Road clear,” the captain said.

The words travelled to the northern signal tower.

Three white flags rose above the palace roof.

At the brickworks, if anyone remained able to look south, they would know the hours had been purchased.

The king stood beneath the flags.

“Signal withdrawal,” he ordered.

The captain did not move.

“The western lane is closed.”

“Signal it.”

“They cannot withdraw,” the captain said.

“Signal it.”

The horn sounded three descending notes.

Once.

Twice.

A third time.

No answer came from the brickworks.

The king waited.

The captain lowered the horn.

“Again.”

She sounded it again.

From beyond the smoke came one note.

Low. Sustained. Almost lost beneath the battle.

The king knew the signal.

*Understood. Impossible.*

His brother had heard that the road was clear.

The king sat down upon the nearest step.

Around him the palace continued moving. Clerks carried lists. Physicians shouted for water. Children cried because the carpets wrapped around them smelled unfamiliar. Somewhere in the lower court, a man argued about onions.

Shuka sat beside the king.

Neither spoke.

The horn did not sound again.

***

The commander’s death arrived in pieces.

One soldier said he had fallen beside the eastern kiln.

Another swore he remained standing after the kiln was lost.

A wounded archer said an enemy prince had struck him from a chariot.

There had been no chariot in the brickworks.

A potter claimed he saw a hundred arrows in the commander’s body. He had watched from the inner bridge nearly a mile away.

A child said the great man had not died at all but had walked into the smoke to sleep.

The king listened to every account.

“Did he hear the withdrawal horn?” he asked each survivor.

Some said yes.

Some did not know.

“Did he see the white flags?”

One man had.

“Was he alive then?”

“Yes.”

“Did he know the road was clear?” the king asked.

The man began to cry.

“Yes, Majesty.”

The king nodded.

That was the fact for which he had been waiting.

He dismissed the court.

No one obeyed because there was nowhere else for the court to go.

The wounded filled the western room. Officials worked in the passages. The women’s garden housed children from the northern quarter. The king possessed no chamber in which grief could be made private.

So he continued.

He approved water for the refugees. He ordered the inner northern gate closed. He reassigned the remaining archers. He signed compensation for the families of the two watchmen killed at the oil cistern.

When the execution tablet of the seventeen-year-old soldier returned by mistake among the other orders, the king stared at the empty circle where his thumbprint should have been.

Then he placed it face down.

The old chamberlain entered near sunset carrying an empty tray.

He waited until the officials had moved away.

“What?” the king asked.

“The kitchen wishes to know whether the remaining breakfast should be cleared.”

The king looked at him.

“What breakfast?”

“In the barracks. His second bowl.”

The king’s face did not move.

The chamberlain continued because stopping would have been worse.

“He told them not to remove it. He said he would finish it when he returned.”

Shuka saw the king’s hand close around the edge of the table.

“The rice will spoil,” the chamberlain said softly.

The king looked down at the unsigned death order.

“Then let it.”

The chamberlain bowed.

He reached the curtain before the king spoke again.

“No.”

The old man turned.

“Feed it to someone.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“Not his dogs. They will wait for him,” the king said.

The chamberlain’s mouth tightened.

“Yes.”

He left.

The king remained with one hand upon the table.

His shoulders began to shake.

There was no cry. No collapse. His body simply failed to continue containing what the crown required it to contain.

Shuka moved towards him.

The king lifted one hand.

Not to forbid comfort.

To delay it until he could breathe.

The moment passed badly.

Then Shuka placed both arms around him.

The king did not return the embrace. He stood inside it like a man beneath rain, unable to stop the weather and too tired to seek shelter.

“I sent him,” he said.

Shuka held on.

“He chose to go.”

“That is the sentence everyone will give me.”

“It is true,” Shuka said.

“Truth can be merciful without being complete.”

Shuka released him.

The king wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

“He chose after I placed the choice before him. I knew what he would choose. That is why I went myself.”

“The quarter is alive because of him.”

“And because I knew how to spend him,” the king said.

“Do not make your grief another form of vanity.”

The king looked at Shuka sharply.

The spy’s voice trembled, but he continued.

“Nine hundred people stood there. He told you not to turn him into proof of your cause. Do not turn him into proof of your guilt either. Let the act belong to him.”

For a moment Shuka thought he had gone too far.

Then the king looked towards the northern smoke.

“He would have liked that answer.”

“He would have improved it.”

“Excessively,” the king said.

They stood together in the western room.

The split pomegranate still lay upon the black table from a morning that now seemed to belong to another reign. Its exposed seeds had dried nearly black. Beside it were Vibhishana’s salt-road map, the blue cord, the unsigned execution order and one damaged clasp from the dead brother’s armour.

An armourer had brought it from a wounded soldier who found it near the kiln line.

The king picked it up.

“You asked what kings owe,” Shuka said.

“Did I?”

“Not in words.”

The king turned the clasp between his fingers.

“A crown is praised for every life it saves,” he said. “No one counts the lives it learns to spend.”

Shuka looked at the execution order.

“You spared the boy.”

“Yes.”

“Why could you not spare your brother?” Shuka asked.

The king’s thumb moved across the scratched metal.

“Because the boy’s life belonged to him.”

“And your brother’s?”

“Belonged to him also,” the king said.

“Then you did not have the right,” Shuka replied.

“No.”

The answer came without defence.

“But you asked.”

“Yes.”

“And he agreed,” Shuka said.

“Yes,” the king replied.

“Then where is the wrong?”

The king closed his fingers around the clasp.

“In knowing love would make him say yes.”

Outside, the city began counting its survivors.

Names were called in temple courts, market halls and beneath trees. Each answer produced a small release. Each silence created a new shape of fear.

The names from the brickworks were written separately.

The royal scribe entered with a fresh tablet.

“Majesty, the official record requires the commander’s name.”

The king did not answer.

The scribe waited.

“What have you written?” Shuka asked.

The man read from the tablet.

“The great prince, commander of the northern defence, of unmatched strength, who awakened at the sounding of war drums and, after consuming—”

“Stop,” said the king.

The scribe stopped.

“How much have you said he consumed?”

The scribe examined the line.

“Thirty vessels of rice, twelve of meat, eight jars of curd and—”

“The curd was thin.”

The scribe looked uncertain.

“Should I include that?”

“It is the most accurate part.”

Shuka covered his mouth.

The king took the tablet.

“He fed his soldiers from those pots. He was awake before the drums. He was a man, not a hill that learned to carry a sword.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“Write the names of the men who stood with him,” the king said.

“We do not possess all of them,” the scribe replied.

“Then leave room.”

“And the prince’s titles?”

The king looked at the damaged clasp in his hand.

“Leave those too.”

The scribe bowed and withdrew.

Shuka waited until the curtain settled.

“What was his name?” he asked.

The king looked towards the empty doorway.

For most of the night he had spoken of brothers without naming them. One name had become treason. The other had become a legend before the body returned.

“When we were children,” the king said, “he believed music lived inside the grain jars.”

Shuka said nothing.

“He would press his ear to the clay while I played. Once he pushed his head too far inside a red pot and caught both ears beneath the rim. I tried to free him with oil. That made it worse. In the end I broke the pot with a practice sword.”

The memory placed a tired smile upon his face.

“Our mother found us covered in grain and pieces of clay. He blamed the pot. I told her a boy with ears like storage jars should expect difficulty.”

“Pot-eared,” Shuka said.

“Yes. Kumbha-karna.”

The old household joke sounded different in the ruined room.

“He hated it at first. Then our sister used it. Then the servants. Eventually even our mother forgot the dignified name chosen by the priests.”

Shuka looked at the northern smoke thinning against the evening sky.

“Was Kumbhakarna truly his name?”

The king closed his hand around the clasp his brother would never return to claim.

“It was the name by which he turned whenever I called.”
